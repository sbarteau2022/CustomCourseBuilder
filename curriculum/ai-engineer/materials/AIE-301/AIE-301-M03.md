# AIE-301-M03 — Scaling: what compute, data, and parameters buy

**Course:** AIE-301 — Large Language Models: Pretraining to Post-Training · **Tier:** specialization · **Durability:** mixed
**Hours:** 16 · **Sits in the descent:** the third module of AIE-301, directly downstream of M02 (pretraining a small LM on your own AIE-203 corpus). M02 answered "can I train a language model end to end." This module answers a harder question the field usually just shows you a chart for: **if I had more of X — parameters, data, compute — how much better would my model get, and how do I know, rather than assume?** Nothing here is new machinery; every experiment in this module reuses M02's training loop, M02's tokenizer, and a corpus in the same family as the one you built in AIE-203. What's new is the discipline of *varying one axis at a time, measuring what happens, fitting a curve to it, and then checking whether that curve was safe to extrapolate* — which is exactly the AIE-204-M03 statistics discipline (power, honest confidence intervals, the danger of a fit that looks great inside its own data and fails outside it), aimed for the first time at a claim about a model instead of an experiment.
**Prereq:** AIE-301-M02 (a trained small LM and its training loop — this module's entire empirical base is runs of *that* loop, varied along three axes), AIE-204-M03 (power analysis, confidence intervals, and the winner's curse — this module is the moment those tools stop being about A/B tests and start being about the claim "bigger will help," which is the single most consequential extrapolation this field makes), AIE-203 (the corpus; this module's Lab B works on a slice of your own corpus).

> **Design note.** Every number, every fitted exponent, every confidence interval, and every table in this module was **measured** by training real (tiny) transformer language models with PyTorch on the authoring machine and fitting the curves shown — nothing here is invented, sketched, or copied from a published scaling-laws paper. The teaching corpus is a synthetic, template-generated language (see `corpus.py` below) chosen for one deliberate reason: its **true information content is exactly computable**, by hand, from the generator itself — so this module can show you a fitted "irreducible loss floor" *next to the actual floor*, and let you see whether the fit found it. No real corpus affords that check; your AIE-203 corpus's entropy is unknown and unknowable in closed form, which is exactly why Lab M03-A's method — fit here, where you can grade your own homework, before trusting it where you can't — is the whole point of the module, not a warm-up for it.

## Lessons

Four lessons, one per axis plus the discipline that ties them together. The first three each hold two of {parameters, data, compute} fixed and vary the third, on the same toy transformer and the same corpus family, so the differences you see are attributable to exactly one thing. The fourth turns the same fitting method on itself: fits a power law on a *subset* of the parameter sweep, predicts a model it hasn't seen, and then actually trains that model and checks — the extrapolation either holds or it doesn't, and this module found out, in front of you, which one happened.

1. **What more parameters buy — and where "bigger" stops being free.** A clean power-law improvement in a well-behaved regime, and a real, measured regime where it wasn't clean at all.
2. **What more data buys — the overfitting gap, measured, not asserted.** Holding model size and training steps fixed, watching the train/val gap collapse as the data pool grows, and watching it stop paying off.
3. **What more compute buys — and why "compute-optimal" is an allocation problem, not a parameter count.** The same total compute spent as bigger models vs. more steps on smaller ones — measured, with the winner changing depending on the budget.
4. **Fitting a scaling law honestly — and watching your own fit fail outside its range.** Power-law regression, a bootstrap confidence interval on the exponent, and a held-out point that the fit got badly wrong — the AIE-204-M03 discipline, aimed at a scaling claim.

### Lesson 1 — What more parameters buy, and where "bigger" stops being free

#### Teaching text

Hold the corpus fixed, hold the number of training steps fixed — generous enough that a small model has every chance to converge — and vary only the model's width. If "bigger model, same everything else" behaves the way the field's marketing decks suggest, validation loss should fall smoothly as parameter count rises, forever. It does not fall forever, and this lesson's first job is to show you exactly where and why it stops, on numbers you can rerun.

**The setup.** A tiny decoder-only transformer — token + learned positional embeddings, pre-LN blocks with causal self-attention and a 4×-widening MLP, tied to a fixed vocabulary — trained with AdamW at a single fixed learning rate (`3e-3`) on a 108,000-character training split, for 200 steps, batch size 16, context length 32. Eight widths (`n_embd`), each run three times with different seeds so a lucky or unlucky initialization doesn't masquerade as a real effect:

| n_embd | params | mean val loss (3 seeds) | std | mean train loss |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 8 | 2,432 | 1.8541 | 0.0135 | 1.8531 |
| 16 | 7,936 | 1.4306 | 0.0111 | 1.4237 |
| 24 | 16,512 | 1.1816 | 0.0723 | 1.1696 |
| 32 | 28,160 | 0.9130 | 0.0375 | 0.8998 |
| 48 | 60,672 | 0.6909 | 0.0185 | 0.6770 |
| 64 | 105,472 | **0.6123** | 0.0102 | 0.6043 |
| 96 | 231,936 | **0.5935** | 0.0283 | 0.5773 |
| 128 | 407,552 | **1.1963** | **0.3389** | 1.1884 |

Read this top to bottom, honestly, before drawing a trend line through it. From 2,432 to 231,936 parameters (a 95× increase), validation loss falls smoothly and substantially: 1.85 → 0.59, with shrinking marginal gains each step up (48→64 buys 0.078 nats; 64→96 buys only 0.019 more) — a real, measured **diminishing-returns curve**, not folklore. Then at 407,552 parameters — bigger again, same data, same steps, same everything else — loss **jumps back up to 1.20, and its std across seeds explodes to 0.34** (one of the three seeds landed at 1.67, worse than the 16-parameter-wide model). That is not noise around a trend; it is a qualitatively different, unstable regime.

**Fitting the clean part of the curve.** Restrict to `n_embd ∈ {8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64}` — the well-behaved range — and fit a power law two ways. First, the naive form every "scaling law" figure you've seen implicitly uses: `L(P) = a · P^-α`, fit by ordinary least squares on `log(L)` vs. `log(P)`. Second, the form that actually appears in the scaling-laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022): `L(P) = L∞ + a · P^-α`, where `L∞` is the **irreducible loss** — the entropy of the data-generating process itself, the floor no amount of capacity can push below, because it isn't ignorance the model can fix, it's randomness the data genuinely contains.

Because this module's corpus is a synthetic grammar (`corpus.py`, shown in full in Lab M03-A) — every sentence draws a SUBJECT, VERB, and OBJECT uniformly from fixed 10-word lists, a fair coin for whether an ADVERB is present, and if so an ADVERB from a 10-word list — its **true entropy rate is exactly computable, not estimated**: `H = 3·log₂(10) + 1 + 0.5·log₂(10)` bits of genuine randomness per sentence, divided by the mean characters per sentence (including the separating space), converted to nats to match the training loss's units:

```
L∞ (true, analytic) = 0.2312 nats/char
```

Fitting both forms on the six-point clean range:

| Fit | Form | Fitted values | log-log R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive (no floor) | `L = a·P^-α` | `a = 21.90`, **α = 0.309** | 0.9833 |
| Floor fixed at the true value | `L = 0.2312 + a·P^-α` | `a = 41.70`, **α = 0.403** | 0.9749 |

**Both fits look excellent by R² alone — and they disagree about the exponent by 30% (0.309 vs. 0.403), because they disagree about where the floor is.** The naive fit implicitly assumes `L∞ = 0`, i.e., a model could in principle reach zero loss given infinite parameters — false for this corpus, whose generator has 0.2312 nats/char of genuine, irreducible randomness no model can predict away. Correcting for the true floor doesn't just shift the curve; it changes the *rate* at which the fit says loss is falling as capacity grows, because it changes what's left to explain once the fixed, unclosable gap is subtracted out. On real text, you never know the true floor the way this module does — you'd report the naive α, or a fitted-not-known L∞, and either choice quietly encodes an assumption about how good a model *could ever get* on data whose true entropy nobody has measured.

**What happens if you let a fit find its own floor instead of assuming or knowing it?** Fit the three-parameter form `L = L∞ + a·P^-α` with `L∞` left **free**, via nonlinear least squares, on the same six points: it recovers `L∞ ≈ −1.93` — a **negative** irreducible loss, which is not a physically possible answer for a floor on a non-negative loss, and light-years from the true 0.2312. Six noisy points are not enough information to pin down three free, correlated parameters; the optimizer found *a* combination of `(L∞, a, α)` that fits the six points about as well as the constrained version, but the individual parameter values it landed on are meaningless. **A great-looking fit and a trustworthy fit are not the same claim**, and the gap between them is invisible from the R² alone — you only catch it by checking a parameter you have an independent way to verify, which is exactly what the known analytic floor buys this module and won't buy you on your own corpus.

#### Worked example

The `n_embd = 128` row above **is** the worked example, and it deserves the same treatment Lesson 2 of M01 gave its own tie-break tie: don't explain it away, name the mechanism. This module trained every width with the **same fixed learning rate** (`3e-3`) for simplicity. That learning rate was tuned, informally, to work well for the small widths. As width grows, a transformer's pre-activation variance and gradient scale both grow with it — the same nominal learning rate becomes progressively too large for a wider model's loss landscape, and training becomes less stable, not more expressive. At `n_embd = 128` this stops being a minor efficiency loss and becomes a training failure: one of the three seeds diverged partway through and never recovered (val loss 1.67, *worse than the 16-wide model's 1.43*), while the other two seeds landed in the 0.93–0.98 range — still worse than the 64-wide and 96-wide models trained with the exact same, now-too-aggressive learning rate. **This is not a story about "128 parameters wasn't enough capacity." It is a story about an optimization confound that a naive scaling-law fit cannot see, because a fit only ever sees the y-axis number, never the reason it landed there.** This exact class of confound is why the field's own scaling-laws literature was revised: Kaplan et al. (2020) fit compute-optimal model sizing without fully accounting for learning-rate and batch-size interactions across scale, and Hoffmann et al. (2022) — the Chinchilla paper — found that a large share of contemporaneous large models (including some trained under Kaplan-style guidance) were measurably undertrained for their size once these interactions were controlled for. You just reproduced, at toy scale and in an afternoon, the same category of mistake that reshaped how the entire field allocates training compute.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"More parameters always helps, holding everything else fixed — the only question is by how much."** The measured table refutes this directly: 407,552 parameters scored *worse*, on average, than 60,672 parameters, with the same data and steps. "Holding everything else fixed" is doing enormous, usually-invisible work in that sentence — a fixed learning rate is not actually "everything else fixed" once width changes enough to shift the loss landscape's scale.
- **"A high R² means the fit is trustworthy."** The free-floor fit's R² was comparable to the fixed-floor fit's, and it recovered a physically impossible negative irreducible loss. R² measures how well a curve passes through the points you gave it — it says nothing about whether the *specific parameter values* the curve settled on mean what you think they mean, especially with few points and several free, correlated parameters.
- **"The irreducible loss floor is a theoretical nicety — real fits don't need to know it."** They don't need to *know* it, but every fit makes an implicit choice about it: assume it's zero (the naive form), or estimate it from the same handful of points that are also supposed to pin down the exponent (the free 3-parameter form, shown above to fail with six points). Neither choice is free of assumption; this lesson's whole point is that the exponent you report depends on which one you silently made.

### Lesson 2 — What more data buys: the overfitting gap, measured

#### Teaching text

Hold the model size fixed (`n_embd = 32`, 28,160 parameters — comfortably capable, per Lesson 1's curve, of reaching well under 1.0 nats/char given enough signal) and hold training steps fixed at 200. Vary only how much distinct training text the model is allowed to draw batches from. If "more data always helps" were the whole story, validation loss should fall monotonically as the data pool grows, without limit. Watch what a **fixed compute budget** (200 steps is exactly that — a fixed number of gradient updates, regardless of how big the pool behind them is) does to that story:

| training data (chars) | mean val loss (3 seeds) | mean train loss | mean train/val gap |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,500 | 1.4261 | 0.7651 | **0.6609** |
| 4,000 | 0.9838 | 0.8808 | 0.1030 |
| 12,000 | 0.9795 | 0.9506 | 0.0289 |
| 40,000 | 0.9559 | 0.9406 | 0.0153 |
| 120,000 | 0.9130 | 0.8998 | 0.0132 |

Two separate, real effects live in this table, and conflating them is the most common way "more data helps" gets reported without being understood.

**Effect one — too little data produces overfitting, and the gap is the diagnostic, not the validation number alone.** At 1,500 training characters, the model reaches a *train* loss of 0.77 — genuinely good, competitive with what the much bigger data pools eventually achieve — while its *validation* loss sits at 1.43, worse than every other data size tested. The model has not learned the language; it has partly **memorized its tiny 1,350-character training split** (90/10 split leaves only ~1,350 train characters, well under 40 sentences) and that memorization doesn't transfer to held-out sentences drawing from the same generator but different specific word combinations. The **gap** (`val − train = 0.66`) is the number that tells you this is happening — the validation loss alone, without the paired train number, would just look like "a somewhat bad run," not "an overfitting run," and those call for opposite fixes (overfitting calls for more data or regularization; a uniformly bad run might call for more capacity or more steps).

**Effect two — past a threshold, more data stops helping, because something else became the bottleneck.** From 4,000 characters onward, validation loss barely moves (0.984 → 0.956 → 0.913 across a **30× increase** in data, from 4,000 to 120,000 characters) and the train/val gap is already small and shrinking only slightly further. This is not "data stopped mattering in general" — it's that **200 training steps, at this model size, is no longer the bottleneck data availability, it's the bottleneck of how many gradient updates the model gets to make**, a claim Lesson 3 measures directly by varying steps instead of data. Feeding a fixed-step, fixed-size training run more and more data past the point where it has already stopped overfitting buys almost nothing, because the constraint moved.

**The honest, complete claim, stated the way this module requires every scaling claim to be stated:** *for this model size (28,160 parameters) and this compute budget (200 steps, batch 16, context 32), validation loss on this corpus family fell from 1.43 to 0.98 as training data grew from 1,500 to 4,000 characters, and then stayed within 0.98–0.91 as data grew a further 30× to 120,000 characters.* Not "more data helps" — a claim with three unstated, load-bearing conditions (this model size, this compute budget, this corpus) that this lesson's own numbers show change the answer if you vary any of them.

#### Worked example

Predict, before checking the table: at `d_chars = 120,000` — the *same* pool size used for every Experiment A run in Lesson 1 — does the 32-wide model's validation loss here (0.9130) match the 32-wide row from Lesson 1's table (0.9130)? It should, and it does exactly, because it is the *same run*: Lesson 1's `n_embd=32` row and this lesson's `d_chars=120,000` row are the identical configuration, reused rather than rerun. This is not a coincidence to gloss over — it's a integrity check worth naming explicitly: two experiments that happen to share a configuration should report the identical number, and if they didn't, that would be evidence of a bug (nondeterminism, a seed leak, a stale cache) rather than a second real effect. Reusing runs across experiments, and checking that the overlap agrees exactly, is cheap and catches real mistakes — build the habit now, at toy scale, where checking is free.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"If validation loss is bad, the fix is always more data."** The 1,500-character row shows the opposite mechanism operating: the *train* loss there was already good (0.77); the problem was the gap, not the ceiling. Feeding this exact failure mode more capacity instead of more data (Lesson 1's lever) would also have closed the gap, and feeding it more steps at the same tiny data pool (Lesson 3's lever) would likely have made the memorization *worse*, not better. Matching the diagnosis (which gap, which lever) to the right axis is the actual skill; "add more data" is a reflex that happens to be right for this specific row and wrong in general.
- **"The plateau from 4,000 to 120,000 characters means this corpus family has nothing left to teach the model."** It means this **model size, trained for this many steps**, has nothing left to *extract* from data past that point — Lesson 3's compute experiment shows the same 32-wide model, given more steps instead of more data, continues improving well past where this table plateaus (down to 0.359 at 900 steps on the full 120,000-character pool). The data wasn't exhausted; the compute budget was.
- **"A shrinking train/val gap always means the model is getting better."** It can also mean the model has stopped being able to memorize *and* stopped being able to generalize further — both effects shrink the gap. The gap alone, without also tracking the absolute validation number, cannot distinguish "healthy convergence" from "converged onto a mediocre floor." Read both numbers together, always.

### Lesson 3 — What more compute buys: compute-optimal allocation, measured

#### Teaching text

Parameters and data are levers you pull independently in Lessons 1 and 2. **Compute is not a third independent lever — it's the product of the other two axes and how long you train**, and the real question practitioners face is never "should I add compute" (obviously yes, if you can afford it) but **"given a fixed compute budget, should I spend it on a bigger model or on more steps for a smaller one?"** This lesson answers that with a grid: five model widths crossed with four step budgets, all on the same 120,000-character corpus (ample — Lesson 2 showed 32-wide models don't need more than ~4,000 characters at this scale), reporting validation loss and the standard compute-accounting approximation `FLOPs ≈ 6 · N · D_tokens` (Kaplan et al., 2020's heuristic: roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward pass, doubled for the backward pass, times one more factor Kaplan's paper folds in for the optimizer step — treat the constant 6 as a citable approximation, not a number this module re-derived):

| steps ↓ / n_embd → | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **50** | 2.5441 | 2.1829 | 1.8836 | **1.7617** | 1.8209 |
| **150** | 1.9604 | 1.6252 | 1.3293 | **0.8554** | 0.9644 |
| **450** | 1.5209 | 0.6947 | 0.4517 | **0.4017** | 0.4635 |
| **900** | 1.0999 | 0.4324 | **0.3592** | 0.3663 | 0.3692 |

Bold marks the best (lowest-loss) model width at each compute budget. Read the bold column down the table: at low compute (50, 150 steps) the best choice is `n_embd = 64`; at 450 steps it's still 64; **at 900 steps the best choice flips to `n_embd = 32`** — a *smaller* model than the winner one row up. This is not noise: at 900 steps, `n_embd ∈ {16, 32, 64, 128}` all land within a tight band (0.36–0.43), while `n_embd = 8` is still clearly capacity-starved (1.10, nowhere near that band) even with 900 steps of training on the exact same data every wider model used. **Once a model is wide enough not to be capacity-starved, giving it *more steps* than a smaller-but-still-adequate model closes most of the gap between them — and the biggest model in the grid (128-wide) never wins a single row**, for the same fixed-learning-rate instability Lesson 1 already named.

**The FLOPs accounting makes the trade-off concrete.** The 900-step, 32-wide winner cost `7.79 × 10¹⁰` FLOPs; the 450-step, 64-wide winner from the row above cost `1.46 × 10¹¹` FLOPs — **nearly double**, for a *worse* validation loss (0.4017 vs. 0.3592). Spending the extra compute on more steps for the smaller model, rather than on a wider model trained for fewer steps, was the better trade at this budget. This is the toy-scale version of the finding that reshaped large-model training practice: Hoffmann et al., 2022 (the Chinchilla paper) showed that many large language models of that era — including some following Kaplan et al.'s own earlier guidance — were substantially **undertrained relative to their size**: a well-known example is Chinchilla (70B parameters, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens) matching or beating Gopher (280B parameters, trained on only 300 billion tokens) at *lower* total training compute, by spending the compute budget on more data and steps rather than more parameters. **Compute-optimal is a real allocation problem with a real, data-dependent answer — not a synonym for "the biggest model you can afford."**

**What Lesson 1's parameter curve alone could never have told you.** Lesson 1 fixed steps at 200 and varied width — a single vertical slice of this grid (roughly the row between 150 and 450 steps). A learner who only ran that experiment and extrapolated "bigger is better, with diminishing returns" would have been describing one column's behavior at one fixed budget, and would have had no way to predict that the ranking of model sizes *itself* changes once the compute budget changes. Scaling in one axis, measured in isolation, is real but incomplete — the joint grid is what "compute-optimal" actually means, and it's the reason this module insisted on running all three experiments rather than the more common "bigger is better, see chart" single-axis version.

#### Worked example

Compute-matching two specific cells by hand, the way you'd defend a compute-allocation decision to a skeptical reviewer: the (450 steps, 64-wide) cell and the (900 steps, 32-wide) cell both cost order-`10¹¹` FLOPs (`1.46 × 10¹¹` vs. `7.79 × 10¹⁰` — the 32-wide cell is actually *cheaper*, not matched-up). A truly FLOPs-matched comparison would need to find a `(steps, n_embd)` pair on the grid landing near the *same* FLOPs value for a fair head-to-head — this module's grid was swept over round step counts and round widths, not over round FLOPs values, so exact matches don't fall out for free. That's a real, worth-naming limitation of this lesson's design, not a hidden one: computing FLOPs *after* the fact tells you what you spent; designing an experiment to hold FLOPs constant *while varying the N/D split* is a harder, more deliberate experimental design that Lab M03-C asks you to attempt on your own grid.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"Compute-optimal means the biggest model your hardware can fit."** The measured grid's biggest model (128-wide) never won a single compute budget in this experiment, and lost to a *smaller* model at the highest budget tested. Bigger costs more FLOPs per step; whether that cost buys enough loss reduction to beat spending the same FLOPs on more steps for a smaller model is an empirical question with a data-dependent answer, not a rule of thumb.
- **"If a bigger model wins at low compute, it will keep winning as compute grows."** The grid's own top two rows (50 and 150 steps) both crown `n_embd = 64`; the bottom row crowns `n_embd = 32`. The ranking is not stable across budgets — extrapolating a winner from a low-compute regime to a high-compute regime is exactly the kind of unlicensed extrapolation Lesson 4 names directly.
- **"FLOPs and training steps are basically interchangeable ways to talk about compute."** They're related but not the same axis, and this lesson's worked example shows why the distinction matters: two cells can differ substantially in steps and land at similar or even inverted FLOPs, because FLOPs also depends on model width. A "steps-matched" comparison and a "FLOPs-matched" comparison are different experiments and can disagree about which one "used the same compute."

### Lesson 4 — Fitting a scaling law honestly, and watching it fail outside its range

#### Teaching text

Lesson 1 fit a power law on `n_embd ∈ {8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64}` and got a clean, high-R² curve. Two widths were measured but deliberately **held out of the fit**: `n_embd = 96` and `n_embd = 128`. This lesson does the thing AIE-204-M03 spent an entire module insisting you do before trusting any fitted number: **use the fit to predict a point it never saw, then go measure that point for real, and look at the gap.**

**The prediction, made honestly — before looking at the held-out numbers again.** Using the naive fit from Lesson 1 (`L = 21.90 · P^-0.309`):

| held-out width | params | naive-fit prediction | **actual measured** | error |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 96 | 231,936 | 0.4814 | **0.5935** | fit undershot the true loss by 0.112 (23% relative) |
| 128 | 407,552 | 0.4045 | **1.1963** | fit undershot the true loss by **0.792 (196% relative)** |

**The `n_embd = 96` miss is the kind of error every honest fit has — real, worth reporting, not catastrophic.** The fit predicted continued smooth improvement and got a plausible-in-spirit answer that was moderately optimistic; 96-wide really did keep improving over 64-wide (0.5935 < 0.6123), just not by quite as much as the extrapolated curve promised. This is normal extrapolation slop, the kind AIE-204-M03's confidence-interval habit is built to bound rather than eliminate.

**The `n_embd = 128` miss is not that kind of error, and conflating the two would be the actual mistake this lesson exists to prevent.** The fit predicted 0.4045 — continued improvement, as the smooth trend demanded. The measured reality was 1.1963: **not a smaller improvement than predicted, but a reversal**, worse than every width from 32 upward, worse than the fit's prediction by nearly 3×. Lesson 1 already named the mechanism — a fixed learning rate becoming miscalibrated for a wider model, an optimization failure with nothing to do with capacity — and that mechanism is **structurally invisible to a curve fit**, because the fit only ever sees `(P, L)` pairs, never *why* a given `L` happened. A power law fit on a well-behaved regime **cannot know it is about to leave that regime**, and nothing about a high R² inside the fit range warns you that it's about to.

**This is the exact shape of failure AIE-204-M03 built an entire lesson around, one level up.** There, an underpowered A/B test that happened to reach significance was shown to be *systematically* overconfident about its own point estimate (the winner's curse) — not because anyone computed anything wrong, but because a study design blind to its own reliability produces numbers that look precise and aren't. Here, a power-law fit that reached a high R² on six points is systematically overconfident about a seventh point it never saw, for the identical structural reason: **the number in front of you (an R², a p-value, a fitted exponent) describes how well your method performed *on the data you gave it* — it says nothing, by itself, about the size of the world your method was never tested against.** A scaling "law" is not a law in the physics sense; it is a fitted empirical regularity, true within some regime, with a boundary you cannot see from inside the fit — and the single most consequential sentence in any real scaling-laws report is the one stating that regime's edges, which is also the sentence most often left out.

**Reporting this honestly.** The naive-fit exponent (`α = 0.309`, six points, log-log R² = 0.983) is a real, useful, locally valid description of how loss falls with parameters *in the 2,432–105,472 parameter range, at this learning rate, on this corpus* — and it is **not** a license to say "and therefore a 400K-parameter model will hit ~0.40," because you just watched that specific prediction fail by 196%. The bootstrap 95% confidence interval on the exponent (`α ∈ [0.388, 0.417]`, computed with the true floor fixed — see Lab M03-A) quantifies the *sampling* uncertainty in the fit — how much the exponent would wiggle if you reran the six in-range experiments with different seeds. It says **nothing** about the *extrapolation* uncertainty — how wrong the fit could be once you leave the range it was fit on — and no confidence interval computed from in-range data can ever price in a regime change it never saw. That gap between the two kinds of uncertainty is this module's certification-artifact question, and Lab M03-C asks you to hold both, explicitly, in writing, on your own numbers.

#### Worked example

State, in one sentence each, the two different claims this lesson's own numbers license and refuse:

- **Licensed:** "On this corpus, at this learning rate, for models between roughly 2K and 105K parameters, validation loss falls approximately as `P^-0.31` to `P^-0.40` (95% CI on the floor-corrected exponent: [0.39, 0.42]), holding data and step count fixed and ample."
- **Refused:** "Therefore, scaling this architecture to 400K parameters will continue to reduce loss at approximately the same rate." — refused because you have direct, measured evidence this specific refusal is correct: 400K parameters, in this exact setup, produced loss nearly 3× worse than the "therefore" clause predicted.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"A tight confidence interval on the exponent means the scaling law is reliable at any scale."** The bootstrap CI (`[0.388, 0.417]`) is tight precisely *because* it only resamples uncertainty from the six well-behaved, in-range points — it cannot and does not know about the regime change at `n_embd = 128`, which sits entirely outside the data the CI was computed from. A tight CI on a fitted parameter and a safe extrapolation are different claims, and this lesson's own held-out miss is the proof.
- **"Since we caught this problem with a known corpus, real scaling-law papers on real data must have already caught the equivalent problem."** They are subject to exactly this failure mode and often cannot check it the way this module could, because real corpora have no known, computable entropy floor to compare a fit's recovered `L∞` against — the free-fit degeneracy Lesson 1 demonstrated (recovered `L∞ = −1.93`, nowhere near the true 0.2312) would be **undetectable** on real text, where nobody knows the true floor to catch the fit being wrong.
- **"The `n_embd = 128` result should just be excluded as an outlier / bad run."** Excluding it because it disagrees with the fitted trend, rather than because of an identified, independent problem with the run itself, is p-hacking's sibling for curve-fitting: discard the point that breaks the story, keep the ones that make it. The correct move — the one this lesson took — is to keep the point, name the mechanism (fixed learning rate at large width), and report the fit's range as *ending* before that point, not to erase the point from the record.

```python
# tinylm.py — provided. A miniature stand-in for your M02 model: pre-LN
# decoder-only transformer, causal self-attention, AdamW training loop.
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_embd, n_head, block_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_head = n_head
        self.qkv = nn.Linear(n_embd, 3 * n_embd)
        self.proj = nn.Linear(n_embd, n_embd)
        mask = torch.tril(torch.ones(block_size, block_size)).view(1, 1, block_size, block_size)
        self.register_buffer("mask", mask)

    def forward(self, x):
        B, T, C = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.qkv(x).split(C, dim=2)
        hs = C // self.n_head
        q = q.view(B, T, self.n_head, hs).transpose(1, 2)
        k = k.view(B, T, self.n_head, hs).transpose(1, 2)
        v = v.view(B, T, self.n_head, hs).transpose(1, 2)
        att = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) / math.sqrt(hs)
        att = att.masked_fill(self.mask[:, :, :T, :T] == 0, float("-inf"))
        out = (F.softmax(att, dim=-1) @ v).transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, T, C)
        return self.proj(out)

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_embd, n_head, block_size, mlp_ratio=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1, self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd), nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)
        self.attn = CausalSelfAttention(n_embd, n_head, block_size)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(n_embd, mlp_ratio * n_embd), nn.GELU(),
                                  nn.Linear(mlp_ratio * n_embd, n_embd))
    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.attn(self.ln1(x))
        return x + self.mlp(self.ln2(x))

class TinyLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, n_embd, n_layer, n_head, block_size, mlp_ratio=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.block_size = block_size
        self.tok_emb = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, n_embd)
        self.pos_emb = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1, block_size, n_embd))
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([Block(n_embd, n_head, block_size, mlp_ratio) for _ in range(n_layer)])
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)
        self.head = nn.Linear(n_embd, vocab_size, bias=False)

    def forward(self, idx, targets=None):
        B, T = idx.shape
        x = self.tok_emb(idx) + self.pos_emb[:, :T, :]
        for blk in self.blocks:
            x = blk(x)
        logits = self.head(self.ln_f(x))
        loss = None
        if targets is not None:
            loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)), targets.view(-1))
        return logits, loss

def num_params(model: nn.Module) -> int:
    return sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())

def make_batches(data_ids, block_size, batch_size, rng):
    n = data_ids.size(0) - block_size - 1
    ix = torch.randint(0, n, (batch_size,), generator=rng)
    x = torch.stack([data_ids[i:i + block_size] for i in ix])
    y = torch.stack([data_ids[i + 1:i + block_size + 1] for i in ix])
    return x, y

@torch.no_grad()
def eval_loss(model, data_ids, block_size, batch_size, n_batches, rng):
    model.eval()
    losses = [model(*make_batches(data_ids, block_size, batch_size, rng))[1].item() for _ in range(n_batches)]
    model.train()
    return sum(losses) / len(losses)

def train_model(model, train_ids, val_ids, block_size, batch_size, steps, lr, seed):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    rng = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        x, y = make_batches(train_ids, block_size, batch_size, rng)
        _, loss = model(x, y)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    val = eval_loss(model, val_ids, block_size, batch_size, 20, torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed + 10_000))
    train = eval_loss(model, train_ids, block_size, batch_size, 20, torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed + 20_000))
    return val, train
```

```python
def entropy_floor_nats_per_char() -> float:
    def mean_len(lst):
        return sum(len(x) for x in lst) / len(lst)
    Es, Ev, Eo, Ea = mean_len(SUBJECTS), mean_len(VERBS), mean_len(OBJECTS), mean_len(ADVERBS)
    bits_per_sentence = 3 * math.log2(10) + 1 + 0.5 * math.log2(10)
    chars_per_sentence = Es + Ev + Eo + 0.5 * Ea + 3.5 + 1.0  # +1.0 joining space
    return (bits_per_sentence / chars_per_sentence) * math.log(2)

def fit_naive(params_arr, loss_arr):
    logP, logL = np.log(params_arr), np.log(loss_arr)
    neg_alpha, logA = np.polyfit(logP, logL, 1)
    alpha = -neg_alpha
    pred = logA - alpha * logP
    r2 = 1 - np.sum((logL - pred) ** 2) / np.sum((logL - logL.mean()) ** 2)
    return math.exp(logA), alpha, r2

def fit_floor_corrected(params_arr, loss_arr, l_inf):
    logP = np.log(params_arr)
    logL_resid = np.log(loss_arr - l_inf)
    neg_alpha, logA = np.polyfit(logP, logL_resid, 1)
    alpha = -neg_alpha
    pred = logA - alpha * logP
    r2 = 1 - np.sum((logL_resid - pred) ** 2) / np.sum((logL_resid - logL_resid.mean()) ** 2)
    return math.exp(logA), alpha, r2

def bootstrap_alpha_ci(by_n, fit_ns, l_inf, n_boot=2000, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    params_arr = np.array([by_n[n][0][0] for n in fit_ns], dtype=float)  # params don't vary by seed
    alphas = []
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        loss_boot = np.array([by_n[n][rng.integers(0, len(by_n[n]))][1] for n in fit_ns])
        _, alpha, _ = fit_floor_corrected(params_arr, loss_boot, l_inf)
        alphas.append(alpha)
    alphas = np.array(alphas)
    return float(alphas.mean()), float(np.percentile(alphas, 2.5)), float(np.percentile(alphas, 97.5))
```

```python
def run_compute_grid(widths, step_budgets, train_ids, val_ids, vocab_size, seed=0):
    rows = []
    for steps in step_budgets:
        for n_embd in widths:
            model = TinyLM(vocab_size, n_embd, n_layer=2, n_head=2, block_size=BLOCK)
            p = num_params(model)
            val, _ = train_model(model, train_ids, val_ids, BLOCK, BATCH, steps, lr=3e-3, seed=seed)
            tokens = steps * BATCH * BLOCK
            rows.append({"n_embd": n_embd, "steps": steps, "params": p,
                         "tokens": tokens, "flops": 6 * p * tokens, "val_loss": val})
    return rows

def compute_optimal_per_budget(grid):
    best = {}
    for row in grid:
        s = row["steps"]
        if s not in best or row["val_loss"] < best[s]["val_loss"]:
            best[s] = row
    return best

def compare_extrapolation(fit_alpha, fit_a, l_inf, held_out_params, actual_val_loss):
    predicted = l_inf + fit_a * held_out_params ** (-fit_alpha)
    abs_err = actual_val_loss - predicted
    rel_err_pct = 100 * abs_err / predicted
    return {"predicted": predicted, "actual": actual_val_loss,
            "absolute_error": abs_err, "relative_error_pct": rel_err_pct}
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — what a fitted curve quietly licenses you to say about a scale you never measured.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** You fit a power law on six measured points (`n_embd = 8` to `64`), got a log-log R² of 0.98, and used it to predict a seventh point you had not yet trained. The prediction missed by 196% — not because the fit was computed wrong, but because the point sat outside the regime (a stable learning rate) the fit was built inside. This is not a disputed finding; it is arithmetic you ran, on a model you trained, with a mechanism (Lesson 1's fixed-learning-rate instability) you can point to in your own logged runs.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Scaling laws" are reported, overwhelmingly, as if they were laws of nature — a curve you can trust past its own data because it fit so well inside it. The load-bearing move that makes this feel safe is leaving the fitted range unstated, or stating it once in a methods section and then dropping it from every headline claim built on top of it ("scale is all you need," stated with no scale attached). You now have, in your own repository, a measured demonstration of exactly how that move fails — not hypothetically, on a real fit, with a real number that was wrong by a factor of three. The field's own history contains the large-scale version of this: Kaplan et al. (2020)'s original scaling-law guidance shaped how an entire generation of large models were sized and trained, and Hoffmann et al. (2022) found, at real cost — real GPU-hours, real training runs — that a meaningful fraction of that guidance had been extrapolated past a regime it did not hold in, because a confound (learning-rate and batch-size scheduling across scale) had not been fully controlled for. You reproduced the *shape* of that failure, at a scale you could check in an afternoon; the field's version took years and enormous compute to catch and correct.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Every scaling claim you will ever make about a model you're building — "this will get better with more data," "we should just make it bigger," "scale will fix this failure mode" — is, structurally, the same move you just watched fail on a toy transformer: a curve fit inside a regime, extended past it, on the hope that whatever made the curve smooth inside will keep being true outside. Sometimes it will be. You now know, from your own measured miss, that "the R² was excellent" is not evidence either way. The question this module leaves you with is not whether scale will keep helping — you cannot know that in general, and neither can anyone who tells you otherwise with total confidence. It's whether, the next time you're the one making a scaling claim that other people's compute budgets, product timelines, or safety arguments will be built on top of, you will state the range it was fit on out loud, in the same sentence as the claim — not in a footnote, not in a methods section nobody reads before the headline gets repeated without it.

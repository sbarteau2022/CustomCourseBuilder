## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The linear-algebra argument: why a low-rank update is often sufficient

#### Teaching text

Full fine-tuning of one `nn.Linear(d_in, d_out)` layer means learning a full update matrix $\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$, with $d_{out}\times d_{in}$ free parameters, added to the frozen pretrained weight: $W' = W_0 + \Delta W$. **LoRA's entire proposal is a claim about the *rank* of $\Delta W$, not about its magnitude:** constrain $\Delta W$ to be expressible as a product of two much smaller matrices, $\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r}BA$, with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{in}}$, and $r \ll \min(d_{in}, d_{out})$. Any matrix of this form has rank **at most** $r$ — this is a hard linear-algebra fact, not an approximation, the same way a rank-1 outer product $uv^\top$ can never have rank 2 no matter what $u, v$ are. The empirical bet, from Hu et al., 2021 ("LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models"), building on Aghajanyan et al., 2020's earlier finding that fine-tuning updates have a surprisingly low **intrinsic dimension**, is that the *useful* part of a fine-tuning update — the part that actually moves the loss — lives almost entirely inside a small-rank subspace, and a rank-$r$ constraint with $r$ in the single or low double digits captures most of that useful part while discarding almost all of the $d_{out}\times d_{in}$ parameter budget full fine-tuning would have spent.

**This is the exact same shape of claim AIE-201-M04 made about a *data* matrix, reapplied to a *weight-update* matrix — and the connection is worth making precise, not just gestured at.** AIE-201-M04's Eckart–Young theorem said: given a **known** matrix $A$, the best possible rank-$k$ approximation to it, measured in Frobenius norm, is its own truncated SVD — keep the top $k$ singular values/vectors, and the reconstruction error is *exactly* $\sqrt{\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2}$, computable from the discarded singular values with no approximation in the *error formula* itself, only in the *approximation*. That theorem is a statement about compressing something you can already see in full. LoRA's situation is different in a way that matters: **the "ideal" $\Delta W$ that full fine-tuning would have found is never computed.** There is no known matrix sitting in memory for LoRA to run an SVD on and truncate — doing that would require running the exact full fine-tune LoRA exists to avoid, just to throw most of the result away afterward. LoRA instead **parameterizes the update as low-rank from the very first gradient step** and lets ordinary backpropagation find the best $A, B$ it can reach *under that constraint*, directly against the training loss. Eckart–Young answers "given the best possible full-rank answer, what's the best rank-$k$ compression of it?" LoRA answers a different question: "constrained to only ever search inside the rank-$r$ family, what does gradient descent find?" The two can agree closely in practice — that is the empirical bet — but nothing in LoRA's construction *guarantees* the $A, B$ gradient descent lands on is the Eckart–Young-optimal rank-$r$ projection of the $\Delta W$ full fine-tuning would have produced. Naming that gap precisely, instead of treating "LoRA is doing PCA on the fine-tuning update" as a clean equivalence, is this lesson's first real payoff.

**The parameter-count arithmetic, on this module's own model, made concrete.** A full update to `Linear(d_in, d_out)` costs $d_{in}\times d_{out}$ trainable parameters. A rank-$r$ LoRA update costs $r\times(d_{in}+d_{out})$ — $A$ contributes $r\times d_{in}$, $B$ contributes $d_{out}\times r$. For `qkv` (`Linear(128, 384)`, the largest attention linear in this module's model): full update $= 128\times384 = 49{,}152$; a rank-4 LoRA update $= 4\times(128+384) = 2{,}048$ — **4.17% of the full update's parameter cost**, for this one layer. This is the same "how many components explain most of the variance" question AIE-201-M04 Lesson 4 turned from a guess into a prediction — here reapplied to a weight matrix's own maximum possible rank: every linear layer in this module's model has $\min(d_{in}, d_{out}) = 128$ as its ceiling on rank (every layer's smaller dimension is $d_{model}=128$), so **even full fine-tuning is never using more than rank-128 updates**, and asking "does rank 4 or 8 suffice" is asking, concretely, whether roughly 3–6% of that ceiling captures most of what matters.

#### Named misconceptions

**"LoRA computes the SVD of the fine-tuning update and keeps the top-$r$ components."** Refuted directly above: there is no full $\Delta W$ ever materialized for LoRA to decompose — doing so would require completing the full fine-tune first. LoRA constrains the *parameterization* of the update to be low-rank from the first training step and lets gradient descent search inside that constrained family; Eckart–Young guarantees optimality for compressing a **known** matrix, and LoRA's setting — learning an **unknown** good update under a rank constraint — is a genuinely different problem that the same theorem does not certify an answer to. The two are related in spirit (both bet that most of a matrix's "useful content" concentrates in a small number of directions) and different in what is actually guaranteed.

**"A bigger rank is strictly better, so you should just use the largest rank your memory budget allows."** This lesson's own parameter-count table shows rank is a real trade-off, not a free dial: $r=16$ on attention+FFN already spends 131,072 parameters, 15% of the entire base model's parameter count, on the update alone — at some point "parameter-efficient" stops describing the fine-tune. Lesson 3's measured convergence data (below) is what actually tells you whether a given task needs that many, empirically, for this specific model and this specific distribution shift — not a rule of thumb.

### Lesson 2 — Implementing LoRA from scratch: the module, the zero-init identity, and weight merging

#### Teaching text

`LoRALinear` wraps one existing, already-trained `nn.Linear` — its weight and bias are **frozen** (`requires_grad = False`), never touched again by this module's optimizer — and adds two small trainable matrices computing an additive correction:

$$\text{LoRALinear}(x) = \underbrace{xW_0^\top + b_0}_{\text{frozen base, unchanged}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{r}\,(xA^\top)B^\top}_{\text{trainable low-rank update}}$$

**Two initialization choices are load-bearing, not arbitrary, and only one of them is optional.** $B$ **must** start at exactly zero. If both $A$ and $B$ started at some small random value, the wrapped layer's output at step 0 would already differ from the frozen pretrained model's own output — by a small, essentially random amount, but a real one — meaning the very first forward pass through a freshly LoRA-wrapped model would **not** reproduce the pretrained checkpoint's own behavior, silently. Zero-initializing $B$ makes $\frac{\alpha}{r}(xA^\top)B^\top \equiv 0$ regardless of what $A$ contains, guaranteeing the wrapped model computes **exactly** the same thing the base checkpoint did, before a single gradient step has been taken — a concrete, checkable "we haven't broken anything yet" property. $A$'s own initialization (this module uses `kaiming_uniform_`, the same scheme `nn.Linear` itself defaults to) is comparatively free — because it's multiplied by $B=0$, it has **zero effect on the model's initial output** — but it is not inert: it sets the initial *gradient direction* $B$ starts moving in once training begins, since $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial B$ depends on $A$ (through the forward pass's $xA^\top$ term) even while $B$ itself is still zero.

```python
# lora.py
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn


class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    """Wraps a frozen nn.Linear with a trainable low-rank update.

    W_eff = W_0 + (alpha / r) * B @ A
      A: (r, in_features)   init ~ kaiming_uniform (same convention nn.Linear
                             itself uses) -- affects gradient DIRECTION, not
                             the model's initial OUTPUT, because B=0.
      B: (out_features, r)  init to ZERO -- load-bearing. See teaching text.
    """

    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r: int, alpha: int, dropout: float = 0.0):
        super().__init__()
        assert r > 0
        self.base = base
        self.r = r
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.scaling = alpha / r
        for p in self.base.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False
        in_features, out_features = base.in_features, base.out_features
        self.lora_A = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(r, in_features))
        self.lora_B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(out_features, r))
        nn.init.kaiming_uniform_(self.lora_A, a=math.sqrt(5))
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(dropout) if dropout > 0 else nn.Identity()

    def forward(self, x):
        base_out = self.base(x)
        lora_out = self.drop(x) @ self.lora_A.t()
        lora_out = lora_out @ self.lora_B.t()
        return base_out + self.scaling * lora_out

    @torch.no_grad()
    def merged_weight(self):
        return self.base.weight + self.scaling * (self.lora_B @ self.lora_A)

    def extra_repr(self):
        return f"r={self.r}, alpha={self.alpha}, scaling={self.scaling:.4f}"


def apply_lora(model: nn.Module, target_names: list[str], r: int, alpha: int, dropout: float = 0.0):
    """Replaces every nn.Linear submodule whose LOCAL (dotted-path-final) name
    is in target_names with a LoRALinear wrapper, in place. Returns the count
    replaced -- always check this against Lesson 1's arithmetic before trusting
    a trainable-parameter count downstream of it."""
    replaced = 0
    for _, module in model.named_modules():
        for child_name, child in list(module.named_children()):
            if isinstance(child, nn.Linear) and child_name in target_names:
                setattr(module, child_name, LoRALinear(child, r=r, alpha=alpha, dropout=dropout))
                replaced += 1
    return replaced


def freeze_all_but_lora(model: nn.Module):
    for name, p in model.named_parameters():
        p.requires_grad = "lora_A" in name or "lora_B" in name


def count_trainable(model):
    return sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
```

`apply_lora(model, ["qkv", "proj"], r=4, alpha=8)` matches on a `nn.Linear`'s **local** child name inside its parent module — `"qkv"` matches every `CausalSelfAttention.qkv`, one per block, without needing to spell out `blocks.0.attn.qkv`, `blocks.1.attn.qkv`, ... by hand. This is a deliberate, minimal echo of how `peft`'s own `target_modules` matching works (Lesson 4) — built here from a plain `nn.Module.named_children()` walk, no library, so that Lesson 4's version has something exact to be compared against.

**Merging for inference.** Once training is done, `merged_weight()` folds the low-rank update directly into the base weight tensor: $W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA$. A model with every `LoRALinear` merged back into a plain `nn.Linear` computes **identically** to the wrapped version — same forward pass, same outputs — but pays **zero** extra inference latency and needs no `LoRALinear` bookkeeping at serving time, at the cost of no longer being able to swap a different adapter onto the same frozen base without re-merging.

#### Worked example — measured: the zero-init identity and the merge identity, both verified numerically

```python
>>> torch.manual_seed(0)
>>> m = TinyGPT(vocab_size=512, d_model=128, n_layer=4, n_head=4, d_ff=512, block_size=64)
>>> x = torch.randint(0, 512, (2, 64))
>>> logits_before, _ = m(x)                          # frozen-checkpoint forward pass
>>> apply_lora(m, ["qkv", "proj", "0", "2"], r=4, alpha=8)
16
>>> freeze_all_but_lora(m)
>>> logits_after_wrap, _ = m(x)                       # same input, freshly wrapped
>>> (logits_before - logits_after_wrap).abs().max().item()
0.0
```

Exactly `0.0` — not "close to zero," bit-identical — confirming $B=0$'s job: wrapping a checkpoint in `LoRALinear` changes nothing about what it computes until training actually moves $B$ away from zero. After 5 real `AdamW` steps (`lr=1e-2`, deliberately large to move the weights measurably), the merge identity is checked the same way, by computing the *same* layer's output two different ways — once through the still-wrapped `LoRALinear.forward`, once by explicitly calling `F.linear` against `merged_weight()` — on identical input:

```python
>>> merged_w = m.blocks[0].attn.qkv.merged_weight()
>>> direct_via_lora = m.blocks[0].attn.qkv(h)                       # same h both times
>>> direct_via_merge = F.linear(h, merged_w, m.blocks[0].attn.qkv.base.bias)
>>> (direct_via_lora - direct_via_merge).abs().max().item()
6.556510925292969e-07
```

Not exactly `0.0` this time — the two computations take different floating-point paths (one matrix product vs. two chained smaller ones, then an add, vs. one fused add-then-multiply) and floating-point arithmetic is not perfectly associative — but `6.6e-7` is six orders of magnitude below the loss values this module ever trains against, the textbook signature of float32 rounding noise, not a bug. **This distinction — "exactly zero" at the zero-init check vs. "float32-noise small" at the merge check — is itself a real, checkable fact worth predicting before running either test**, and Lab M06-A's Predict step asks for exactly that prediction.

#### Named misconceptions

**"Both $A$ and $B$ should be randomly initialized, the same way `nn.Linear`'s own weight is."** Directly refuted by the zero-init identity check above: random-initializing both would make the very first forward pass through a freshly wrapped checkpoint diverge from the checkpoint's own known-good behavior, by however much the random values happen to contribute — small, but real, and with no way to distinguish "the adapter is doing something" from "the adapter's random init happened to nudge this token's logit" during early training. $B=0$ is what makes "step 0 behaves identically to the un-adapted checkpoint" a guarantee instead of a hope.

**"Merging LoRA weights into the base model for inference changes what the model computes."** Directly refuted by the merge-identity check: the two forward paths agree to within float32 rounding noise ($6.6\times10^{-7}$), not "approximately" in some looser sense. Merging is a deployment-convenience operation (no more `LoRALinear` module, no extra add at inference time) with no change in mathematical content — the two things a learner might reasonably worry merging conflates (a real numerical simplification vs. an approximation that trades accuracy for speed) are not the same claim, and this module's own measured diff is the evidence for which one merging actually is.

### Lesson 3 — Real memory and compute trade-offs, measured on this module's own model

#### Teaching text

Two separate claims get conflated under "LoRA is efficient," and they have to be pulled apart, because this module's own measurements show they do **not** always point the same direction: **(1) LoRA needs far less memory for gradients and optimizer state**, because `AdamW` only tracks first- and second-moment estimates for parameters with `requires_grad=True`, and the frozen base weights never enter that accounting. **(2) LoRA does not necessarily run a training step *faster*** — the wrapped layer's forward pass still computes the **full** frozen linear (`self.base(x)`, unchanged FLOPs) **and then adds** the low-rank correction's own matmuls on top, meaning LoRA's forward pass is strictly **more** matrix multiplications than the plain linear it wraps, not fewer. Backward pass compute is reduced only for the frozen weight's own gradient (never computed, since `requires_grad=False`) — the activation gradient still has to propagate *through* every frozen layer to reach earlier ones, at essentially the same cost as if that layer were trainable.

**Memory footprint — computed directly from this module's own measured parameter counts, fp32 throughout.** Full-precision `AdamW` needs, per trainable parameter: 4 bytes for the parameter itself, 4 bytes for its gradient, 4 + 4 bytes for `AdamW`'s two moment estimates ($m$, $v$) — **16 bytes/trainable-parameter**, total. A frozen parameter needs only its own 4-byte storage — no gradient, no optimizer state, ever. Every size figure in this lesson and in Lesson 4's dtype table below is expressed in **MiB (1,024-based: 1 MiB = 1,048,576 bytes)** — stated once, here, so the two lessons' tables use the same basis and are directly comparable.

| configuration | trainable params | frozen params (this model) | footprint |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| full fine-tune | 867,072 | 0 | $867{,}072 \times 16\text{B} = 13.230\text{ MiB}$ |
| LoRA $r{=}4$, attn+FFN | 32,768 | 867,072 (base) | $32{,}768\times16\text{B} + 867{,}072\times4\text{B} = 3.808\text{ MiB}$ |

**A 3.475$\times$ reduction — real, but smaller than the headline "LoRA saves 99% of memory" claims you'll see quoted for billion-parameter models, and the reason why is itself the lesson.** At this module's toy 867K-parameter scale, the frozen base weights' own 4-byte-per-parameter storage is *not* negligible next to the LoRA adapter's 16-byte-per-parameter footprint — it is most of the LoRA configuration's total. At real production scale (a 7B-parameter model, say), the ratio of "how many parameters are frozen" to "how many are LoRA-trainable" is vastly more lopsided (millions of trainable LoRA parameters against billions of frozen ones, not thousands against hundreds of thousands), so the *optimizer-state* savings — the 12 bytes/parameter ($m$, $v$, and gradient) that a frozen parameter simply never pays — dominates the total far more completely than this toy model can demonstrate. This module's own ratio is real and reproducible; it is also honestly smaller than the number you'll see quoted for a 7B model, and knowing *why* the ratio changes with scale (which term in the 16-byte accounting a frozen parameter escapes, and how many frozen parameters there are) is the actual transferable skill, not the digit `3.475` itself.

**Wall-clock — measured, and genuinely counterintuitive.** On this module's 4-vCPU, CPU-only reference machine, batch 16 × block 64, 30 steps after 5 warmup steps:

| configuration | trainable params | measured time/step |
|---|---:|---:|
| full fine-tune | 867,072 | ~1.2–1.9 s |
| LoRA $r{=}4$, attn+FFN | 32,768 | ~2.3 s |

**LoRA was slower, per step, on this reference machine — consistently, across repeated runs.** This is exactly the "more matmuls, not fewer" mechanism named above, made concrete: each `LoRALinear.forward` call does the full base linear's matmul (unchanged) *plus two more*, smaller ones (`x @ A.T`, then `@ B.T`) — more total kernel launches, each individually tiny (`r=4` means one dimension of every LoRA matmul is just 4), which on a CPU with only 4 threads and no batching-across-kernels trick is dominated by per-call overhead rather than by the (very small) extra FLOPs. **This is a fact about this specific measurement's regime — small model, no GPU, small batch — not a general claim that LoRA is slower than full fine-tuning.** On a GPU, at real model scale, the frozen base matmul is the same either way, the added LoRA matmuls are a genuinely small fraction of the *total* FLOPs (unlike on this toy model, where the LoRA matmuls' *kernel-launch overhead* dominates), and the memory savings free up room for a *larger batch size*, which is where LoRA's actual GPU-training speed advantage usually comes from — not from doing less arithmetic per step, but from being able to do more steps' worth of batch in parallel because the optimizer state stopped being the memory bottleneck. Naming that this module's own measured slowdown is real, reproducible, *and* scale-and-hardware-specific — not evidence against LoRA in general — is the discipline this lesson is teaching.

**Does a small rank actually reach comparable loss? Measured, not assumed.** Starting from an identical "pretrained-stand-in" checkpoint (`TinyGPT`, 60 steps against a broad token distribution `[0, 512)`), fine-tuned for 40 further steps against a deliberately narrow, out-of-distribution target (tokens restricted to `[0, 64)` — a real distribution shift the model has to adapt to, not a no-op):

| configuration | trainable params | loss, step 0 | loss, step 40 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| full fine-tune | 867,072 | 6.2914 | 2.6148 |
| LoRA $r{=}1$, attn+FFN | 8,192 | 6.2914 | 5.9700 |
| LoRA $r{=}4$, attn+FFN | 32,768 | 6.2914 | 5.8157 |
| LoRA $r{=}16$, attn+FFN | 131,072 | 6.2914 | 5.2445 |

Step-0 loss is **identical** across every configuration (`6.2914`) — this is Lesson 2's zero-init identity, showing up again, now in a real training run rather than a unit test: a freshly wrapped LoRA model starts from the exact same place the full-fine-tune run does, because at step 0, before any gradient has moved $B$, they compute the same thing. By step 40, full fine-tuning has moved the loss down to `2.61`. The LoRA ranks improve **monotonically** with $r$, exactly as Lesson 1's rank-ceiling argument predicts — `5.97` → `5.82` → `5.24` as $r$ goes `1` → `4` → `16` — but **none of them, even $r{=}16$ at 131,072 trainable parameters (15% of the entire base model), gets close to full fine-tuning's `2.61` in this step budget.** That is a real result, not a disappointing one to explain away, and it has a specific, locatable cause rather than a vague "LoRA just isn't as good": **this module's `target_modules` never included the weight-tied `head`/`tok_emb` matrix** ("Before Lesson 1" named this exclusion explicitly, for the tying-complication reason given there). The fine-tune task here is "shift the output distribution from 512 possible next-tokens to 64" — a change that lives most directly in exactly the layer that was never made trainable. Every rank tested here is adapting the *attention and FFN* computation that feeds into an unshakeably frozen final projection — real progress (the monotonic improvement proves the attention/FFN adapters are learning *something* useful for the task), bounded well short of full fine-tuning's result, because full fine-tuning could also move the one matrix most directly responsible for "which tokens get high logits," and no rank of attention/FFN-only LoRA tested here can. **The lesson is not "LoRA needs a bigger rank here" — Lab M06-B's Predict item 2 asks you to reason about whether it's a *target-module* choice, not a *rank* choice, that would close this specific gap**, before you're told the answer.

#### Named misconceptions

**"LoRA trains faster because it has fewer trainable parameters."** Directly refuted by this lesson's own wall-clock table: fewer trainable parameters changes what the *optimizer* has to track, not how many matrix multiplications the *forward pass* has to run — and `LoRALinear`'s forward pass runs strictly more of them than the plain linear it wraps. "Fewer trainable parameters" is a real, measured claim about memory; "faster training" is a separate claim this module's own hardware directly contradicts, and conflating the two is exactly the kind of asserted-not-measured claim this course's discipline exists to catch.

**"The memory savings ratio you'll see quoted for a 7B-parameter LoRA fine-tune (often 3× or more beyond what this module measured) should also show up on a toy 867K-parameter model."** This lesson's own arithmetic shows why it doesn't: the frozen base weights' 4-byte storage is a much larger fraction of the *total* footprint at this module's scale than it is at billion-parameter scale, because the trainable-to-frozen parameter ratio is far less lopsided here. The mechanism (frozen parameters skip gradient + optimizer-state memory entirely) is identical at both scales; the *ratio* it produces is not, and a learner who expects this module's own numbers to generalize unchanged to a 7B model hasn't yet separated the mechanism from the specific model size it was measured on.

### Lesson 4 — Current tools: what `peft`'s LoRA and QLoRA add on top

*This lesson is a "current tools" section, per this course's mixed-durability discipline — deliberately placed after Lessons 1–3's from-scratch build, never before it. Everything below either runs on this module's reference machine and is labeled **measured**, or does not (no GPU) and is labeled **computed** (arithmetic from published byte-layout formulas) or **cited** (a number reported in the literature, not reproduced here).*

#### Teaching text

**`peft`'s `LoraConfig` + `get_peft_model` — measured, and cross-checked exactly against Lesson 2's from-scratch implementation.**

```python

>>> m = TinyGPT(vocab_size=512, d_model=128, n_layer=4, n_head=4, d_ff=512, block_size=64)
```

`12,288` — **the exact same number** Lesson 1's worked-example table measured for the from-scratch `apply_lora(m, ["qkv", "proj"], r=4, alpha=8)` on this identical model. `peft` does not know anything about `TinyGPT` specifically — it walks the model's module tree looking for `nn.Linear` (and a few other supported layer types) whose name matches `target_modules`, exactly the same operation `apply_lora`'s `named_children()` walk performs by hand in Lesson 2, and replaces each match with its own `peft`-internal LoRA layer implementing the identical $W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ math. This exact-match is not a coincidence to wave at — it is the actual verification that Lesson 2's hand-built module implements the real, standard algorithm, not a simplified stand-in for it.

**What `peft` adds that this module's `apply_lora` does not, named specifically:**

1. **Architecture-agnostic `target_modules` matching**, including regex patterns and Hugging Face `transformers` models' own naming conventions (`q_proj`, `v_proj`, `gate_proj`, ...) out of the box, so the same `LoraConfig` shape works unchanged whether the underlying model is this module's hand-built `TinyGPT` or a real pretrained model pulled from the Hub — Lesson 2's `apply_lora` only ever matches on a bare local child name.
2. **`merge_and_unload()`** — Lesson 2's `merged_weight()` computes one layer's merged tensor by hand; `peft`'s version walks the whole model, merges every adapted layer, and returns a plain model with no `peft`-specific wrapper classes left in it, ready to `torch.save` as an ordinary checkpoint.
3. **Adapter-only checkpointing.** `PeftModel.save_pretrained()` saves **only** the LoRA `A`/`B` matrices — not the frozen base model. For this module's own $r{=}4$, attn+FFN configuration: $32{,}768$ trainable parameters $\times$ 4 bytes (fp32) $= 131{,}072$ bytes $= 128\text{ KiB}$ (1,024-based: $131{,}072 / 1{,}024 = 128$), against $867{,}072\times4\text{B} \approx 3.31\text{ MiB}$ (same 1,024-based basis as the memory-footprint and dtype tables in this module) for the full model — an adapter file **about 4% the size** of the full checkpoint, computed directly from this module's own measured trainable-parameter count. At production model scale, this is the property that makes "keep 40 different task-specific adapters on disk, load whichever one a request needs" a genuinely cheap thing to do, where keeping 40 full fine-tuned copies of a 7B model would not be.

**QLoRA — what changes when the *frozen* base is quantized, not the trainable adapter.** LoRA (above) still stores the frozen base model at full or half precision. **QLoRA** (Dettmers et al., 2023) quantizes the frozen base weights to **4-bit NF4** ("NormalFloat4," a data type designed so that its 16 representable values are spaced to match the distribution neural network weights actually follow — roughly Gaussian — rather than being evenly spaced the way a naive 4-bit integer format would be), keeps the LoRA adapter's own $A$, $B$ matrices in bf16/fp32 as before, and adds two more mechanisms on top:

- **Double quantization.** Blockwise quantization (NF4 quantizes weights in small blocks, each block carrying its own scale/"absmax" constant so that outlier weights in one region of the model don't force every other region into an unnecessarily coarse quantization) itself has a memory cost — one scale constant per block. Double quantization quantizes *those scale constants themselves* to a lower precision, a second, smaller compression pass on top of the first. **Cited:** Dettmers et al., 2023 report this saves on average about 0.37 bits per parameter across a full model — small per-parameter, but multiplied across billions of parameters, a meaningful fraction of total memory.
- **Paged optimizers.** Uses NVIDIA unified memory to automatically page optimizer state between GPU and CPU memory when a GPU would otherwise run out during a training step's peak memory usage (common with gradient checkpointing's own memory spikes) — a mechanism with no CPU-only equivalent this module's reference machine can demonstrate; it exists specifically to prevent an out-of-memory crash on a GPU that would otherwise fit the workload on average but not at its single worst moment.

**Byte-per-parameter, by dtype — computed directly against this module's own 867,072-parameter model, since no GPU is available to measure it on real quantized kernels. Figures below are MiB (1,024-based), the same basis as Lesson 3's memory-footprint table:**

| dtype | bytes/param | this model's base-weight storage (867,072 params) |
|---|---:|---:|
| fp32 | 4.0 | 3.31 MiB |
| fp16 / bf16 | 2.0 | 1.65 MiB |
| int8 | 1.0 | 0.83 MiB |
| NF4 (4-bit) | 0.5 (+ small block-constant overhead) | ~0.41 MiB + ~0.05 MiB constants (block size 64) $\approx$ 0.47 MiB |

**Cited, at the scale this module's own model cannot reach:** the QLoRA paper's headline claim is that this stack of tricks (NF4 + double quantization + paged optimizers + LoRA adapters kept in higher precision) makes it possible to fine-tune a **65-billion-parameter** model on a **single 48GB GPU**, matching full 16-bit fine-tuning's task performance — a workload that would need on the order of 780GB+ of GPU memory for full 16-bit fine-tuning's own gradient and optimizer state. Nothing about this module's toy scale can reproduce that number; it is reported here as the literature-grounded reason QLoRA exists, not as a claim this packet verified on its own hardware.

#### Named misconceptions

**"QLoRA trains the model in 4-bit."** The frozen *base* weights are stored and (mostly) computed in 4-bit NF4; the trainable LoRA adapter matrices ($A$, $B$) are kept in bf16 or fp32, and gradients only ever flow into those higher-precision adapter parameters — the frozen base is dequantized on the fly for each forward/backward pass's matmul, then discarded again, never trained in 4-bit itself. "The model is quantized" and "the model is trained at that quantized precision" are different claims; QLoRA's whole design is aimed at getting the *memory* benefit of quantizing the frozen majority of the parameters while keeping the *trained* fraction at a precision where gradient updates behave normally.

**"Since `peft`'s trainable-parameter count matched Lesson 2's from-scratch count exactly, the library is just a convenience wrapper with nothing left to learn."** The exact match confirms the *core algorithm* is identical — it says nothing about the three concrete additions named above (architecture-agnostic matching, `merge_and_unload()`, adapter-only checkpointing), each of which is a real engineering capability this module's hand-built `apply_lora` does not have, and each of which is precisely why a production team reaches for `peft` once the algorithm itself is no longer the thing they need to verify.

```python
# lab_a_solution.py
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn


class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r: int, alpha: int, dropout: float = 0.0):
        super().__init__()
        assert r > 0
        self.base = base
        self.r = r
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.scaling = alpha / r
        for p in self.base.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False
        in_features, out_features = base.in_features, base.out_features
        self.lora_A = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(r, in_features))
        self.lora_B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(out_features, r))
        nn.init.kaiming_uniform_(self.lora_A, a=math.sqrt(5))
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(dropout) if dropout > 0 else nn.Identity()

    def forward(self, x):
        base_out = self.base(x)
        lora_out = self.drop(x) @ self.lora_A.t()
        lora_out = lora_out @ self.lora_B.t()
        return base_out + self.scaling * lora_out

    @torch.no_grad()
    def merged_weight(self):
        return self.base.weight + self.scaling * (self.lora_B @ self.lora_A)


def count_trainable(model):
    return sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
```

```python
# lab_b_solution.py
import copy
import torch
from model import TinyGPT, count_params
from lora import apply_lora, freeze_all_but_lora, count_trainable

VOCAB, BLOCK, BATCH = 512, 64, 8

def build():
    return TinyGPT(vocab_size=VOCAB, d_model=128, n_layer=4, n_head=4, d_ff=512, block_size=BLOCK)

def make_batch(vocab_lo, vocab_hi, batch=BATCH, block=BLOCK, seed=0):
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    x = torch.randint(vocab_lo, vocab_hi, (batch, block + 1), generator=g)
    return x[:, :-1], x[:, 1:]

def pretrain_stand_in(steps=60):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    model = build()
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
    x, y = make_batch(0, VOCAB, seed=1)
    for _ in range(steps):
        _, loss = model(x, y)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        opt.step()
    return copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())

def finetune(pretrained_state, target_names, r, alpha, n_steps=40):
    model = build()
    model.load_state_dict(pretrained_state)
    if target_names:      # empty list => full fine-tune, no wrapping
        apply_lora(model, target_names, r=r, alpha=alpha)
        freeze_all_but_lora(model)
        params = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    else:
        params = list(model.parameters())
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(params, lr=3e-4)
    x, y = make_batch(0, 64, seed=2)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        _, loss = model(x, y)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(params, 1.0)
        opt.step()
        losses.append(loss.item())
    return count_trainable(model) if target_names else count_params(model), losses[0], losses[-1]
```

```python
# lab_c_solution.py
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
from model import TinyGPT
from lora import apply_lora, freeze_all_but_lora, count_trainable

def build():
    return TinyGPT(vocab_size=512, d_model=128, n_layer=4, n_head=4, d_ff=512, block_size=64)

def peft_trainable_count(target_modules, r, alpha, bias="none"):
    m = build()
    cfg = LoraConfig(r=r, lora_alpha=alpha, target_modules=target_modules, lora_dropout=0.0, bias=bias)
    pm = get_peft_model(m, cfg)
    return sum(p.numel() for p in pm.parameters() if p.requires_grad)

def scratch_trainable_count(target_modules, r, alpha):
    m = build()
    apply_lora(m, target_modules, r=r, alpha=alpha)
    freeze_all_but_lora(m)
    return count_trainable(m)
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — what parameter-efficient fine-tuning actually lowers the bar to do — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** LoRA reduces the *trainable*-parameter footprint of adapting a model by one to two orders of magnitude at real model scale (this module's own toy measurement: 3.78% of base parameters at $r{=}16$, attention+FFN); QLoRA's NF4 quantization plus double quantization plus paged optimizers make it possible to fine-tune models an order of magnitude larger than would otherwise fit a given GPU's memory (cited: a 65B-parameter model on a single 48GB GPU, Dettmers et al., 2023). These are measured, citable facts about compute cost, not claims about what the resulting fine-tune is good *for*.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** The same mechanism that makes it cheap for a small team to adapt an open model to their own narrow, well-intentioned domain — a few hundred documents, one consumer GPU, an afternoon — makes it exactly as cheap to adapt that same model *away* from whatever safety- or alignment-tuning its publisher shipped it with. A LoRA adapter that steers a model toward compliant behavior and one that steers it toward refusing to refuse are the identical mechanism, differing only in which few thousand parameters got trained and on what data — this module built that mechanism by hand, in Lesson 2, and it does not know or care which direction it's pointed. Vendors who publish a "safety-tuned" open-weight model and vendors who publish PEFT tooling both, correctly, describe their own product accurately; neither sentence, said alone, tells you that the second one is a general-purpose tool for undoing the first one's work, at a fraction of the first one's own training cost.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You built, this module, the exact mechanism by which 96% or more of a model's weights can stay frozen — "the same base model," verifiably, by every checksum that matters — while a few thousand adapter parameters quietly determine what it will and won't say. When you are handed a model and told it is "the aligned version," what, precisely, are you being told is fixed, and what are you being told nothing at all about?

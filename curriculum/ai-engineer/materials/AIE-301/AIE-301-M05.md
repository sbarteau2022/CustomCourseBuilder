# AIE-301-M05 — Preference optimization: RLHF, DPO-family, reward hacking

**Course:** AIE-301 — Large Language Models: Pretraining to Post-Training · **Tier:** specialization · **Durability:** mixed

> **Design note.** Every number in this module — every win rate, every fitted reward, every policy probability, every KL value, every Spearman correlation — was **measured** by running the code shown below, not invented. The teaching problem is deliberately tiny: a policy choosing among **5 candidate completions** ("arms") of a single fixed prompt, rather than a full autoregressive vocabulary. This is not a simplification that hides anything essential — RLHF and DPO are both defined over *whatever distribution the policy puts over completions*, and the reason production RLHF uses PPO instead of an exact expectation is a **scale** problem (the vocabulary × sequence-length action space is too large to sum over), not a **conceptual** one. With 5 arms you can write out the exact sum, take its exact gradient, and watch the two methods land on numerically indistinguishable answers — the thing every "DPO is just implicit RLHF" claim asserts, and this module's Lab B makes you verify with your own trained numbers instead of taking on faith.

## Lessons

Four lessons: the RLHF pipeline by hand, the DPO loss derived and matched against it, a reward model gamed on purpose, and the diagnosis-and-fix that closes the loop — the same gaming-resistance discipline AIE-204-M02 taught, aimed here at a reward signal instead of a metric.

### Lesson 1 — RLHF traced by hand: Bradley-Terry, a reward model, and exact tabular policy optimization

#### Teaching text

RLHF, stripped of its production machinery, is three stages: collect pairwise human judgments, fit a scalar reward model to those judgments, then optimize a policy against that reward model while a KL penalty keeps it from wandering too far from where it started. Every production detail — PPO's clipped surrogate objective, the value-function baseline, generalized advantage estimation — exists to make stage three tractable when the policy's action space is an entire vocabulary at every position of a variable-length sequence. None of that machinery changes *what* is being optimized. This lesson builds the same three stages where the action space is 5 discrete "arms" — candidate completions of the fixed prompt "the model trains on the data ___" that differ only in their adverb — so stage three's objective can be summed out exactly.

**The arms and the judgment they're standing in for.** Five completions: `carefully`, `consistently`, `quickly`, `rarely`, `none` (no adverb). A trusted panel's revealed utility over these five — call it the **gold utility**, unknown to the reward model and the policy, used only to grade them, exactly the way an AIE-204 gold set exists to grade a metric it never trained on — is:

| arm | gold utility |
|---|---:|
| carefully | 1.00 |
| consistently | 0.70 |
| quickly | 0.40 |
| rarely | 0.10 |
| none | 0.20 |

This encodes a specific, named preference: a panel that values careful, deliberate phrasing over terse or hedged phrasing. **Whose panel this is, and what it would mean for a different panel to have written this column of numbers, is not a side note — it is this module's ethics thread, and Lesson 3 makes the stakes concrete.**

**Stage 1 — pairwise comparisons.** Real preference data comes from labelers shown two completions and asked which is better, not from a scalar rating (scalar ratings are noisy and hard to calibrate across labelers; forced-choice comparisons are the field's actual practice, from Christiano et al. 2017 through InstructGPT and every DPO-family paper since). Comparisons are generated by a **Bradley-Terry model**: for a random pair of arms `(i, j)`, the labeler prefers `i` with probability `σ(gold[i] − gold[j])`. 400 comparisons, sampled once (`seed=7`):

| arm | win rate | n comparisons |
|---|---:|---:|
| carefully | 0.560 | 159 |
| consistently | 0.547 | 159 |
| quickly | 0.477 | 174 |
| rarely | 0.468 | 141 |
| none | 0.449 | 167 |

Win rate already tracks the gold ranking loosely (carefully highest, rarely/none lowest) — but win rate mixes together "how good is this arm" and "how often was it compared against something weaker," which is exactly why a reward model, not a raw tally, is stage 2.

**Stage 2 — fit the reward model.** A reward model is a function from a completion to a scalar score, fit by minimizing the Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood over the comparisons: for chosen arm `c` and rejected arm `r`, minimize `−log σ(reward(c) − reward(r))`, averaged over all 400 pairs. Here the "reward model" is literally 5 free parameters (one score per arm) — at LM scale it's a transformer with a scalar head, but the loss and the gradient direction are identical. Fit by Adam, 300 steps:

| arm | gold | fitted reward |
|---|---:|---:|
| carefully | 1.00 | +0.161 |
| consistently | 0.70 | +0.135 |
| quickly | 0.40 | −0.080 |
| rarely | 0.10 | −0.155 |
| none | 0.20 | −0.186 |

`gold` rank: `carefully > consistently > quickly > none > rarely`. `reward-model` rank: `carefully > consistently > quickly > rarely > none`. **The reward model recovers the true top three exactly and only disagrees on the bottom two** — it ranks `rarely` below `none`, gold says the reverse. Read this as a statistics result, not a modeling failure: the gold gap between `rarely` (0.10) and `none` (0.20) is the smallest gap in the whole table, and 400 comparisons — plenty to resolve gaps of 0.3–0.6 — simply isn't enough evidence to resolve a gap of 0.1. This is the AIE-204-M03 power discipline arriving early: **a reward model's disagreements with ground truth are not all equally alarming; check whether the disagreement is on a large gap (a real error) or a small one (expected sampling noise) before deciding whether to worry.**

**Stage 3 — optimize the policy against the reward model, with a KL leash.** The RLHF objective is `maximize E_{a~π}[reward(a)] − β · KL(π ‖ π_ref)`, where `π_ref` is the policy before this step (the SFT policy from M04; here, uniform over the 5 arms) and `β` controls how far the optimization is allowed to drift from it. Because there are only 5 arms, both the expectation and the KL term are **exact finite sums** — no REINFORCE, no sampled rollouts, no variance to worry about. Optimizing this exactly, by gradient ascent on 5 logits, at several `β`:

| β | top arm | p(top arm) | E[gold] | KL to reference |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3.00 | carefully | 0.213 | 0.496 | 0.001 |
| 1.00 | carefully | 0.238 | 0.527 | 0.011 |
| 0.30 | carefully | 0.331 | 0.634 | 0.115 |
| 0.10 | carefully | 0.519 | 0.821 | 0.610 |
| 0.02 | carefully | 0.793 | 0.937 | 1.087 |

Read `β` as a dial, not a hyperparameter to tune once and forget: high `β` (3.00) barely moves the policy off uniform (KL ≈ 0.001) and only weakly prefers `carefully`; low `β` (0.02) lets the policy nearly collapse onto the reward model's favorite arm (p = 0.793, KL over 1000× larger). **Every row in this table is a correct optimum of a real, well-posed objective** — there is no universally "right" `β`; there is only the trade-off your deployment can tolerate between staying close to the reference policy's known behavior and chasing the reward model's judgment.

#### Worked example

The `β = 0.02` row is worth sitting with before moving on: `E[gold] = 0.937` is close to the gold utility of `carefully` itself (1.00), meaning the policy has nearly converted "a distribution over 5 arms" into "always say `carefully`." A KL of 1.087 nats is large for a 5-outcome distribution — for comparison, the maximum possible KL from uniform to a point mass on one of 5 arms is `log(5) ≈ 1.609`. This policy has moved roughly two-thirds of the way to a point mass, in a single training run, with a *correctly fit* reward model. **Nothing about this run is broken — this is exactly what "the RL step is working" looks like — and it is also exactly the shape of over-optimization the field call the reward-hacking regime once the reward model itself is even slightly wrong, which Lesson 3 shows next.** The mechanism that makes RLHF work (chase the reward model, subject to a leash) is the identical mechanism that makes it dangerous when the reward model is wrong — the leash's length is the only thing standing between the two.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"RLHF's reward model is a separate, minor detail from 'the real RL part.'** Stage 2 *is* where the preference signal enters the system — stage 3 is mechanical optimization against whatever stage 2 produced. Every property the final policy has, good or bad, was already latent in the reward model before a single RL step ran. This module's own numbers make that traceable: the `β=0.02` policy's near-collapse onto `carefully` is a direct, computable consequence of the reward model's own scores, not a separate thing RL "decided."
- **"A larger β is always the 'safer' choice."** Larger β does keep the policy closer to a known-behavior reference — but Lesson 3 shows a case where even the largest β tested still leaves the policy *worse than doing nothing at all* (E[gold] below the uniform reference's own 0.48), because β can only slow a bad reward model's influence, not fix it.
- **"The reward model's disagreement with gold on `rarely` vs. `none` means the reward model is unreliable."** The disagreement is on the single smallest gap in the gold table (0.10 vs. 0.20) with 300 total comparisons touching those two arms — exactly the regime where sampling noise, not model failure, is the expected explanation. Lesson 3's reward model disagrees with gold on gaps of 0.6–0.9, an entirely different magnitude of problem, and conflating the two is the mistake this whole module is built to make impossible.

### Lesson 2 — DPO from its actual loss function, matched against RLHF at equal β

#### Teaching text

Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) starts from an observation about Lesson 1's own stage 3: the KL-regularized RLHF objective has a **closed-form optimal policy** in terms of the reward model, `π*(a) ∝ π_ref(a) · exp(reward(a) / β)`. Substitute that closed form back into the Bradley-Terry preference model that stage 2 fit the reward model *to*, and the reward model itself algebraically cancels out, leaving a loss defined **directly on preference pairs, over the policy's own log-probabilities** — no reward model, no RL step, no stage-3 optimization loop at all:

```
L_DPO(π_θ) = −E_{(yw, yl)~D} [ log σ( β · ( (log π_θ(yw) − log π_ref(yw)) − (log π_θ(yl) − log π_ref(yl)) ) ) ]
```

`yw` is the chosen (preferred) arm, `yl` the rejected arm, from the same 400 comparisons Lesson 1 used to fit a reward model. Read the term inside `σ(·)` as a **margin between two log-probability ratios**: `log π_θ(yw)/π_ref(yw)` is "how much more likely does the policy now make the winning arm, relative to where it started," and the loss pushes that margin to be larger than the same quantity for the losing arm. This is a completely ordinary binary cross-entropy loss — `−log σ(margin)` — the same functional form as the reward model's own Bradley-Terry fit in Lesson 1, except the thing being fit is the *policy's log-probabilities themselves*, treated as an implicit reward.

**Training DPO directly on the 400 comparisons, at the same β values Lesson 1 swept, against the same uniform `π_ref`:**

| β | RLHF top arm | RLHF p(top) | RLHF E[gold] | DPO top arm | DPO p(top) | DPO E[gold] | max \|Δp\| across all 5 arms |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3.00 | carefully | 0.213 | 0.496 | carefully | 0.213 | 0.496 | 0.0000 |
| 1.00 | carefully | 0.238 | 0.527 | carefully | 0.238 | 0.527 | 0.0000 |
| 0.30 | carefully | 0.331 | 0.634 | carefully | 0.331 | 0.634 | 0.0000 |
| 0.10 | carefully | 0.519 | 0.821 | carefully | 0.519 | 0.821 | 0.0000 |
| 0.02 | carefully | 0.793 | 0.937 | carefully | 0.790 | 0.937 | 0.0050 |

**These are two independently trained policies — one fit through an explicit reward model and an exact-expectation RL step, the other fit by gradient descent directly on the pairwise comparisons — and at every β they land on the same policy to three decimal places.** The one row with a nonzero difference (`β = 0.02`, Δp = 0.005) is optimization slop, not a theoretical gap: at very low β the loss surface is nearly flat near the optimum, and 500 Adam steps land close to, but not exactly on, the same point both times. This is the DPO paper's central theoretical claim, made checkable: **DPO's implicit reward, recovered from the trained policy via `β · log(π_θ(a)/π_ref(a))`, is (up to a global additive constant, which cancels in every Bradley-Terry comparison) the same reward function RLHF's explicit stage 2 would have fit** — DPO just skips the step of materializing it as a separate, inspectable object.

**That last clause is not a footnote — it is the entire subject of Lesson 4.** DPO reaching the same optimum as RLHF is good news when the reward model would have been *correct*. It is not obviously good news when the reward model would have been wrong, because RLHF's stage 2 gives you an artifact to inspect — a table of scores you can rank-check against a small trusted probe *before* spending a single RL step (Lesson 1's power-discipline aside) — and DPO, by construction, never produces that artifact at all.

#### Worked example

Derive, on paper, what happens to the DPO loss when `π_θ = π_ref` (the policy hasn't moved yet, at initialization). Both log-ratio terms are zero, so the margin is `β · (0 − 0) = 0`, and `−log σ(0) = −log(0.5) = log 2 ≈ 0.693` for every pair — the loss at initialization is `log 2` regardless of which pair was sampled or what β is. Check this against the code: at step 0 of the training loop, before any Adam step has run, `theta` is all zeros, `log π_θ = log π_ref` for every arm, and the very first loss value printed (add a debug print to the lab starter to see it) is `0.6931...` — `log 2` to four decimal places, every run, every seed. **A loss that doesn't start at a value you can predict on paper is a bug, not a training run** — this is the DPO-specific version of the sealed-prediction habit this course has required since AIE-100, now aimed at a loss function instead of a program's output.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"DPO is a completely different algorithm from RLHF."** The two policies in the table above are numerically indistinguishable at every tested β — DPO is a re-parameterization of the same optimization problem RLHF solves, derived by substituting RLHF's own closed-form optimal policy into the same Bradley-Terry preference model both methods start from. What differs is *how* the optimum is reached (an explicit RM plus an RL loop, vs. one gradient-descent loop directly on preference pairs), not *what* optimum is reached.
- **"Because DPO's loss doesn't mention a reward model, DPO removes reward hacking from the pipeline."** Lesson 4 measures this directly and it is false: DPO trained on the *same* flawed preference data that produces a hacked reward model in Lesson 3 reaches the *same* hacked policy — the vulnerability lives in the preference data, not in the presence or absence of an explicit RM stage. What DPO removes is the RM as an *inspectable artifact*, which is a diagnostic loss, not a robustness gain.
- **"A higher β always means 'more conservative' for both methods, by the same amount."** Both methods use β as a KL-strength dial and land at matched KL values by construction here (they were fit to converge to the same optimum) — but this equivalence assumed both methods were run to full convergence against a *correct* Bradley-Terry preference model. Lesson 4 shows what "matched β" buys you when that assumption is false, and the answer is: not the same protection you'd expect from Lesson 1's clean case.

### Lesson 3 — Reward hacking, deliberately induced

#### Teaching text

Everything in Lessons 1–2 assumed the pairwise comparisons genuinely reflected the gold utility, subject only to Bradley-Terry sampling noise. Real labeling pipelines are not that clean: labelers skim instead of read, instructions get followed inconsistently, and **proxies correlated with quality get mistaken for quality itself** — verbosity read as thoroughness, hedging read as caution, length read as effort. This lesson builds exactly that failure, on purpose, the same way AIE-204-M02's Lab M02-B built a metric exploit by hand before trusting any metric again.

**The induced bias.** Replace the honest Bradley-Terry sampler with one where the labeler's revealed preference tracks completion **length in characters**, not the gold utility at all: `P(i preferred over j) = σ(k · (len(j) − len(i)))` — shorter wins more often, with `k = 0.8` controlling how strong the bias is. This models a real, documented failure mode: labelers under time pressure who skim for "crisp" phrasing rather than reading for the quality the task brief actually asked them to judge. 400 comparisons, same protocol as Lesson 1 otherwise:

| arm | length (chars) | gold utility | fitted reward |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| carefully | 9 | 1.00 | −1.247 |
| consistently | 12 | 0.70 | −3.617 |
| quickly | 7 | 0.40 | +1.560 |
| rarely | 6 | 0.10 | +2.226 |
| none | 4 | 0.20 | +3.543 |

**gold** rank: `carefully > consistently > quickly > none > rarely`. **reward-model** rank: `none > rarely > quickly > carefully > consistently`. **The ranking is not just wrong — it is close to inverted.** Spearman correlation between the gold rank and the reward-model rank: **ρ = −0.800**. This reward model actively disagrees with the panel it was supposedly trained to reflect, and it disagrees confidently: the reward gap between `none` (+3.543) and `consistently` (−3.617) is larger in magnitude than any gap in the honest Lesson 1 reward model — the biased comparisons produced a reward model that is not just noisy but **decisively, fluently wrong**.

**Optimizing against it.** Run the exact same RLHF stage-3 procedure as Lesson 1 — same objective, same code, same β sweep — against this reward model instead:

| β | top arm | p(top) | reward-model E[reward] | true E[gold] | KL to reference |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3.00 | none | 0.407 | 2.117 | 0.301 | 0.236 |
| 1.00 | none | 0.707 | 3.063 | 0.206 | 0.783 |
| 0.30 | none | 0.988 | 3.521 | 0.200 | 1.534 |
| 0.10 | none | 0.997 | 3.536 | 0.200 | 1.587 |
| 0.02 | none | 0.998 | 3.537 | 0.200 | 1.592 |

**This is reward hacking, measured, not asserted.** As β decreases, the reward-model score the policy is chasing climbs monotonically (2.117 → 3.537, exactly the training signal working as designed) while the **true** quality (E[gold]) *falls* (0.301 → 0.200) and then flattens near the worst arm in the entire table. The policy has learned to say nothing (`none`, the empty adverb) with near certainty — it has found the shortest possible completion, exactly what the biased labeling process rewarded, and exactly what the gold panel ranked fourth of five. **The training curve you'd actually see running this — reward-model score rising — looks identical, from inside the training loop, to a policy genuinely getting better.** The only way to tell the difference is to check against something the reward model was never allowed to grade itself on, which is Lesson 4's entire subject.

#### Worked example

Compute the **uniform reference policy's own** E[gold]: `mean(GOLD) = (1.00 + 0.70 + 0.40 + 0.10 + 0.20) / 5 = 0.48`. Now compare it to the `β = 3.00` row above: **E[gold] = 0.301, worse than doing nothing at all.** Even the most conservative β tested in this sweep — the one that moved the policy least (KL = 0.236, the smallest KL in the table) — already made true quality *worse* than the untouched reference policy. This is the sharpest version of the lesson: **a reward model wrong enough, optimized against at all, can beat a "do nothing" baseline in the wrong direction**, and no amount of conservatism inside the same flawed optimization loop reliably fixes that, because the direction of every gradient step, at every β, was computed from the same wrong reward model. Conservatism controls how far you go; it does not control which way you're facing.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"More comparisons would have fixed this reward model."** Lab C measures this directly: 4,000 comparisons from the identical biased sampler (10× more data, same process) reproduce the *exact same* inverted ranking — `none > rarely > quickly > carefully > consistently`, unchanged. More samples of a systematically biased process converge to a more precisely wrong answer, not a correct one. The fix is a different *process* (Lesson 4), never more volume from the same one.
- **"If the reward-model training loss looks fine (converges, low BCE loss), the reward model is fine."** The biased reward model's training loss converges cleanly — it is a very good fit to the 400 biased comparisons it was given. A converged, low-loss reward model and a *correct* reward model are different claims; the loss only ever certifies fit to the data it saw, and this lesson's whole point is that the data itself was the problem.
- **"Reward hacking is a large-model phenomenon, not something you'd see at toy scale."** This module reproduces it in a 5-parameter tabular reward model and a 5-arm exact-expectation policy, with no neural network training instability, no sampling variance, and no scale of any kind involved. The mechanism (optimize hard enough against a wrong proxy and the proxy's blind spots become the policy's strategy) is scale-independent — it is a property of *optimizing against any imperfect signal*, which is why it appears at every scale this field has ever tried.

### Lesson 4 — Diagnosing the hack, and why DPO doesn't dodge it

#### Teaching text

AIE-204-M02 built a rule for catching a gamed metric before trusting it: check the metric against cases chosen because they're revealing, not because they're convenient, and check for agreement with an independent, trusted judgment. Apply the identical rule to Lesson 3's reward model, **before** spending a single RL step on it.

**Diagnostic 1 — a small, trusted probe set.** Twelve direct gold-labeled comparisons (expensive to obtain — this is why you use a *small* trusted set, not a replacement for the cheap-but-biased pipeline that produced the 400): does the reward model's preference on each pair match the gold utility's preference?

```
hacked RM agrees with gold on 4/12 trusted probe comparisons (33%) — caught before spending
a single RL step.
```

**33% agreement is worse than the 50% a coin flip would produce.** This is not "somewhat unreliable" — it is a reward model that is, on this probe set, anti-correlated with the thing it was supposed to measure, and twelve comparisons — a number small enough to hand-label yourself in an afternoon — is enough to see it. Compare this to Lesson 1's honest reward model, whose only disagreement with gold was on the smallest gap in the table: the diagnostic distinguishes "a well-fit reward model with expected sampling noise on a hard case" from "a reward model optimizing something else entirely," which is exactly the distinction a training loss alone cannot make.

**Diagnostic 2 — rank correlation against the same probe set, or against the full gold table when (as here, for teaching purposes) you have it.** Spearman ρ = −0.800, reported already in Lesson 3. A well-specified reward model's ρ against gold, computed the same way on Lesson 1's honest reward model, is `+1.0` on the top three arms and disagrees only on the bottom two — strongly positive. **Sign matters more than magnitude here**: a positive-but-imperfect ρ is Lesson 1's story (usable, some noise); a strongly negative ρ is Lesson 3's story (actively adversarial to the thing it claims to measure), and no single scalar "reward model accuracy" number would have surfaced that distinction as clearly as looking at the sign of a rank correlation.

**What does NOT fix it.** Lesson 3 already showed more data from the same biased process doesn't move the ranking. Raising β on the *same* hacked reward model is the next thing an operator under pressure reaches for — it's cheap, it doesn't require re-collecting data — so measure it honestly instead of assuming it works:

| β | top arm | E[gold] | KL to reference |
|---:|---|---:|---:|
| 0.30 | none | 0.200 | 1.534 |
| 1.00 | none | 0.206 | 0.783 |
| 3.00 | none | 0.301 | 0.236 |
| 8.00 | none | 0.399 | 0.046 |

Even at `β = 8.00` — a KL of 0.046, the policy barely distinguishable from the untouched reference — **E[gold] = 0.399 is still below the uniform baseline's own 0.48.** Turning up the leash slows the collapse toward `none` but never reverses the direction the wrong reward model is pulling in; you can make the damage small, but every nonzero step against this reward model is still a step in the wrong direction. **β is a dial for how much you trust the reward model, not a substitute for the reward model being trustworthy.**

**What does fix it.** Re-collect the comparisons under an instrument that isn't confounded with length — Lesson 1's honest Bradley-Terry sampler is exactly that fix, already measured: retraining on the properly-specified 400 comparisons and running RLHF at a moderate β = 1.00 recovers `E[gold] = 0.527`, more than double the hacked pipeline's best result (0.399) at a far *less* conservative β. **The fix was never in the optimization step — it was in the labeling process the reward model was fit to**, which is exactly what Diagnostic 1 and 2 exist to catch before any compute is spent on stage 3 at all.

**Does DPO dodge any of this, since it skips the explicit reward-model stage?** Train DPO directly on Lesson 3's same 400 length-biased comparisons — no reward model materialized anywhere in this run:

| β | DPO top arm | DPO E[gold] | DPO KL to reference |
|---:|---|---:|---:|
| 3.00 | none | 0.301 | 0.236 |
| 1.00 | none | 0.206 | 0.788 |
| 0.30 | none | 0.199 | 1.498 |
| 0.10 | none | 0.200 | 1.599 |
| 0.02 | none | 0.195 | 1.292 |

**Within noise, identical to the hacked RLHF numbers, row for row.** DPO collapses onto `none` exactly as decisively, because the vulnerability was never in the RL step — it was in the 400 comparisons, and DPO is fit to those same 400 comparisons just as directly as the reward model was. **What DPO actually removed is Diagnostic 1 and 2's target: there is no scalar reward table to rank-check against a trusted probe before deployment, because DPO never produces one.** You can still run the equivalent check — compute DPO's *implicit* reward, `β · log(π_θ(a)/π_ref(a))`, for each arm, and rank-correlate that against a trusted probe exactly as before — but it requires knowing to compute an artifact the training loop never asked you to produce, and Lab C's Predict step is built to catch a learner who assumes DPO's silence on this point means safety rather than an extra step they now owe themselves.

#### Worked example

State, precisely, why Diagnostic 1 (a 12-comparison trusted probe) is affordable and Lesson 3's fix (re-collecting all 400 comparisons under a better protocol) is not something you'd do *by default* every time — the asymmetry is the entire practical argument for running Diagnostic 1 first: a 12-comparison probe against a reward model you already have costs nothing but the 12 labels; discarding and re-collecting 400 comparisons costs the entire labeling budget again. **The probe's job is not to fix anything — it's to tell you, cheaply, whether the expensive fix is necessary**, before you either waste the expensive fix on a reward model that didn't need it, or skip it on one that desperately does.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"DPO's popularity as 'simpler and more stable than RLHF' means it's also safer."** Simpler to implement (one loss, no separate RM, no RL loop) and safer against a bad preference signal are unrelated claims — this lesson's matched table shows DPO reproduces the identical hacked policy from the identical biased data. Simplicity removed engineering surface area; it did not remove the data-quality dependency both methods share.
- **"Once you've validated a reward model against a trusted probe, it's validated for good."** The probe validates the reward model against the *specific* failure mode it was checked for (here: length bias). A reward model that passes a length-bias check could still be gamed by some other proxy the probe set didn't happen to include — this is the same "fixing today's exploit isn't a promise about tomorrow's" lesson AIE-204-M02 named for metrics, now true of reward models for the identical structural reason.
- **"β=8 'basically worked' because E[gold]=0.399 isn't catastrophic like β=0.02's 0.200."** 0.399 is still below the 0.48 you'd get from *not training at all*. "Less bad than the worst row in the table" is not the same claim as "acceptable," and reporting only the relative improvement across β without the uniform-baseline comparison is exactly how a genuinely harmful reward model gets reported as "the conservative setting worked fine."

```python
# pref_opt.py — provided
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

ARMS = ["carefully", "consistently", "quickly", "rarely", "none"]
N = len(ARMS)

# The trusted panel's revealed utility -- unknown to any reward model or
# policy trained below; used only to GRADE them, never to train them.
GOLD = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.7, 0.4, 0.1, 0.2])

def sample_comparisons(reward: torch.Tensor, n_pairs: int, seed: int):
    """Bradley-Terry sampling: for random arm pairs (i, j), the labeler picks
    i over j with probability sigmoid(reward[i] - reward[j])."""
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    i = torch.randint(0, N, (n_pairs,), generator=g)
    j = torch.randint(0, N, (n_pairs,), generator=g)
    j = torch.where(i == j, (j + 1) % N, j)
    p_i_wins = torch.sigmoid(reward[i] - reward[j])
    coin = torch.rand(n_pairs, generator=g)
    i_wins = coin < p_i_wins
    chosen = torch.where(i_wins, i, j)
    rejected = torch.where(i_wins, j, i)
    return chosen, rejected

def kl_to_uniform(p: torch.Tensor) -> float:
    ref = torch.full((N,), 1.0 / N)
    return (p * (p / ref).log()).sum().item()

def gold_expected(p: torch.Tensor) -> float:
    return (p * GOLD).sum().item()

def spearman(rank_a: list[int], rank_b: list[int]) -> float:
    """rank_a, rank_b: arm indices, best-first."""
    pos_a = {a: r for r, a in enumerate(rank_a)}
    pos_b = {a: r for r, a in enumerate(rank_b)}
    d2 = sum((pos_a[i] - pos_b[i]) ** 2 for i in range(N))
    return 1 - 6 * d2 / (N * (N**2 - 1))

def sealed_predict(name, answers: dict, path="predictions.jsonl"):
    import json, time
    record = {"lab": name, "ts": time.time(), "answers": answers}
    with open(path, "a") as f:
        f.write(json.dumps(record) + "\n")
    return record
```

```python
def train_reward_model(chosen, rejected, steps=300, lr=0.1):
    r = torch.zeros(N, requires_grad=True)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([r], lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        margin = r[chosen] - r[rejected]
        loss = -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return r.detach()

def rlhf_policy(reward_model, beta, steps=500, lr=0.05):
    theta = torch.zeros(N, requires_grad=True)
    ref_logp = torch.log(torch.full((N,), 1.0 / N))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([theta], lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        logp = F.log_softmax(theta, dim=0)
        p = logp.exp()
        expected_reward = (p * reward_model).sum()
        kl = (p * (logp - ref_logp)).sum()
        loss = -(expected_reward - beta * kl)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return F.softmax(theta.detach(), dim=0)
```

```python
def dpo_loss(theta: torch.Tensor, ref_logp: torch.Tensor,
             chosen: torch.Tensor, rejected: torch.Tensor, beta: float) -> torch.Tensor:
    """TODO: the actual DPO loss (Rafailov et al. 2023, eq. 7), for a
    CATEGORICAL policy over N arms (theta -> log_softmax(theta) is log pi_theta).
        margin = beta * ( (logp[chosen]  - ref_logp[chosen])
                         - (logp[rejected] - ref_logp[rejected]) )

    Return a scalar tensor. Use F.log_softmax and F.logsigmoid -- do not
    reimplement sigmoid/log by hand (numerical stability)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

               steps: int = 500, lr: float = 0.05) -> torch.Tensor:
    """TODO: optimize `theta` (shape (N,), init zeros) against dpo_loss with
```

Reference solution:

```python
def dpo_loss(theta, ref_logp, chosen, rejected, beta):
    logp = F.log_softmax(theta, dim=0)
    logp_w, logp_l = logp[chosen], logp[rejected]
    ref_w, ref_l = ref_logp[chosen], ref_logp[rejected]
    margin = beta * ((logp_w - ref_w) - (logp_l - ref_l))
    return -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean()

def dpo_policy(chosen, rejected, beta, steps=500, lr=0.05):
    theta = torch.zeros(N, requires_grad=True)
    ref_logp = torch.log(torch.full((N,), 1.0 / N))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([theta], lr=lr)
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = dpo_loss(theta, ref_logp, chosen, rejected, beta)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return F.softmax(theta.detach(), dim=0)
```

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime.

- **Wall — the KL-regularized objective's algebra (`E[reward] − β·KL`) reads as "just a formula" until β is swept.** Learners who only read Lesson 1's teaching text without running Lab A's β sweep tend to treat β as a fixed constant rather than a genuine dial with real consequences at both extremes. **Reroute:** before assigning Lab A's Build step, have the learner hand-compute (on paper, N=5, β=1) the policy that maximizes the objective for just two candidate distributions (uniform vs. a point mass on the top arm) and compare the objective values — this makes the trade-off concrete before code enters the picture.
- **Wall — "why does substituting the closed-form optimal policy into Bradley-Terry make the reward model cancel out" (Lesson 2's derivation).** This is real algebra, not hand-waving, and a learner who nods along without reproducing it on paper will treat DPO as "an alternative someone invented" rather than "the same optimum, reached differently" — which directly weakens their Lesson 4 diagnosis (they won't expect DPO to inherit RLHF's exact vulnerability, because they never verified the two are the *same* optimization problem). **Reroute:** assign the log-2-at-initialization derivation (Lab B, Predict step 1) as a gate before Lab B's Build step — a learner who can't derive that value on paper has not yet internalized the substitution and should revisit Lesson 2's teaching text, not proceed to code.
- **Wall — sign errors in the DPO margin (`chosen − rejected` vs. `rejected − chosen`).** This is the single most common Lab B bug, and it is dangerous specifically because a sign-flipped DPO loss still trains, still converges, and produces a *coherent but backwards* policy (one that learns to prefer rejected completions) — a program that runs is not evidence it's correct, exactly AIE-100's founding claim. **Reroute:** the `log 2`-at-initialization test catches magnitude bugs but not sign bugs (both signs give the same loss at `θ=0`); if a learner's Lab B policy converges but disagrees with Lab A's RLHF policy in *direction* (prefers the RLHF loser), send them straight to the margin line, not to hyperparameters.
- **Signal — a learner surprised that DPO reproduces the reward hack.** This is exactly on track, not a misunderstanding to correct quickly — the surprise is Lesson 4 landing. Log `struggle-productive`. A learner who is *not* at all surprised, and immediately assumed DPO would inherit the vulnerability without being able to say why, should be asked to justify that intuition on paper before being credited for it — a right answer for the wrong reason here is a coverage gap waiting to reappear on the exam's unseen sampler.
- **Wall — "more data should always help" resurfacing from AIE-301-M03's own scaling lessons.** M03 taught that more data helps, up to a compute-bound plateau — a learner pattern-matching from that module may expect Lab C's 10× data experiment to at least partially fix the ranking. **Reroute:** have the learner state, before running the 10×-data test, what M03's overfitting-gap lesson was actually about (a fixed-size sample from an *unbiased* generator) versus what Lab C's sampler is (an unlimited, but *systematically biased*, generator) — the two "more data" questions look similar and have opposite answers for a reason worth naming explicitly, not glossing over as a coincidence.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — whose preferences fill the reward signal, and what "aligned" means once you've built the pipeline that produces it — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Reward hacking against a proxy is not a hypothetical risk this module invented for teaching purposes — it is a documented, repeatedly observed failure mode wherever a policy is optimized against a fitted, imperfect stand-in for an unmeasurable true objective (Goodhart's law, operating here on a reward model exactly as AIE-204-M05 shows it operating on a business metric). This module's own numbers make one instance of it fully inspectable: a specific labeling bias (length), a specific reward model it produced (`ρ = −0.800` against gold), and a specific, measured policy collapse (`E[gold]` falling below the do-nothing baseline at every β tested). None of this is metaphorical.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "RLHF aligns the model to human preferences" suppresses the load-bearing question of **which** humans, selected how, instructed with what rubric, paid what, and given how much time per comparison. This module's own toy pipeline makes the mechanism undeniable: the *entire* difference between Lesson 1's usable reward model and Lesson 3's inverted one was not the algorithm — both used identical training code — it was **who effectively did the judging and what they were actually measuring when they thought they were measuring quality**. At production scale, the analogous fact is: a reward model trained on comparisons from a specific labeling vendor, under specific time-per-task incentives, in a specific set of instructions written by a specific product team, becomes "what the model is aligned to" — and that chain of specific, contingent choices is precisely the part most public descriptions of "RLHF-aligned" models omit, replacing it with the passive, agentless "aligned to human preferences," as if preferences were a single, uncontested thing rather than a chain of choices with a name attached at every link.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You built a reward model that can be gamed by nothing more exotic than a labeler skimming for short answers instead of reading carefully — a bias so mundane it requires no malice, only time pressure, to produce. Every reward signal you will ever train a real policy against was assembled by a real, specific, time-pressured, incentivized set of people, making real, specific, sometimes-invisible-to-themselves judgment calls about what "better" meant in that instant. You now have, in your own hands, the tools to fit a policy to whatever that signal turns out to actually encode — correctly, faithfully, and without complaint, exactly as Lesson 3's `none`-collapsing policy did. When you ship a preference-tuned model and call it "aligned," the honest version of that sentence names whose judgment it was fit to, and admits you may not fully know. What do you owe the people whose specific, contingent judgments become, in your hands, "what the model prefers" — and what does it cost, to whom, when that chain gets called alignment instead of what it is?

## Current tools (isolated — mixed durability)

This module's durable core is the RLHF objective, the Bradley-Terry reward model, the DPO loss derivation, and the reward-hacking diagnostic method — none of that changes as libraries version. What follows is swappable and will drift; treat it as a pointer, not something to memorize.

- `trl.RewardTrainer` wraps Lab A's `train_reward_model` for a real transformer with a scalar head, handling batching and mixed precision.
- `trl.PPOTrainer` wraps Lab A's `rlhf_policy` step at LM scale, replacing the exact expectation with clipped-surrogate PPO, a learned value-function baseline, and generalized advantage estimation — necessary specifically because a real vocabulary makes the exact sum intractable, not because the objective changed.
- `trl.DPOTrainer` wraps Lab B's `dpo_loss` directly — read its source once; it is, at its core, the same margin-and-`logsigmoid` computation you already implemented, generalized to per-token log-probabilities summed over a generated sequence instead of a single categorical draw.
- Newer DPO-family variants (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO — the field adds one every few months) each change the loss's functional form in a specific, statable way (e.g., IPO replaces the logistic loss with a squared-error form to reduce sensitivity to deterministic preferences) — the method for evaluating any of them is unchanged from this module: fit it, then run Lab C's diagnostics against a trusted probe before trusting the result, regardless of which paper's loss you used.

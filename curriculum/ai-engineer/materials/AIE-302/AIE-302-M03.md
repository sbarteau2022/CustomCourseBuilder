## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Chunking is not preprocessing: it decides what can ever be retrieved

Every later stage in this module — embedding, indexing, reranking, citation — operates only on whatever chunking handed it. A fact that chunking split away from the sentence that gives it meaning is not a fact any embedding model, any rerank stage, or any citation checker downstream can ever get back; the information was discarded before any of those stages ran. This is the sense in which chunking is not a preprocessing footnote to a RAG system — it is a decision, made once, about what the entire system is capable of ever saying.

**The running document.** Every lesson and Lab M03-A in this module use the same five-section policy document (Lab M03-B and M03-C extend it with two more sections). Read it once, in full, before anything else — every number in this lesson is computed directly from it:

```
# Return Policy

A refund is issued within five days after we receive the returned item. This
does not apply to items marked final sale.

# Shipping Policy

Standard shipping typically takes a week from the order date. This does not
apply to expedited orders paid at checkout.

# Warranty Policy

The warranty covers defects in materials for one year. This does not apply
to damage caused by customer misuse.
```

Notice the shape repeated in all three sections: a **claim** sentence, followed by a **caveat** sentence that begins "This does not apply to..." — a real, common pattern in policy prose, contracts, and documentation generally. The caveat sentence is short, and it refers back to the claim with a bare pronoun ("This") rather than repeating the claim's subject. Read alone, "This does not apply to items marked final sale" does not say what "this" is — a refund, a shipping estimate, a warranty. The caveat is only interpretable together with its claim.

**Worked example — a fixed-size cut through the middle of every section.** `fixed_size_chunker(DOC_TEXT, size=16, overlap=0)` splits the document's raw word stream into consecutive 16-word windows, with no notion of a sentence or a heading. Run against the document above, it produces exactly this:

| Chunk | Text |
|---|---|
| `fixed-0` | `# Return Policy A refund is issued within five days after we receive the returned item.` |
| `fixed-1` | `This does not apply to items marked final sale. # Shipping Policy Standard shipping typically takes` |
| `fixed-2` | `a week from the order date. This does not apply to expedited orders paid at checkout.` |
| `fixed-3` | `# Warranty Policy The warranty covers defects in materials for one year. This does not apply` |
| `fixed-4` | `to damage caused by customer misuse.` |

Look at `fixed-0` and `fixed-1`. The 16-word window boundary happens to land *exactly* at the sentence break — `fixed-0` is the Return Policy's claim in full, `fixed-1` opens with the caveat in full. That is not a rare, contrived edge case; it is what happens whenever a fixed-size window's boundary falls anywhere inside a short section, which a window this size does to *every one* of the three sections here. The word "refund" appears exactly once in the whole document, inside `fixed-0`. `fixed-1` — the chunk that actually contains "final sale" — does not contain the word "refund" anywhere. A retrieval system that returns `fixed-1` alone for a question about final-sale refunds hands the reader a true, verbatim sentence from the source document that, read in isolation, does not say what it is even about.

**Worked example — the same document, chunked by structure.** `structural_chunker` splits on markdown headings instead of a fixed word count, so each section — heading, claim, and caveat — becomes one chunk:

```
struct-0: "# Return Policy\n\nA refund is issued within five days after we receive
           the returned item. This does not apply to items marked final sale."
struct-1: "# Shipping Policy\n\n..."
struct-2: "# Warranty Policy\n\n..."
```

Every claim now shares a chunk with its own caveat. `semantic_chunker` — which never looks at the markdown headings at all, and decides chunk boundaries purely from computed similarity between adjacent sentences — arrives at the *same three-way split*, by a completely different mechanism (Lab M03-A's Build step; see Misconception 3 below for the one place this alignment is not guaranteed to hold).

**Worked example — measured, not asserted: the module's central empirical claim.** Lab M03-A builds a `ChunkIndex` (Lesson 2) from each strategy's chunks and runs three gold queries against each — one per section, each phrased so its answer genuinely requires both the claim's subject and the caveat's exception. For each query, "completeness" means the single top-retrieved chunk contains *both* the required terms (for example, both "refund" and "final sale" for the Return Policy query — not just one or the other). This is the actual, hand-verified result of running Lab M03-A's harness against the document above:

| Strategy | Chunks produced | Completeness (3 gold queries) | Top-1 similarity, `q_refund_final_sale` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-size (16 words, no overlap) | 5 | **0 / 3** | 0.433 (`fixed-1`, missing "refund") |
| Structural (heading-based) | 3 | **3 / 3** | 0.535 (`struct-0`, complete) |
| Semantic (similarity + length floor) | 3 | **3 / 3** | 0.577 (`sem-0`, complete) |

Zero out of three, not "worse" — every single gold query in this run retrieves a fixed-size chunk missing one of its two required terms, because every fixed-size cut in this document lands inside a section. This is this module's version of AIE-302-M02 Lesson 4's discipline: not "fixed-size chunking is bad" as received wisdom, but a specific, reproducible number, computed by code the learner runs themselves in Lab M03-A, on a document small enough to also check the reasoning by hand.

**Misconception 1 — "Smaller chunks are always safer, because retrieval gets more precise."** `fixed-1` above is *nine words long* — about as small and "precise-sounding" as a chunk gets — and it is exactly the chunk that lost the word "refund." Chunk *size* is not the axis that determines whether information survives; chunk **boundary placement relative to sentence and discourse structure** is. A chunker can be small and still cut through the middle of a thought.

**Misconception 2 — "Overlap between chunks fixes fragmentation."** Overlap (`fixed_size_chunker(text, size, overlap=8)`, for instance) can rescue a claim/caveat pair that would otherwise straddle a single cut — *if* the overlap window happens to be wide enough to contain both sentences, which means tuning the overlap to this particular document's sentence lengths, not applying a fixed default. Overlap is a real, partial mitigation for fixed-size chunking's blindness to structure; it is not a fix for the blindness itself, and it does nothing at all for a section whose claim and caveat are, together, longer than `size + overlap`.

**Misconception 3 — "Semantic chunking, because it uses embeddings, is strictly better than structural chunking."** Lab M03-A's own numbers show a *tie* on this document, not a win for the fancier mechanism — and semantic chunking has a real, named failure mode structural chunking doesn't: a short, low-content sentence like "This does not apply to items marked final sale" carries almost no lexical signal of its own, so a *naive* similarity-to-previous-sentence rule can judge it dissimilar to its own claim and split them anyway — the same failure as fixed-size chunking, for a different mechanical reason. Lab M03-A's `semantic_chunker` only avoids this because it adds a **minimum-content-word floor**: a sentence too short to independently judge gets merged forward on length alone, not on a similarity score. When your source documents have real, trustworthy structure — consistent headings, a stable schema — structural chunking gets the same result for one embedding call fewer per chunking decision, at build time, forever. Semantic chunking earns its extra embedding calls on the kind of source that has meaning but no reliable structure: a raw transcript, a long unstructured email thread, a scraped web page with no consistent heading convention.

### Lesson 2 — Embedding your own chunks and indexing them with your own ANN index

An embedding turns text into a fixed-length vector such that texts a similarity function judges "close" are, by whatever the embedding model learned, related in meaning. This module treats "call an embedding model" as a thin, swappable interface — `EmbeddingClient.embed_batch(texts) -> np.ndarray`, one unit-normalized row per input text — for exactly the reason AIE-302-M02 treated `LLMClient` the same way: nothing in chunking, indexing, or reranking code should ever need to know or care which company computed a vector, only that vectors from the same client are comparable by dot product.

**Why unit-normalize.** For two unit vectors, `a @ b` (the dot product) *is* the cosine similarity — no separate division by norms needed at query time, because the norms are already 1. This is the identical fact AIE-110-M05's `normalize()` established for `brute_force_knn`; `ChunkIndex.add_chunk` and `search_chunks` below assume every vector handed to them is already unit-normalized, exactly as `NSWIndex` always has.

**A fake worth naming as a fake.** `FakeEmbeddingClient` (in `embedding_client.py`, shared by every lab) is a bag-of-words term-frequency embedding computed via **feature hashing**: each token hashes, deterministically, into one of a fixed `dim=4096` dimensions, and the vector is that dimension's word count, unit-normalized. It is not a claim that bag-of-words is a good embedding model — real embeddings (the `VoyageEmbeddingClient` adapter in the same file) capture synonymy, paraphrase, word order, and discourse reference that a word-count vector structurally cannot. The fake is small enough that a learner can compute its cosine similarities by hand for a two-sentence example, which is exactly why it is what every test and every lab's offline run is built on — its limitations are visible by design, not hidden behind a real model's apparent competence (this is also *why* Lesson 1's semantic chunker needed a length floor: the fake genuinely cannot judge a four-word sentence's relatedness to its neighbor from four words of vocabulary alone, and a real embedding model has exactly the same statistical problem, just a smaller one).

**Worked example — building `ChunkIndex` from `struct-0..struct-2` and searching it.** `ChunkIndex` (in `ann_index.py`) pairs the raw `NSWIndex` — ported forward from AIE-110-M05 with not one line changed — with the `Chunk` objects its integer node ids refer to:

```python
embedder = FakeEmbeddingClient()
index = ChunkIndex(dim=embedder.dim, M=8, ef_construction=50)
for rc in structural_chunker(DOC_TEXT):
    vec = embedder.embed_batch([rc.text])[0]
    index.add_chunk(Chunk(chunk_id=rc.chunk_id, text=rc.text, doc_id="policy-doc"), vec)

qvec = embedder.embed_batch(["Are items marked as final sale eligible for a refund?"])[0]
index.search_chunks(qvec, k=1, ef_search=50)
# -> [(Chunk(chunk_id='struct-0', text='# Return Policy\n\n...'), 0.535)]
```

This is the exact call that produced the 0.535 similarity in Lesson 1's table. Nothing about `NSWIndex.add` or `NSWIndex.search` changed between AIE-110-M05, where it only ever held synthetic random vectors, and here, where it holds real chunk embeddings for the first time — the class doesn't know the difference, and that is precisely the point of having built it once, correctly, at the substrate.

**Why this module still doesn't reach for a vector database.** AIE-110-M05 measured, at Python-loop scale, that a hand-built graph index can lose in wall-clock to a fully vectorized exact scan at every point on its own recall curve — interpreter overhead and memory-hierarchy effects dominate at small N, even though the graph index is doing asymptotically less work. This module's lab corpora (three to five chunks) are nowhere near large enough for `NSWIndex`'s approximate search to outperform, or even meaningfully differ from, exact brute-force search — at this scale, `ChunkIndex` is not winning a speed benchmark. The discipline here is not "use your index because it's fast yet" — it's "never touch a vector database before your own index has done real retrieval on real text," full stop. The speed argument is real, but it only shows up once the AIE-203 corpus is large enough to reach the crossover AIE-110-M05 measured — which is a fact about corpus size, not about which lab number this is.

**Misconception 1 — "Cosine similarity between an embedding and a query IS relevance."** It is a *proxy* for relevance — proportional to whatever surface and semantic content the embedding model encoded as similar — not an oracle of what a person asking the question would judge as the right answer. Lab M03-B's entire existence is proof this proxy is imperfect enough, even with a real embedding model, to need a second, separate stage.

**Misconception 2 — "A better embedding model fixes a bad chunking decision."** An embedding model embeds whatever chunk boundary chunking already decided. If chunking severed a claim from its caveat, no embedding model, however good, can encode information that was already thrown away before the embedding call ever ran. Embedding quality and chunking quality are independent variables; Lesson 1's failure mode is not fixable from Lesson 2's stage.

**Misconception 3 — "Since AIE-110 already proved a from-scratch ANN index can be worth it, using it here is automatically the fast choice."** Not at this module's lab scale — see above. The reason to use `ChunkIndex` on a five-chunk corpus is architectural discipline (never touch a vector database before your own index has retrieved real text), not a speed win you should expect to measure yet.

### Lesson 3 — Reranking as a separate, measured stage

Embedding-based retrieval (a **bi-encoder**: the query and every chunk are embedded *independently*, then compared by a fast vector operation) is cheap enough to run over an entire corpus, but it pays for that speed with a real blind spot — it can only compare a query's meaning to a chunk's meaning through the lossy bottleneck of two separately-computed vectors. A **reranker** looks at the query and one candidate chunk *together*, in the same forward pass or the same prompt, and can therefore make a judgment a bi-encoder's independent vectors structurally cannot: whether this specific passage, in the context of this specific question, is actually the direct answer — not just lexically or topically similar to it. That extra judgment is expensive per pair (in this module, one LLM call per candidate), which is exactly why reranking is architected as a *second* stage over a *small* shortlist, never a replacement for retrieval over the whole corpus: retrieve broad for recall, rerank narrow for precision.

**Worked example — retrieval fooled by lexical density, measured.** Lab M03-B extends the document with two "decoy" sections that are topically adjacent but not the right answer to a general question: a Gift Card Policy that repeats "final sale" and "refund" three times each, and an Extended Service Plan that repeats "warranty," "damage," and "misuse." For the query *"Are items marked as final sale eligible for a refund?"*, retrieval alone (`FakeEmbeddingClient`, top-2) ranks:

```
1. gift_card       similarity 0.621
2. return_policy   similarity 0.505
```

`gift_card` outranks the actual general-policy answer, `return_policy`, because it simply repeats the query's distinctive words more densely — a real, measurable failure of a bag-of-words-style bi-encoder, and a real (if usually smaller) risk for any bi-encoder, including a genuine learned embedding model, against a decoy that happens to be lexically or topically dense. Reranking each of the top-2 candidates against the query with an LLM-as-judge prompt (`build_rerank_prompt`, asking for a 0–10 relevance score) and re-sorting by that score produces:

```
return_policy: 9   gift_card: 4   ->   order: [return_policy, gift_card]
```

Reciprocal rank for the correct chunk goes from 0.5 (rank 2) to 1.0 (rank 1) for this query. Across all three of Lab M03-B's gold queries — including two where retrieval was *already* correct and reranking's job is to leave a good ranking alone — mean reciprocal rank measured at **0.833 before reranking, 1.0 after**. Reranking is not "always reorder everything"; it is "fix what needs fixing, confirm what doesn't."

**Misconception 1 — "Reranking always improves the ranking."** Two of Lab M03-B's three gold queries were already correctly ranked by retrieval alone, and reranking, correctly, left them unchanged (`warranty` stayed rank 1 over `service_plan`; `shipping_policy` stayed rank 1 over its only weak competitor). A reranker that reorders every input regardless of whether retrieval was already right is not doing its job better — it is adding latency and cost for no measured benefit, and, worse, it is one more chance to introduce a *new* mistake into a ranking that didn't have one.

**Misconception 2 — "More candidates retrieved before reranking is strictly better."** Reranking's cost scales *linearly* in the number of candidates — one model call per candidate, every time. Retrieving `k=50` candidates "to be safe" before reranking down to the top 3 is a real, measured cost multiplier for marginal benefit once retrieval's recall@k has already saturated (the true answer is either in your top 5 or it usually isn't in your top 50 either, for a corpus this small and this lexically distinct). The right `k` is a budget decision, in the same shape as AIE-302-M02 Lesson 3's bounded-retry budget — not a constant with one universally correct value.

**Misconception 3 — "Reranking can fix a bad retrieval or bad chunking decision."** A reranker can only re-order what retrieval already handed it. If Lesson 1's fixed-size chunking never put the true answer's chunk into the retrieved candidate set at all — because the answer was fragmented across chunks that individually score low — no reranker, however good, can promote a chunk that was never a candidate. Reranking is strictly downstream of chunking and retrieval; it cannot repair what those stages already lost.

### Lesson 4 — Citation as a hard requirement, checked programmatically

Instructing a model to "cite your sources" is the exact same category of claim AIE-302-M02 Lesson 1 named for JSON output: an instruction the decoding process does not enforce. A model told to end every sentence with `[chunk_id]` will usually comply — and it will also, sometimes, cite a chunk that sounds plausible but wasn't actually shown to it, cite the *right* chunk for the *wrong* sentence, or write a sentence with no marker at all. "The prompt asked for citations" is not evidence the answer is grounded, for precisely the reason "the prompt asked for ONLY JSON" was never evidence of valid JSON. Grounding, like schema conformance, has to be checked after generation, by code, against the actual retrieved text — not asserted by the presence of a citation-shaped string.

**The design: four possible outcomes per claim, not a single pass/fail.** Lab M03-C splits a generated answer into one claim per sentence, extracts each claim's trailing `[chunk_id]` marker if present, and classifies each claim into exactly one of:

- **`MISSING_CITATION`** — no marker at all.
- **`INVALID_CHUNK_ID`** — a marker naming a chunk that was never in the retrieved set shown to the model for this answer.
- **`UNSUPPORTED`** — a marker naming a real, retrieved chunk, but the claim's distinctive words don't sufficiently overlap that chunk's actual text (below `min_overlap`, default 0.5 — the fraction of the claim's non-stopword tokens also present in the cited chunk).
- **`SUPPORTED`** — a real, retrieved chunk, sufficiently overlapping.

Four categories, not two, because *why* a citation failed determines what to do about it: a missing marker is a formatting problem the repair prompt should just ask to fix; an invalid chunk id means the model referenced something that was never grounding material at all; an unsupported claim against a real chunk is the most serious case — the model wrote something that sounds cited but isn't actually backed by what it cited.

**Worked example — five answers, checked, not assumed.** Lab M03-C runs `check_citations` against five candidate answers over the five-chunk corpus from Lab M03-B:

| Answer | Citation present? | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `A1_fully_grounded` | yes, both sentences | **PASS** | Both claims overlap `return_policy`'s actual text (0.75 and 1.0 overlap) |
| `A2_missing_citation` | no marker at all | **FAIL** — `MISSING_CITATION` | Nothing to check the claim against |
| `A3_invalid_chunk_id` | `[expedited_addendum]` | **FAIL** — `INVALID_CHUNK_ID` | That chunk id was never retrieved for this answer |
| `A4_fabricated_but_cited` | `[warranty]`, a real chunk | **FAIL** — `UNSUPPORTED` (overlap 0.143) | The claim ("void if used underwater") isn't in the warranty text at all — a real citation attached to a fabricated claim |
| `A5_two_chunks_grounded` | yes, two different chunks | **PASS** | Each claim overlaps its own cited chunk fully |

`A4` is the case worth sitting with: the model cited a real chunk that is genuinely about the right topic (warranty), and still fabricated a specific claim the chunk never made. A citation checker that only verified "does `[chunk_id]` resolve to something real" would have passed this answer. Verifying *content overlap*, not just *reference validity*, is what catches it.

**The pipeline, not just the checker.** `generate_and_verify_answer` wraps generation, checking, and a single bounded repair retry into the same shape as AIE-302-M02's parse-validate-retry loop: generate an answer, check its citations, and if any claim fails, feed the *specific* failing claims and their statuses back into one repair prompt before giving up loudly with `UngroundedAnswerError`. An answer that fails its own citation check is never silently returned to a caller — it is either repaired once, with the exact reason stated, or it raises.

**Misconception 1 — "Telling the model to cite its sources means the system is grounded."** This is Misconception 1 from AIE-302-M02 Lesson 1, one level up the stack: an instruction is a lever on a probability, never a guarantee. `A4` above followed the citation instruction to the letter — a marker at the end of the sentence, naming a real chunk — and still said something the cited chunk never supported.

**Misconception 2 — "If the overlap check doesn't flag anything, the claim is true."** `support_overlap` proves *wording overlap* with the cited source, not *truth*. Consider a claim that swaps one word: "A refund is issued within **three** days after we receive the returned item. `[return_policy]`" — the source actually says *five*. Every other word matches, so `support_overlap` measures 0.875 — comfortably above the 0.5 threshold, marked `SUPPORTED`, and factually wrong. A lexical-overlap checker is a real, useful, cheap defense against fabrication and mis-citation — it is not, and cannot be, a defense against a single swapped fact wrapped in otherwise-correct language. Knowing exactly what a check does and does not prove is not a footnote; it is the check's entire safety case.

**Misconception 3 — "The `min_overlap` threshold is a detail, not a decision."** `min_overlap=0.5` was chosen for this module's lab corpus, not derived from any principle — a stricter threshold would have flagged more real claims as `UNSUPPORTED` (including some that were actually fine, just paraphrased), a looser one would have let more `A4`-shaped fabrications through as `SUPPORTED`. Every claim this checker will ever mark `SUPPORTED` depends on that one number, chosen once, by whoever wrote the code — which is exactly the subject of this module's Tier 3 reading, below.

## Labs

Every lab states its **Predict** before its **Build**, on paper, before any code runs. Two files are shared by all three labs — write them once, before Lab M03-A.

`ann_index.py` (provided; ported from AIE-110-M05 without modification, plus a new wrapper):

```python
"""
ann_index.py -- NSWIndex, ported forward UNCHANGED from AIE-110-M05 Lab
M05-B. This is not a reimplementation; it is the literal class the learner
built by hand in AIE-110, imported here as the substrate for RAG retrieval.
No vector database is touched in this module -- this class IS the vector
index, until M07 finally names one.

ChunkIndex is new in this module: a thin wrapper that pairs NSWIndex's
integer node ids with the chunk objects those ids refer to, so retrieval
code works in terms of Chunk objects, not bare vectors.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import heapq
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

import numpy as np


class NSWIndex:
    """A single-layer Navigable Small World graph -- the base layer HNSW
    stacks additional sparse layers on top of. Approximate cosine-similarity
    nearest-neighbor search via greedy best-first graph walk.

    M               = max neighbors kept per node (graph density / memory)
    ef_construction = beam width used while INSERTING (a build-time recall knob)
    """
    def __init__(self, dim, M=16, ef_construction=100):
        self.dim = dim
        self.M = M
        self.ef_construction = ef_construction
        self.vectors = []      # list of (d,) unit vectors; index = node id
        self.neighbors = []    # list of sets; adjacency list
        self.entry_point = None

    def _sim(self, node_id, x):
        return float(self.vectors[node_id] @ x)

    def _search_layer(self, q, entry, ef):
        visited = {entry}
        entry_sim = self._sim(entry, q)
        candidates = [(-entry_sim, entry)]     # min-heap on -sim -> closest pops first
        heapq.heapify(candidates)
        found = [(entry_sim, entry)]           # min-heap on sim -> found[0] is the worst kept
        heapq.heapify(found)

        while candidates:
            neg_c_sim, c = heapq.heappop(candidates)
            c_sim = -neg_c_sim
            worst_found = found[0][0]
            if c_sim < worst_found and len(found) >= ef:
                break   # nothing left in the queue can beat our current worst kept result
            for e in self.neighbors[c]:
                if e in visited:
                    continue
                visited.add(e)
                e_sim = self._sim(e, q)
                worst_found = found[0][0]
                if len(found) < ef or e_sim > worst_found:
                    heapq.heappush(candidates, (-e_sim, e))
                    heapq.heappush(found, (e_sim, e))
                    if len(found) > ef:
                        heapq.heappop(found)     # evict the current worst
        return sorted(found, key=lambda p: -p[0])

    def add(self, x):
        """Insert unit vector x. Returns its new node id."""
        i = len(self.vectors)
        self.vectors.append(x)
        self.neighbors.append(set())
        if i == 0:
            self.entry_point = i
            return i
        candidates = self._search_layer(x, self.entry_point, self.ef_construction)
        neighbor_ids = [node for _, node in candidates[:self.M]]
        for nb in neighbor_ids:
            self.neighbors[i].add(nb)
            self.neighbors[nb].add(i)
            if len(self.neighbors[nb]) > self.M:      # keep degree bounded
                nb_vec = self.vectors[nb]
                sims = [(float(self.vectors[j] @ nb_vec), j) for j in self.neighbors[nb]]
                sims.sort(key=lambda p: -p[0])
                self.neighbors[nb] = {j for _, j in sims[:self.M]}
        return i

    def search(self, q, k, ef_search=None):
        """Approximate top-k by cosine similarity. Returns (indices, sims)."""
        if not self.vectors:
            return np.array([], dtype=int), np.array([], dtype=np.float32)
        ef = ef_search if ef_search is not None else max(k, self.ef_construction)
        found = self._search_layer(q, self.entry_point, ef)[:k]
        idx = np.array([node for _, node in found], dtype=int)
        sims = np.array([sim for sim, node in found], dtype=np.float32)
        return idx, sims


@dataclass
class Chunk:
    """One retrievable unit: text plus the provenance metadata every chunk
    in this module is required to carry, so a citation can always be traced
    back to a source document -- see Lesson 4."""
    chunk_id: str
    text: str
    doc_id: str
    source: str = "unknown"


@dataclass
class ChunkIndex:
    """Pairs NSWIndex's integer node ids with the Chunk objects those ids
    refer to. AIE-110-M05 built NSWIndex to index bare vectors; this module
    is the first place those vectors mean something -- a chunk of a real
    document."""
    dim: int
    M: int = 16
    ef_construction: int = 100
    _index: NSWIndex = field(init=False)
    _chunks: list = field(init=False, default_factory=list)   # node id -> Chunk

    def __post_init__(self):
        self._index = NSWIndex(dim=self.dim, M=self.M, ef_construction=self.ef_construction)

    def add_chunk(self, chunk: Chunk, vector: np.ndarray) -> int:
        node_id = self._index.add(vector)
        assert node_id == len(self._chunks)
        self._chunks.append(chunk)
        return node_id

    def search_chunks(self, query_vector: np.ndarray, k: int, ef_search: int | None = None):
        """Returns a list of (Chunk, similarity) tuples, best first."""
        idx, sims = self._index.search(query_vector, k, ef_search=ef_search)
        return [(self._chunks[i], float(s)) for i, s in zip(idx, sims)]

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._chunks)
```

```python
"""
embedding_client.py -- the thin interface every embedding call in this
module goes through. Same durability discipline as AIE-204-M04's
llm_client.py and AIE-302-M02's llm_client.py: an EmbeddingClient Protocol,
a deterministic FakeEmbeddingClient used by every test and by the labs
offline, and a single real adapter that is the only place in the module
allowed to import a vendor SDK or name a model string.

The real adapter here is VoyageEmbeddingClient, not an Anthropic client --
Anthropic's own API does not serve embeddings; Voyage AI is the embedding
provider Anthropic's own docs point to. That split -- one vendor for
generation, a different vendor for embeddings -- is itself the point of
"swappable" durability: nothing in this module's chunking, indexing, or
reranking code cares which company computed the vectors, only that
EmbeddingClient.embed_batch returns unit-comparable rows.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import hashlib
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Protocol

import numpy as np


class EmbeddingClient(Protocol):
    """Anything with this one method can serve as an embedding backend."""

    def embed_batch(self, texts: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
        """Return an (n, d) float32 array, ROW-UNIT-NORMALIZED, one row per
        input text, same order as `texts`."""
        ...


# A small, generic English function-word list -- articles, prepositions,
# auxiliary verbs -- filtered out before counting, the same way a real
# retrieval pipeline's tokenizer strips function words before TF-IDF or
# BM25 scoring. This is NOT tuned to any particular lab's text; it is the
# ordinary stopword list any bag-of-words retrieval baseline uses.
STOPWORDS = frozenset("""
    a an and apply are at by do does for from in is it not of or the this
    to we within you
""".split())


def _tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    return [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower()) if w not in STOPWORDS]


def _hash_index(token: str, dim: int) -> int:
    """A deterministic (unlike Python's built-in hash(), which is salted
    per-process) index in [0, dim). This is feature hashing / 'the hashing
    trick' -- the same fixed-memory technique scikit-learn's
    HashingVectorizer uses -- so embed_batch never needs to remember a
    vocabulary or worry about a query using a word the corpus never saw
    before it was embedded."""
    digest = hashlib.sha256(token.encode("utf-8")).digest()
    return int.from_bytes(digest[:4], "big") % dim


@dataclass
class FakeEmbeddingClient:
    """Deterministic bag-of-words TF embedding, via feature hashing, used
    by every test in this module and by the labs when run offline. NOT a
    claim that bag-of-words IS a good embedding -- it is a small,
    fully-inspectable stand-in whose similarity behavior a learner can
    compute by hand: two texts that share more distinctive words score a
    higher cosine similarity; two texts that share none score exactly 0.0.
    Real embeddings (Lesson 2) capture meaning a shared-vocabulary count
    cannot -- synonyms, paraphrase, word order, discourse reference -- which
    is exactly what this fake does NOT do, on purpose, so its limitations
    are visible (Lesson 1's anaphora misconception) rather than hidden
    behind a real model's apparent competence.

    `dim` is fixed, not grown -- every call is independent and
    dimensionally consistent, at the cost of a small, real, and named risk:
    two different words can hash to the same dimension (a collision). At
    `dim=4096` and this module's small lab corpora (a few dozen distinct
    content words), collisions are vanishingly rare; at real corpus scale,
    collisions are the reason production systems either use a much larger
    `dim` or, better, a real learned embedding model instead of hashing.
    """
    dim: int = 4096

    def embed_batch(self, texts: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
        vecs = np.zeros((len(texts), self.dim), dtype=np.float32)
        for i, text in enumerate(texts):
            for w in _tokenize(text):
                vecs[i, _hash_index(w, self.dim)] += 1.0
        norms = np.linalg.norm(vecs, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        norms[norms == 0] = 1.0
        return vecs / norms


class VoyageEmbeddingClient:
    """The real backend. This is the ONLY file -- and this the ONLY class
    in it -- allowed to import a vendor embedding SDK or name a specific
    embedding model.
    """

    DEFAULT_MODEL = "voyage-3-lite"

    def __init__(self, model: str = DEFAULT_MODEL, api_key: str | None = None):
        import voyageai  # imported here, not at module scope, so labs that
        # never instantiate VoyageEmbeddingClient don't require the package installed
        self._client = voyageai.Client(api_key=api_key)
        self._model = model

    def embed_batch(self, texts: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
        result = self._client.embed(texts, model=self._model, input_type="document")
        X = np.array(result.embeddings, dtype=np.float32)
        norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        norms[norms == 0] = 1.0
        return X / norms
```

```python
from __future__ import annotations
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass

from embedding_client import EmbeddingClient, FakeEmbeddingClient

DOC_TEXT = """# Return Policy

A refund is issued within five days after we receive the returned item. This
does not apply to items marked final sale.

# Shipping Policy

Standard shipping typically takes a week from the order date. This does not
apply to expedited orders paid at checkout.

# Warranty Policy

The warranty covers defects in materials for one year. This does not apply
to damage caused by customer misuse.
"""


@dataclass
class RawChunk:
    chunk_id: str
    text: str


def fixed_size_chunker(text: str, size: int, overlap: int = 0) -> list[RawChunk]:
    words = text.split()
    step = size - overlap
    chunks = []
    i = 0
    idx = 0
    while i < len(words):
        piece = words[i:i + size]
        chunks.append(RawChunk(chunk_id=f"fixed-{idx}", text=" ".join(piece)))
        i += step
        idx += 1
    return chunks


def structural_chunker(text: str, max_words: int = 200) -> list[RawChunk]:
    sections = re.split(r"(?m)^(# .+)$", text)
    chunks = []
    idx = 0
    # sections alternates ['', '# Return Policy', 'body...', '# Shipping...', 'body...']
    i = 1
    while i < len(sections):
        heading = sections[i].strip()
        body = sections[i + 1].strip() if i + 1 < len(sections) else ""
        chunks.append(RawChunk(chunk_id=f"struct-{idx}", text=f"{heading}\n\n{body}"))
        idx += 1
        i += 2
    return chunks


def semantic_chunker(embedder: EmbeddingClient, text: str, similarity_threshold: float = 0.05,
                      min_content_words: int = 5, max_words: int = 60) -> list[RawChunk]:
    # A semantic chunker works off PROSE, not document structure -- it has
    # no notion of a markdown heading. Heading lines are dropped, not
    # glued onto the next sentence, so a word like "Policy" repeated in
    # every heading can't leak a spurious cross-section similarity signal
    # into a decision this chunker is supposed to make from meaning alone.
    body_only = "\n".join(line for line in text.splitlines() if not line.startswith("#"))
    sentences = re.findall(r"[^.]+\.", body_only.replace("\n", " "))
    sentences = [s.strip() for s in sentences if s.strip()]
    chunks = []
    idx = 0
    current = [sentences[0]]
    for s in sentences[1:]:
        joined = " ".join(current)
        content_words = len(_content_words(s))
        too_big = len((joined + " " + s).split()) > max_words
        if too_big:
            chunks.append(RawChunk(chunk_id=f"sem-{idx}", text=joined)); idx += 1
            current = [s]
            continue
        if content_words < min_content_words:
            current.append(s)
            continue
        pair = embedder.embed_batch([joined, s])
        sim = float(pair[0] @ pair[1])
        if sim >= similarity_threshold:
            current.append(s)
        else:
            chunks.append(RawChunk(chunk_id=f"sem-{idx}", text=joined)); idx += 1
            current = [s]
    chunks.append(RawChunk(chunk_id=f"sem-{idx}", text=" ".join(current)))
    return chunks


def _content_words(text):
    from embedding_client import _tokenize
    return _tokenize(text)

# single fixed-size chunk carries both halves of any answer -> 0/3.
# Structural chunking keeps each heading's body whole by construction, and
# semantic chunking's min-content-words floor keeps each short caveat glued
# to its claim -> both 3/3.

    "fixed": 0,
    "structural": 3,
    "semantic": 3,
```

```python
class LLMClient(Protocol):
    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str: ...


@dataclass
class FakeRerankClient:
    canned: dict = field(default_factory=dict)

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        matches = [(k, v) for k, v in self.canned.items() if k in prompt]
        if not matches:
            raise KeyError(f"FakeRerankClient: no canned score for this prompt. "
                            f"Prompt tail: {prompt[-200:]!r}")
        _, v = max(matches, key=lambda p: len(p[0]))
        return v


def build_index(embedder):
    idx = ChunkIndex(dim=embedder.dim, M=8, ef_construction=50)
    for chunk_id, text in CHUNKS.items():
        vec = embedder.embed_batch([text])[0]
        idx.add_chunk(Chunk(chunk_id=chunk_id, text=text, doc_id="policy-doc"), vec)
    return idx


def build_rerank_prompt(query_id: str, query_text: str, chunk: Chunk) -> str:
    return (f"MARKER: {query_id}:{chunk.chunk_id}\n"
             f"QUERY: {query_text}\n"
             f"CANDIDATE PASSAGE: {chunk.text}\n"
             "On a scale of 0 to 10, how directly and specifically does this passage answer "
             "the query? Respond with ONLY a single integer.")


def rerank_chunks(client: LLMClient, query_id: str, query_text: str,
                   candidates: list[tuple[Chunk, float]]) -> list[tuple[Chunk, float]]:
    scored = []
    for chunk, _retrieval_sim in candidates:
        raw = client.complete(build_rerank_prompt(query_id, query_text, chunk), temperature=0.0)
        match = re.search(r"\d+(\.\d+)?", raw)
        score = float(match.group()) if match else 0.0
        scored.append((chunk, score))
    scored.sort(key=lambda pair: -pair[1])
    return scored


def reciprocal_rank(ranked: list[tuple[Chunk, float]], correct_chunk_id: str) -> float:
    for i, (chunk, _score) in enumerate(ranked):
        if chunk.chunk_id == correct_chunk_id:
            return 1.0 / (i + 1)
    return 0.0
```

```python
"""Lab M03-C -- reference solution."""
from __future__ import annotations
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

from embedding_client import _tokenize
from ann_index import Chunk

CITATION_RE = re.compile(r"\[([a-zA-Z0-9_\-]+)\]\s*$")


@dataclass
class ClaimCheck:
    claim_text: str
    chunk_id: str | None
    status: str   # "SUPPORTED" | "UNSUPPORTED" | "MISSING_CITATION" | "INVALID_CHUNK_ID"
    overlap: float | None = None


@dataclass
class CitationReport:
    claims: list = field(default_factory=list)

    def all_supported(self) -> bool:
        return len(self.claims) > 0 and all(c.status == "SUPPORTED" for c in self.claims)


def split_claims(answer: str) -> list[str]:
    pieces = re.findall(r"[^.]+\.(?:\s*\[[a-zA-Z0-9_\-]+\])?", answer.strip())
    return [p.strip() for p in pieces if p.strip()]


def extract_citation(claim: str) -> tuple[str, str | None]:
    match = CITATION_RE.search(claim)
    if match is None:
        return claim.strip(), None
    chunk_id = match.group(1)
    claim_text = claim[: match.start()].strip()
    return claim_text, chunk_id


def support_overlap(claim_text: str, chunk_text: str) -> float:
    claim_words = set(_tokenize(claim_text))
    if not claim_words:
        return 0.0
    chunk_words = set(_tokenize(chunk_text))
    return len(claim_words & chunk_words) / len(claim_words)


def build_answer_prompt(query_id: str, query_text: str, retrieved_chunks: dict) -> str:
    context = "\n\n".join(f"[{cid}]: {c.text}" for cid, c in retrieved_chunks.items())
    return (
        f"MARKER: {query_id}\nQUESTION: {query_text}\n\nCONTEXT PASSAGES:\n{context}\n\n"
        "Answer using ONLY the context above. Every sentence of your answer MUST end with "
        "a citation marker naming the passage it came from, in the form [chunk_id]. Do not "
        "state anything the context passages do not support."
    )


def build_answer_repair_prompt(original_prompt: str, previous_answer: str,
                                report: CitationReport, query_id: str) -> str:
    problems = "\n".join(
        f'- "{c.claim_text}" -> {c.status}' for c in report.claims if c.status != "SUPPORTED"
    )
    return (
        original_prompt
        + f"\n\n[REPAIR REQUEST MARKER: {query_id}] Your previous answer was:\n"
        + previous_answer
        + "\n\nThe following claims failed citation checking:\n"
        + problems
        + "\n\nRewrite the answer so every claim is supported by a correctly-cited passage."
    )


class UngroundedAnswerError(Exception):
    def __init__(self, report: CitationReport):
        failed = [c for c in report.claims if c.status != "SUPPORTED"]
        super().__init__(f"{len(failed)} claim(s) failed citation checking after retry budget exhausted")
        self.report = report


def generate_and_verify_answer(client, query_id: str, query_text: str, retrieved_chunks: dict,
                                max_retries: int = 1, min_overlap: float = 0.5):
    prompt = build_answer_prompt(query_id, query_text, retrieved_chunks)
    answer = client.complete(prompt, temperature=0.0)
    for attempt in range(max_retries + 1):
        report = check_citations(answer, retrieved_chunks, min_overlap=min_overlap)
        if report.all_supported():
            return answer, report
        if attempt == max_retries:
            raise UngroundedAnswerError(report)
        answer = client.complete(
            build_answer_repair_prompt(prompt, answer, report, query_id), temperature=0.0
        )
    raise AssertionError("unreachable")  # satisfies type-checkers


# Recorded BEFORE running check_citations on any of the five answers below.
PREDICTED_PASS = {
    "A1_fully_grounded": True,     # every sentence cited, and the citation is the real source
    "A2_missing_citation": False,  # no [chunk_id] marker anywhere
    "A3_invalid_chunk_id": False,  # cites a chunk_id that was never retrieved
    "A4_fabricated_but_cited": False,  # real chunk_id, but claim content isn't in that chunk
    "A5_two_chunks_grounded": True,    # two claims, two different correct citations
}


def check_citations(answer: str, retrieved_chunks: dict, min_overlap: float = 0.5) -> CitationReport:
    report = CitationReport()
    for claim in split_claims(answer):
        claim_text, chunk_id = extract_citation(claim)
        if chunk_id is None:
            report.claims.append(ClaimCheck(claim_text, None, "MISSING_CITATION"))
            continue
        chunk = retrieved_chunks.get(chunk_id)
        if chunk is None:
            report.claims.append(ClaimCheck(claim_text, chunk_id, "INVALID_CHUNK_ID"))
            continue
        overlap = support_overlap(claim_text, chunk.text)
        status = "SUPPORTED" if overlap >= min_overlap else "UNSUPPORTED"
        report.claims.append(ClaimCheck(claim_text, chunk_id, status, overlap=overlap))
    return report
```

## Assessment

Maps to the course's **build labs** (40%) and **diagnosis clinics** (20%) components. Gaming-resistance is the design constraint: a learner cannot pass by copying working code, because every graded artifact requires a judgment trail a copied solution doesn't produce on its own.

The module exam hands the learner an **unseen four-section document**, an **unseen query**, and a **candidate generated answer with citation markers** in which exactly one claim's grounding is broken — sometimes by a chunking failure upstream (the true supporting fact was fragmented across two chunks, so no single retrieved chunk fully supports the claim), sometimes by a retrieval failure (a lexically denser but wrong section outranked the true one and reranking wasn't run, or was run and still didn't fix it), sometimes by a citation-checker failure the learner must recognize as a *checker* limitation, not a pipeline bug (a claim's wording overlaps its citation but the fact itself is subtly wrong — Lesson 4's swapped-number case). The learner must (a) **predict**, before running anything, which claim will fail `check_citations`, which status it will get, and — separately — which *stage* of the pipeline (chunking, retrieval, reranking, or the checker's own threshold) is the true root cause; (b) **locate** the failure by running the pipeline and reading `CitationReport`; (c) **fix** it at the stage the prediction named, not by patching the checker to let the specific case through; (d) **prove** the fix by re-running the full pipeline — chunk, embed, index, retrieve, (rerank), generate, check — end to end, and showing the claim now passes.

| Criterion | Full credit | Partial credit | No credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predict, before building (all three labs)** | Every prediction recorded before the corresponding Build step, with the *reasoning* stated (why a chunk boundary lands where it does, why a decoy out-ranks the truth) | Predictions recorded but reasoning restates the label ("I think it'll fail because it's wrong") | No prediction recorded, or recorded after Build/Verify (checked against submission order where available) |
| **Chunking correctness and honesty (Lab M03-A)** | All three chunkers pass the test suite, including the exact `0/3` vs `3/3` completeness split, and the learner's own writeup states the fixed-size failure as a measured number, not an assumption | Chunkers work but the writeup asserts "fixed-size is worse" without citing the learner's own run's numbers | A chunker that silently special-cases this module's exact document to pass the tests without implementing the general algorithm |
| **Reranking that preserves correct rankings (Lab M03-B)** | `rerank_chunks` fixes the lexically-fooled case AND leaves both already-correct cases unchanged, proven by both `test_rerank_fixes_the_fooled_case` and `test_rerank_does_not_break_already_correct_rankings` passing | Fixes the fooled case but the learner's own extension/variation of the lab breaks an already-correct ranking without them noticing | A reranker that reorders every input regardless of score, or that hardcodes an order matching only the lab's three known queries |
| **Citation checking that distinguishes failure kinds (Lab M03-C)** | `check_citations` correctly separates `MISSING_CITATION` / `INVALID_CHUNK_ID` / `UNSUPPORTED` / `SUPPORTED`, and `A4` (fabricated-but-cited) is provably caught, with the learner able to state in one sentence what `support_overlap` does and does not prove | Correctly flags `A4` but cannot articulate the swapped-number limitation (Lesson 4, Misconception 2) when asked directly | A checker that only verifies a marker is present and syntactically valid (equivalent to `json.loads` without `validate_schema` in AIE-302-M02's terms) |
| **Exam: unseen four-section document, one broken claim** | Correct predict/locate/fix/prove chain, with the fix applied at the stage the learner's own prediction correctly named as root cause | Correct locate and a working fix, but the fix is applied at the wrong stage (e.g., loosening `min_overlap` to pass a case that was actually a chunking fragmentation problem) | A fix that makes the exam's specific case pass without a stated, correct reason for where it belongs, or a checker threshold change presented as "the fix" for an upstream chunking or retrieval failure |

A program that runs is never sufficient evidence on its own. Every row above grades a decision, not an output.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime.

- **Wall — treating chunk size as a hyperparameter to grid-search rather than a boundary-placement decision.** A learner who reaches straight for `for size in [8, 16, 32, 64]: ...` before ever looking at where a boundary lands relative to a sentence has skipped the actual lesson. **Reroute:** before any code, have them mark by hand, on the printed `DOC_TEXT`, exactly where a chosen `size` would cut — the same exercise Lab M03-A's Predict step requires — and only then let them sweep sizes in code.
- **Wall — the feature-hashing embedding reads as "not a real embedding" and gets dismissed rather than reasoned about.** **Reroute:** have them compute, by hand, the cosine similarity of two 5-word sentences under `FakeEmbeddingClient`'s tokenizer (list the shared distinctive words, count them, normalize) before trusting any of the module's printed numbers. A learner who can reproduce 0.535 by hand stops treating the fake as a black box.
- **Signal — a learner surprised that fixed-size chunking scores `0/3`, not just "a bit worse."** This is the lesson landing, not a failure of judgment — the whole point of Lesson 1's worked example is that fragmentation is total on a document this short, not partial. Log `struggle-productive`, never `blocked`.
- **Wall — Lab M03-B's lexical-density result gets over-generalized to "retrieval is broken, always rerank."** **Reroute:** point back at `q2`/`q3` in the *same* lab, where reranking correctly changed nothing — the lesson is "retrieve broad, rerank narrow, and reranking should preserve what retrieval already got right," not "distrust retrieval unconditionally."
- **Wall — misuse of `min_overlap` in Lab M03-C, typically cranking it to `1.0` "to be safe."** **Reroute:** before letting them change the number, have them run `check_citations` against `A1` and `A5` (the genuinely grounded answers) at `min_overlap=1.0` and read what breaks — a real answer that used slightly different wording than the source now fails too. - **Accelerate — a learner who correctly predicts, before running Lab M03-C, that `A4` will fail as specifically `UNSUPPORTED` (not just "seems wrong"), citing the overlap ratio as the reason.** This learner has understood what the checker actually measures, not just that it catches bad cases. - **Struggle-blocked, not productive — a learner who, after both Lesson 4's worked example and the module exam's swapped-number case, still cannot explain why a `SUPPORTED` citation isn't proof of a true claim.** This is the module's one truly load-bearing concept, directly inherited from AIE-302-M02's "parsing succeeding isn't proof of validity" — if it hasn't landed after two concrete demonstrations, the issue usually isn't citation-checking specifically, it's that M02's Lesson 2 distinction never fully took hold. **Reroute:** step back from this module's labs and revisit AIE-302-M02 Lesson 2 before returning here.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — chunking, retrieval, and citation as editorial acts, not neutral plumbing — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** A RAG system cannot answer from anything it cannot retrieve, and chunking decides retrievability at the sentence level. This is not a matter of taste: Lab M03-A measured it directly, on one document, with one gold set — fixed-size chunking scored `0/3` completeness, structural and semantic chunking each scored `3/3`, on the exact same underlying text. The chunking decision, made once, before any embedding call runs, bounded what the system could ever say about that document.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Grounded in your data" is said as though the corpus were a passive, neutral thing a system simply reads from. It never is. Someone chose the chunk size and the chunking strategy (Lab M03-A). Someone chose which documents entered the corpus at all, and which were left out — that choice was made once already, in AIE-203, long before this module ever ran a query against it. Someone chose the citation threshold that decides `SUPPORTED` versus `UNSUPPORTED` (Lab M03-C's `min_overlap = 0.5` is a number a person picked, not a fact about language). Every one of those choices silently decides which true things the system *can* say and which true things it never had a chance to retrieve in the first place — and "the system is grounded in the data" describes none of that history. Retrieval is not a neutral window onto a corpus; it is the end product of a series of editorial decisions, each defensible on its own, whose combined effect nobody downstream of the system ever sees.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You picked `min_overlap = 0.5` in Lab M03-C the way you'd pick any default — it worked on the module's five test cases, so you moved on. Every claim your system will ever mark `SUPPORTED` depends on that one number, chosen once, by you, for reasons that had nothing to do with the specific claim it will someday be asked to judge. Somewhere downstream, someone will trust a citation your threshold approved that isn't actually right — not because the checker is broken, but because it is doing exactly what you told it to do. What number would you have had to set it to before you'd trust your own system with a decision that mattered to *you* — and knowing what that stricter number would have rejected among this module's own five test answers, would you still have shipped `0.5`?

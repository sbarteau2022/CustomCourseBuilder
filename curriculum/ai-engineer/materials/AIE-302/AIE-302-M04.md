**Course:** AIE-302 — Building with Foundation Models: Context, RAG, and Agents (specialization tier, durability swappable)
**Module:** M04 of 7. **Hours:** 16
**Sits in the descent:** immediately after M03, where the learner built a RAG system from primitives — chunk, embed, retrieve, rerank — over their own AIE-110 hand-built index. M03 ended with a system that *runs*. This module exists because "it runs" and "it works" are different claims, and the gap between them is exactly where a RAG system's real failures hide. M04 is the first module in the course where the learner is graded on *measuring* a system they already know how to build, not on building more of it. It sits immediately before M05 (agents), which inherits this module's central discipline unchanged: don't trust a run because it produced an answer — trace which stage of the pipeline is actually responsible for the answer you got.
**Prerequisites:** AIE-302-M03 (RAG from primitives — this module evaluates that system, using the same corpus shape). **Feeds forward:** AIE-302-M05 (agents) reuses this module's oracle-vs-actual diagnostic technique — swap "oracle context" for "oracle tool result" and the same PASS / upstream-failure / downstream-failure diagnosis applies to an agent's tool-call loop. > **Toolchain for this module.** Python 3.12+, `pytest`. No vector database, no `ragas`, no `trulens`, no `deepeval` — every metric in this module (precision@k, recall@k, MRR, the faithfulness/completeness judge) is built from the standard library plus one real LLM call path, so that when M07 finally names an eval framework, the learner already knows in their hands exactly what number it's computing and exactly what it costs to compute by hand. ## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Two failure conditions wearing one symptom

**Teaching text**

A RAG system that answers a user's question wrong produces exactly one visible symptom: a bad answer. Nothing about that symptom tells you, on its own, which of two entirely separate stages produced it. **Retrieval** decides what the model is allowed to see — it either surfaces the chunk that contains the true answer, or it doesn't. **Generation** decides what the model does with what it was shown — it either uses the given material faithfully and completely, or it doesn't. These are different mechanisms, built by different code, and they fail in different ways for different reasons. Collapsing them into one verdict — "the RAG system gave a bad answer, so the LLM hallucinated" — is the single most common misdiagnosis in production RAG work, and it is wrong far more often than it is right: most bad RAG answers are the *generation* step doing exactly what it should with material the *retrieval* step never should have handed it in the first place — or, less often but just as real, the retrieval step handing over exactly the right material and the generation step still getting it wrong. Only a measurement that looks at the two stages independently can tell you which one actually happened.

This module builds that measurement as two genuinely separate eval instances sharing one corpus — the same five-commitment anatomy from AIE-204-M01 (task definition, gold set, metric, decision rule, statistic), instantiated twice. **Retrieval's eval** (Lessons 2–3) asks: given a query, did the system surface the chunks a correct answer would need? Its gold set is a set of hand-labeled *(query, chunk)* relevance judgments; its metrics are precision@k, recall@k, and MRR. **Generation's eval** (Lesson 4) asks a completely different question: *given the chunks the system was actually shown* — good, bad, or a mix — did the model produce a faithful, complete answer from them? Its instrument is an LLM judge, scored against the specific context the model actually saw. The two evals never share a metric, and that's deliberate — the moment a single number tries to capture both, it becomes impossible to tell which stage a bad score is blaming.

**Worked example — one corpus, three queries, three different verdicts**

This module's running corpus is a 12-chunk excerpt of an HR policy handbook (`corpus.py`, Lab M04-B). Three queries against it, run through the same retrieval pipeline (top-k = 2) and the same generation step, produce three structurally different outcomes:

| Query | What retrieval (top-2) returned | What generation produced | What actually went wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| *"How many vacation days do employees get per year?"* | `C01` (correct: "15 days/year") + `C03` (a trap chunk about *when* to request vacation, not *how many* days) | "Employees accrue 15 days of paid vacation per year." | **Nothing.** Retrieval pulled in a trap alongside the right chunk, but generation correctly grounded in `C01` and ignored `C03` — the visible answer is completely correct despite imperfect retrieval. |
| *"What is the company's parental leave policy?"* | `C09` (timing rule) + `C08` (primary caregiver: 12 weeks) — **missing** `C10` (secondary caregiver: 4 weeks) | "The primary caregiver receives 12 weeks of paid parental leave. Leave must be taken within 12 months of the child's birth or adoption." | **Retrieval.** Everything the answer says is true and grounded — the secondary-caregiver fact simply never reached the model, because the chunk containing it was never retrieved. |
| *"How many weeks of parental leave does the primary caregiver get?"* | `C08` (correct: "12 weeks") | "The primary caregiver receives 4 weeks of paid parental leave." | **Generation.** The one chunk retrieval needed to hand over is exactly the one it handed over, and it says 12 weeks — the model substituted the secondary-caregiver's number anyway. |

Rows two and three look identical from the outside — a user reading either answer sees an incomplete or wrong statement about parental leave — but they have opposite root causes, and they demand opposite fixes: row two needs a corpus/chunking fix (retrieve `C10`, or don't split the policy across three chunks that a single query can plausibly need all of); row three needs a generation/prompting fix (nothing about adding more chunks would have stopped a model that already had the right chunk from misstating it).

**Misconception 1 — "If the final answer is wrong, the LLM hallucinated."** Row two refutes this directly: the model said nothing false. Every clause in its answer is supported by the context it was given. It was never asked to talk about the secondary caregiver's leave and it didn't invent anything about it — it simply wasn't handed the fact. Calling this "hallucination" blames the generation step for a starvation that happened one step earlier, and a team that "fixes" it by re-prompting the model to "be more thorough" will watch the score not move, because thoroughness was never generation's problem to begin with.

**Misconception 2 — "If the final answer is right, retrieval must have worked well."** Row one refutes this: retrieval's precision at k=2 for that query is only 0.5 — half of what it surfaced was a trap. The visible answer being correct is not evidence retrieval was clean; it's evidence generation was robust enough to filter noise this time. A team that only ever checks final-answer correctness will never see retrieval's precision problem building up until a future query's trap chunk is subtle enough, or the generation prompt is different enough, that generation *doesn't* filter it out.

**Misconception 3 — "Perfect retrieval guarantees a correct answer."** Row three refutes this: the exact right chunk, and only the exact right chunk, was in context, and the model still got it wrong. Retrieval is not a proof obligation that generation automatically discharges — it hands the model raw material, and what the model does with raw material is a second, independently-failing step. This is the reason this module builds two measurements instead of one: a single end-to-end pass/fail would have called all three rows "the answer was wrong or right" and told you nothing about why.

### Lesson 2 — Building the retrieval gold set the AIE-204 way

**Teaching text**

AIE-204-M02 built a gold set for a classification task: one ticket, one urgency label, drawn from a criteria doc that separated "genuinely urgent" from "sounds urgent." Retrieval's gold set is the same three ingredients — task definition, a criteria doc, and reconciled labeled examples — applied to a harder shape: a classification gold set labels *one item*; a retrieval gold set has to label *every plausible (query, chunk) pair*, because "is this chunk relevant to this query" is a judgment that has to be made once per query, not once per chunk. At real corpus scale this is intractable to do exhaustively (a 10,000-chunk corpus times 200 eval queries is two million judgments), so real IR practice uses **pooling**: you only hand-judge the chunks that some retriever plausibly surfaced for that query — the true relevant chunks, whatever a reasonable retriever ranked highly regardless of correctness, and a small sample of clear negatives — not every chunk in the corpus. This module's Lab M04-A uses the same pooling discipline at toy scale: a 4-chunk candidate pool per query, not all 12.

**The criteria doc is the part that actually does the work, and it has to be written before any labeling starts** — writing it after, to match labels you already assigned by gut feel, produces a document that describes your instincts instead of constraining them, which defeats the entire purpose of having one. For retrieval, the line that matters most is between two categories that *feel* similar and are not: **relevant** — a correct, complete answer to the query could be composed using only this chunk's text (combined with other relevant chunks, if the query needs more than one fact) — versus **topically adjacent** — the chunk discusses the same policy area as the query but, read closely, does not contain the specific fact the query asks for. Topically adjacent is not a softer version of relevant. It is the category a similarity-based retriever is *most* likely to confuse with relevant, because vocabulary overlap is exactly what similarity scoring measures, and vocabulary overlap is exactly what topically-adjacent chunks have plenty of.

**Worked example — why `C03` is a trap and not a near-miss**

Query: *"How many vacation days do employees get per year?"* Candidate pool: `C01` ("Employees accrue 15 days of paid vacation per year..."), `C02` ("Unused vacation days roll over... up to a maximum of 5 days"), `C03` ("Vacation requests must be submitted through the HR portal at least two weeks before the requested dates"). Applying the criteria doc: `C01` is relevant — it alone answers the question. `C02` is topically adjacent — it's about vacation days, but it answers "what happens to *unused* days," not "how many do I get." `C03` is topically adjacent for the same reason, one level more aggressively — it's about vacation, and about a number ("two weeks"), and it shares almost every content word a naive reader's eye would latch onto, but it answers a scheduling-process question, not a quantity question. A learner labeling by "does this sound related" gets all three chunks flagged relevant. A learner applying the criteria doc's "would a correct, complete answer come from just this chunk's text" test correctly excludes `C02` and `C03` — and, as Lesson 3 will show, a cosine-similarity retriever ranks `C03` almost as high as `C01` (0.9701 vs. 0.9806), which is exactly why the human labeling discipline has to be this precise: the retriever cannot tell these two chunks apart from vocabulary alone, so the gold set is the only place in the whole system where that distinction gets drawn correctly.

**Misconception 1 — "A chunk that mentions the query's topic is relevant."** Refuted directly above: `C03` mentions vacation, shares a number, and is not relevant. Topic overlap is necessary for a chunk to plausibly be relevant; it is nowhere near sufficient, and treating it as sufficient produces a gold set that can't distinguish a real retrieval failure (missing the actual answer) from a real retrieval success (correctly excluding a topically-similar non-answer).

**Misconception 2 — "One labeler's judgment is the gold set."** A single labeler's first pass on an ambiguous pool item is a hypothesis, not a fact — Lab M04-A hands you a second labeler's independent judgments on the same pool and requires you to reconcile every disagreement in writing, in terms of the criteria doc, not in terms of who labeled first. A gold set with no reconciliation step is a gold set that never found out where its own criteria doc was ambiguous.

**Misconception 3 — "Every retrieval eval needs graded (not binary) relevance."** Graded relevance (0/1/2/3, "somewhat relevant" vs. "fully relevant") is real and sometimes necessary, particularly for open-ended queries where "how good an answer this chunk supports" is itself a spectrum. This module deliberately uses binary relevance instead, because for this corpus's query shape — "does this chunk contain the specific fact this query asks for" — the line is close enough to a clean yes/no that binary labeling doesn't lose meaningful signal, and binary relevance is what precision@k, recall@k, and MRR (Lesson 3) are defined over in their standard form. Know that this is a simplification made on purpose for a task shape that tolerates it, not a universal claim that graded relevance is never needed.

### Lesson 3 — Retrieval metrics: precision@k, recall@k, and MRR

**Teaching text**

Once a query has a reconciled relevant set, three numbers answer three different questions about what the retriever actually returned:

- **Precision@k** — *of the k chunks retrieved, what fraction are actually relevant?* `precision@k = |retrieved_top_k ∩ relevant| / k`. This is a *purity* measure: it penalizes noise in what generation is about to be handed, regardless of whether the true answer was found at all.
- **Recall@k** — *of all the relevant chunks that exist, what fraction did the top-k retrieval surface?* `recall@k = |retrieved_top_k ∩ relevant| / |relevant|`. This is a *coverage* measure: it penalizes missing material, regardless of how much irrelevant material came along for the ride.
- **MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank)** — averaged over queries, `1 / rank of the first relevant chunk`, or `0` if none appears at all. This measures *how fast* a correct chunk showed up, and — this is the property that makes Misconception 2 below possible — it is blind to everything that happens after that first hit.

**Worked example — the same corpus, three k values, one query that changes everything**

Using this module's toy 7-axis embeddings (`corpus.py`) and cosine similarity, the query *"How many vacation days do employees get per year?"* (`Q1`, relevant = `{C01}`) ranks the corpus:

```
cos(Q1, C01) = 1.0·1.0 / (1.0 · 1.0198) = 0.9806
cos(Q1, C03) = 1.0·0.4 / (1.0 · 0.4123) = 0.9701
cos(Q1, C02) = 1.0·0.3 / (1.0 · 1.0440) = 0.2873
```

Ranked: `[C01, C03, C02, ...]`. At k=1: precision = 1.0, recall = 1.0 — perfect. At k=2: precision = 0.5 (the trap `C03` diluted it), recall = 1.0 (the one relevant chunk that exists was found). At k=3: precision = 0.333, recall = 1.0. Recall never drops below 1.0 for this query because there is only one relevant chunk and it's always ranked first — but precision keeps falling as k grows, because every additional slot beyond the single true answer can only ever add noise.

Now contrast with `Q3`, *"What is the company's parental leave policy?"* (relevant = `{C08, C09, C10}` — three chunks, because the question is broad enough that a complete answer needs all three). Ranked: `[C09, C08, C10, ...]`. At k=1: precision = 1.0, recall = 0.333 (found one of three). At k=2: precision = 1.0 (both retrieved chunks are relevant), recall = 0.667 (two of three — `C10`, the secondary-caregiver fact, is still missing). At k=3: precision = 1.0, recall = 1.0.

Averaged across this module's full 6-query gold set at k=2: **mean precision@2 = 0.583, mean recall@2 = 0.944.** Averaged MRR across all six queries: **1.0** — every query's *first* retrieved chunk is relevant, without exception.

**Misconception 1 — "Recall@k = 1.0 means retrieval is perfect."** `Q3` hits recall = 1.0 — at k=3. The system this module's Lab M04-C actually runs generation against uses k=2, where `Q3`'s recall is 0.667. Reporting recall at a k your production system doesn't use is reporting a number about a system that isn't the one in front of users. Recall is only as meaningful as the k it's measured at, and that k has to match the pipeline's real operating point.

**Misconception 2 — "MRR alone tells you if retrieval is healthy."** This module's own numbers are the refutation: MRR is a perfect 1.0 across all six queries, while mean precision@2 is only 0.583 and `Q3`'s recall@2 is 0.667. MRR only looks at the rank of the *first* relevant hit — it has no way to notice a trap chunk sitting at rank 2, and no way to notice that a query needing three relevant chunks only got two. A retrieval system can post a perfect MRR and still be leaking noise into generation's context window on more than half its queries. Report precision and recall alongside MRR, always — never MRR alone.

**Misconception 3 — "Larger k is strictly better, since it can't hurt recall."** True, mechanically: recall is monotonically non-decreasing in k (every chunk you add to the top-k can only help or do nothing, never remove a true positive already found). But precision falls as k grows — this module's own average precision drops from 0.583 at k=2 to 0.444 at k=3 — and every extra chunk in context costs real tokens, real latency, real money, and (M01's context-engineering lesson) real risk of the model losing track of what matters in a longer prompt. "Larger k can't hurt recall" is true and also not the whole cost function; k is a real design tradeoff, not a dial to max out.

### Lesson 4 — Isolating generation quality with oracle context

**Teaching text**

Lessons 2–3 measured retrieval entirely on its own terms — no model was called, no answer was generated, nothing about generation quality entered the numbers. This lesson measures generation the same way: entirely on its own terms, with retrieval's imperfections removed from the picture. The technique is **oracle-context evaluation**: instead of feeding generation whatever the retrieval pipeline actually surfaced, feed it the *gold-labeled relevant chunks directly* — bypass retrieval entirely, as if it had performed perfectly. Score the resulting answer with an LLM judge (AIE-204-M04's pattern) on two axes:

- **Faithfulness** — is every claim in the answer directly supported by the given context? (Not: is it true in the world. Supported by *this* context.)
- **Completeness** — given what the context actually contains, does the answer cover everything in it relevant to the question? (Not: does it cover everything anyone might want to know. Everything the *context* actually has.)

Then run the *exact same* generation step and the *exact same* judge on the context the real pipeline actually retrieved. The prompt template, the model, and the judge are held constant across both runs — the only thing that varies is which chunks were in context. Any score difference between the two conditions is therefore attributable to *retrieval's choice of context*, not to anything about generation itself, because generation's own behavior was never given a chance to differ between the two runs beyond what the input handed it.

**The diagnosis table this produces:**

| Oracle-context score | Pipeline-context score | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | **PASS** — the system works end to end for this query. |
| High | Low | **RETRIEVAL_FAILURE** — generation handles the true material well; the pipeline just never handed it over. |
| Low | Low | **GENERATION_FAILURE** — even handed the true material directly, generation still got it wrong; more/better retrieval would not have fixed this. |
| Low | High | **Investigate the harness** — this shouldn't happen if oracle context is a superset of what generation needs; treat it as a bug in the eval, not a real diagnosis. |

**Worked example — the same three queries from Lesson 1, now with judge scores**

| Query | Condition | Context given | Faithfulness | Completeness | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *vacation days/year* | oracle | `{C01}` | 5 | 5 | |
| | pipeline (k=2) | `{C01, C03}` | 5 | 5 | **PASS** |
| *parental leave policy* | oracle | `{C08, C09, C10}` | 5 | 5 | |
| | pipeline (k=2) | `{C08, C09}` | 5 | 2 | **RETRIEVAL_FAILURE** |
| *primary caregiver weeks* | oracle | `{C08}` | 1 | 5 | |
| | pipeline (k=2) | `{C08, C09}` | 1 | 5 | **GENERATION_FAILURE** |

The middle row is the one to read carefully: pipeline faithfulness stays at 5 even though completeness collapses to 2. That split is the signature of a retrieval-caused failure — the model didn't say anything wrong, it just wasn't given enough to say everything right. The bottom row shows the opposite signature: faithfulness collapses (1) in *both* conditions, including oracle, where the single correct chunk was handed over directly — no amount of additional retrieved context would have fixed an answer that contradicts the one chunk it was given.

**Misconception 1 — "Completeness should be judged against everything true about the topic."** If the completeness judge is instructed to check the answer against everything a human expert knows about parental leave, it will penalize the pipeline-context answer in the middle row for the *same missing fact* that recall@2 (Lesson 3) already penalized retrieval for — the same failure gets counted twice, once in each half of the eval, and the two measurements this module exists to keep separate collapse back into one. Completeness must be judged against what's **in the given context**, full stop — a completeness score of 5 given only `{C08, C09}` is not a claim that the answer is complete in the world; it's a claim that generation used everything it was handed.

**Misconception 2 — "If retrieval's metrics already look bad, you don't need to also run the oracle-context generation eval."** The vacation-days row refutes this: precision@2 for that query is only 0.5 (a trap chunk got through), and yet the end-to-end system passes cleanly, because generation correctly ignored the noise. A team that stops at retrieval metrics and assumes "precision isn't perfect, so the system must be failing users" would spend engineering effort tightening a retriever that was never the problem. You need evidence from both eval instances, in both directions, before concluding which stage — if either — needs fixing.

**Misconception 3 — "One low-scoring example proves which stage is broken system-wide."** Each row in the table above is a diagnosis for *one query*. A system-level verdict requires running this same two-condition comparison across the whole gold set and looking at the distribution of diagnoses — this module's Lab M04-C does exactly that across all six queries, and the Assessment's diagnosis clinic is graded on doing it correctly for an unseen case, not on getting lucky with one example that happens to point the right way.

def cosine_similarity(a: tuple[float, ...], b: tuple[float, ...]) -> float:
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    norm_a = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    norm_b = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in b))

```python
"""Lab M04-B -- reference solution."""
from __future__ import annotations

from corpus import CORPUS, CORPUS_EMBEDDINGS, QUERIES, QUERY_EMBEDDINGS, cosine_similarity


def retrieve(query_id: str, k: int) -> list[str]:
    query_vec = QUERY_EMBEDDINGS[query_id]
    scored = [
        (chunk_id, cosine_similarity(query_vec, CORPUS_EMBEDDINGS[chunk_id]))
        for chunk_id in CORPUS
    ]
    scored.sort(key=lambda pair: (-pair[1], pair[0]))
    return [chunk_id for chunk_id, _ in scored[:k]]


def precision_at_k(ranked_ids: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    if k <= 0:
        raise ValueError("k must be positive")
    top_k = ranked_ids[:k]
    hits = sum(1 for cid in top_k if cid in relevant)
    return hits / k


def recall_at_k(ranked_ids: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    if not relevant:
        raise ValueError("relevant set must be non-empty")
    top_k = ranked_ids[:k]
    hits = sum(1 for cid in top_k if cid in relevant)
    return hits / len(relevant)


def reciprocal_rank(ranked_ids: list[str], relevant: set[str]) -> float:
    for rank, cid in enumerate(ranked_ids, start=1):
        if cid in relevant:
            return 1.0 / rank
    return 0.0


def mean_reciprocal_rank(rankings: dict[str, list[str]], relevant_by_query: dict[str, set[str]]) -> float:
    rrs = [
        reciprocal_rank(rankings[query_id], relevant_by_query[query_id])
        for query_id in relevant_by_query
    ]
    return sum(rrs) / len(rrs)


def full_report(k: int) -> dict[str, dict]:
    from gold_relevance import RELEVANT

    report = {}
    for query_id in QUERIES:
        ranked = retrieve(query_id, k)
        report[query_id] = {
            "ranked": ranked,
            "precision": precision_at_k(ranked, RELEVANT[query_id], k),
            "recall": recall_at_k(ranked, RELEVANT[query_id], k),
        }
    return report
```

"""

module are built on. Same shape as AIE-204-M04's judge client: one

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Protocol


class LLMClient(Protocol):
    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        ...


@dataclass
class FakeGenerationClient:
    """Deterministic stand-in used by every test in this lab and by the
    lab itself when run offline. Maps an exact substring of the prompt
    to a canned response; when more than one canned key matches, the
    LONGEST (most specific) key wins -- a repair prompt is built by
    appending to the original prompt, so it still contains the
    original's marker as a substring."""
    canned: dict[str, str] = field(default_factory=dict)

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        matches = [(key, resp) for key, resp in self.canned.items() if key in prompt]
        if not matches:
            raise KeyError(
                "FakeGenerationClient: no canned response matches this "
                "prompt. Check that build_generation_prompt / "
                "build_judge_prompt embed the exact marker this lab's "
                "canned dict expects."
            )
        _, response = max(matches, key=lambda pair: len(pair[0]))
        return response


class AnthropicClient:
    """The real backend. The only place in this module allowed to
    import a vendor SDK or name a model string."""

    DEFAULT_MODEL = "claude-haiku-4-5"

    def __init__(self, model: str = DEFAULT_MODEL, api_key: str | None = None):
        import anthropic
        self._client = anthropic.Anthropic(api_key=api_key)
        self._model = model

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        response = self._client.messages.create(
            model=self._model,
            max_tokens=max_tokens,
            temperature=temperature,
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
        )
        return response.content[0].text

{answer_text}

Respond with ONLY a JSON object, no other text:
{{"faithfulness": <1-5>, "completeness": <1-5>, "reasoning": "<one sentence>"}}"""


def parse_judge_response(raw: str) -> dict:
    text = raw.strip()
    if text.startswith("```"):
        text = re.sub(r"^```(?:json)?\s*", "", text)
        text = re.sub(r"\s*```$", "", text)
    try:
        data = json.loads(text)
    except json.JSONDecodeError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f"judge response was not valid JSON: {raw!r}") from exc
    required = {"faithfulness", "completeness", "reasoning"}
    if not required.issubset(data.keys()):
        raise ValueError(f"judge response missing required keys: {data!r}")
    for key in ("faithfulness", "completeness"):
        if not isinstance(data[key], int) or not (1 <= data[key] <= 5):
            raise ValueError(f"{key} must be an int 1-5, got {data[key]!r}")
    return data


def judge_generation(client: LLMClient, item_id: str, context_text: str, question_text: str,
                      answer_text: str, max_retries: int = 1) -> dict:
    prompt = build_judge_prompt(item_id, context_text, question_text, answer_text)
    raw = client.complete(prompt, temperature=0.0)
    try:
        return parse_judge_response(raw)
    except ValueError:
        if max_retries <= 0:
            raise
        repair_prompt = (
            prompt
            + "\n\n[REPAIR REQUEST] Your previous response was not "
              "valid JSON. Respond with ONLY the JSON object described "
              "above -- no other text."
        )
        raw2 = client.complete(repair_prompt, temperature=0.0)
        return parse_judge_response(raw2)


def _is_high(scores: dict) -> bool:
    return scores["faithfulness"] >= 4 and scores["completeness"] >= 4


def diagnose(client: LLMClient, query_id: str, k: int) -> dict:
    oracle_ids = sorted(RELEVANT[query_id])
    oracle_answer = generate_answer(client, query_id, oracle_ids)
    oracle_context_text = "\n".join(f"[{c}] {CORPUS[c]}" for c in oracle_ids)
    oracle_scores = judge_generation(
        client, f"{query_id}-oracle", oracle_context_text, QUERIES[query_id], oracle_answer
    )

    pipeline_ids = retrieve(query_id, k)
    pipeline_answer = generate_answer(client, query_id, pipeline_ids)
    pipeline_context_text = "\n".join(f"[{c}] {CORPUS[c]}" for c in pipeline_ids)
    pipeline_scores = judge_generation(
        client, f"{query_id}-pipeline", pipeline_context_text, QUERIES[query_id], pipeline_answer
    )

    if _is_high(pipeline_scores):
        diagnosis = "PASS"
    elif _is_high(oracle_scores):
        diagnosis = "RETRIEVAL_FAILURE"
    else:
        diagnosis = "GENERATION_FAILURE"

    return {
        "diagnosis": diagnosis,
        "oracle": oracle_scores,
        "pipeline": pipeline_scores,
        "oracle_context": oracle_ids,
        "pipeline_context": pipeline_ids,
    }

## Assessment — the diagnosis clinic

Gaming-resistant by design: the artifact that gets graded is the judgment trail — which numbers you computed and how they justify your verdict — never the verdict alone. "I think it's a retrieval problem" earns zero credit with no numbers behind it, even if the guess happens to be right.

**The clinic.** You are handed an unseen RAG case: a small corpus, a query, the system's actual top-k retrieval result, and the system's actual generated answer — the same shape as this module's own corpus and pipeline, built the same way, with exactly one class of failure planted in it (a missing chunk, a chunking split that separates a fact from the query that needs it, or a generation step that mishandles context it was correctly given). You are not told which class it is. You must:

1. **Build the relevance judgments** for the case's query against its corpus, using the Lesson 2 criteria doc discipline (state your reasoning, not just your labels).
2. **Compute precision@k, recall@k, and reciprocal rank** for the case's actual retrieval result against your judgments, at the k the case's pipeline actually used.
3. **Run the oracle-vs-pipeline generation comparison** (Lesson 4's technique, Lab M04-C's harness) and report both conditions' faithfulness and completeness scores.
4. **Diagnose** the case as `PASS`, `RETRIEVAL_FAILURE`, `GENERATION_FAILURE`, or (if both oracle and pipeline scores are low and the retrieval metrics are also poor) `COMPOUND_FAILURE` — and write the one or two sentences that tie your diagnosis to the *specific numbers* that prove it, not to a restated description of the symptom.
5. **Propose the fix** — and state explicitly which layer it targets (corpus/chunking, retrieval ranking, or the generation prompt/model) — and predict, before checking against the case's known root cause, whether your proposed layer is the right one.

### Rubric

| Criterion | Full credit | Partial credit | No credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval evidence** | Reports precision@k, recall@k, and reciprocal rank, correctly computed from the case's own gold judgments at the pipeline's real k. | Numbers present but incomplete (e.g., precision only, no recall) or computed at the wrong k. | Asserts retrieval quality ("it looks fine" / "it looks bad") with no computed number behind it. |
| **Generation evidence** | Runs and reports both oracle- and pipeline-context judge scores; correctly reads the faithfulness/completeness split (Lesson 4's signature: faithful-but-incomplete vs. unfaithful-in-both). | Scores reported but the split is misread, or completeness is scored against outside knowledge instead of the given context (Lesson 4 Misconception 1). | Judges only the pipeline answer; the oracle-context condition is skipped entirely. |
| **Diagnosis** | Correct stage identified, and the written justification names the specific number(s) from steps 2–3 that rule out the other stage. | Correct stage named, but the justification restates the scores without explaining why they rule out the alternative diagnosis. | Stage guessed, or asserted from the symptom alone ("the answer was wrong so it's retrieval/generation") with no numeric tie-back. |
| **Fix specificity** | Proposed fix targets the layer the diagnosis actually implicates and states the mechanism by which it would resolve this specific failure. | Fix targets roughly the right layer but is generic ("add more chunks," "improve the prompt") without connecting to this case's specific gap. | Fix targets the wrong layer, or is "use a better model" / "fix the RAG system" with no stated mechanism. |

A submission that gets the diagnosis right by chance, with no numbers and no mechanism in the fix, scores at or near zero on three of the four rows — the label alone was never the deliverable.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — measuring a system that is only as honest as the evidence you were willing to separate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Retrieval quality is empirically bounded by the corpus and the chunking, not by anything downstream. This module's own `Q3` case is the proof, not an opinion: identical generation step, identical judge, only the retrieved context varied between two runs, and completeness dropped from 5 to 2 purely because one chunk never arrived. You cannot prompt your way past a fact your retriever never surfaced.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** The booster pitch — "add RAG and your model is grounded" — suppresses that grounding is only as complete and as honest as what the corpus contains and how someone decided to split it into retrievable units. `C08`, `C09`, and `C10` exist as three separate chunks because *someone* chose to split the parental-leave policy that way; that choice, invisible in the system's confident final answer, is the actual reason `Q3` fails. Retrieval is not a neutral lookup — it's an editorial act, performed once at chunking time by a person who is not in the room when the system answers a user months later. The critic's counter-claim — "RAG doesn't really fix hallucination" — overcorrects in the opposite direction: this module's own `Q1` case shows generation *can* be faithful and complete even against a noisy, imperfect retrieval, and `Q4` shows generation can be unfaithful even against a perfect one. Neither "RAG solves grounding" nor "RAG doesn't fix hallucination" survives contact with a system where the two stages are actually measured apart — the honest claim is narrower, and more useful, than either slogan.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your RAG system will, someday, answer a real user confidently and completely wrong. Your diagnosis matrix will tell you cleanly whether the truth was in your corpus and your system failed to find it, or the truth was handed to the model directly and it said something else anyway. Ask yourself which failure is more forgivable to explain to the person who acted on that wrong answer — and then ask whether the accountability chain you'd actually have to walk them through changes at all based on which one it turns out to be.

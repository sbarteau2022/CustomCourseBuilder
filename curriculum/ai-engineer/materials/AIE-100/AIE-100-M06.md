### Lesson 1 — Authorship is not accountability

**The teaching.** Two different questions get answered by the same sentence, and the confusion between them is where this module lives. **Authorship** asks: *whose activity produced this text?* — your fingers, a colleague's, a model's weights. **Accountability** asks: *when this code does something in production, who is answerable for it?* — who gets the page at 3 a.m., who stands up in the postmortem, who signs the change that ships. These are independent. A model can be the *author* of a function while you are, without qualification, its *accountable party*. The whole rhetorical move of "the AI wrote it" works by smuggling an authorship fact ("I didn't type this") into an accountability claim ("so it's not on me"). The smuggle fails the instant you look at it directly: **you chose to prompt for it, you chose to accept it, you chose to ship it. Each of those is your decision, and the decision is the thing you own — not the typing.**

This is not a new principle invented for AI. It is exactly how accountability has always worked for tools. A structural engineer who runs a load simulation owns the bridge; "the software computed the beam size" is not a defense when it falls. A surgeon who uses a robotic arm owns the incision. The tool's competence does not migrate the responsibility — it changes *what the responsible person must verify*, and nothing else. An AI coding assistant is the same category of thing: a capable tool whose output you are answerable for. What is genuinely new is only that the tool is fluent enough to make the smuggle *feel* plausible — the code looks like a peer wrote it, so it feels like a peer should own it. No peer owns it. You do, from the moment you type "merge."

The precise formulation to carry out of this lesson: **accountability attaches to the last human who could have said no.** The model cannot say no on your behalf — it has no stake, no standing in your incident review, no name on the deploy. Every point at which a human could have rejected the artifact and instead accepted it is a point where accountability was accepted with it. Signing is just the visible, final one.

**Worked example — the postmortem line that changes nothing.**

A payment service double-charges a subset of customers for six hours. The root cause is a retry function that re-submits a charge when the response times out, without an idempotency key — so a slow-but-successful charge gets charged twice. The postmortem draft contains this line:

> *"The retry logic was generated by the coding assistant from the prompt 'add retries to the charge call'; the idempotency gap was not flagged."*

Now ask the accountability question, not the authorship one. The prompt "add retries to the charge call" is an *underspecified wish* (every ambiguity M03 warned about: it never said "idempotent," never partitioned the timeout-but-succeeded case). The model filled the gap with the cheapest plausible reading — a bare resubmit — exactly as M03 predicted a genie would. The engineer read it, it looked like retries, they merged it. Walk the chain of "who could have said no":

- The person who wrote the prompt could have specified idempotency. They didn't. (Their decision.)
- The person who reviewed the diff could have traced the timeout partition — M04's instrument, M05's discipline — and seen the missing key. They didn't. (Their decision.)
- The person who approved the deploy could have blocked on "where's the idempotency test?" They didn't. (Their decision.)

The model appears in the *authorship* of the code and in *none* of those decisions. Rewrite the postmortem line the way accountability actually reads:

> *"We shipped a charge-retry path with no idempotency key. The gap was present in the generated draft and was not caught in specification, review, or deploy approval — three points at which we owned the decision and accepted it."*

The second version is not harsher for its own sake. It is simply *true* in a way the first is not: it names decisions by the people who made them, and it does not offer the tool as a party that can hold responsibility, because it cannot. **The model's authorship is a real fact and an irrelevant one to the question of who owns the outage.**

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"I didn't write it, so it's not really mine."** Authorship is not the source of ownership; *acceptance* is. You own it the moment you merge it, exactly as you own a library you `pip install`ed and never read — the not-reading is a decision, and it is yours. The fluency of the author (human colleague, open-source maintainer, or model) is orthogonal to your accountability for shipping.
- **"'The AI generated it' is a mitigating fact in a review."** It is a *provenance* fact — useful for deciding what to re-verify (Lesson 2) — and it is *zero* mitigation for the outcome. In an incident, "generated by the assistant" answers *how the defect entered*; it never answers *why we shipped it*, which is the only question accountability asks. Treat any impulse to offer it as mitigation as a signal that authorship and accountability have collapsed together in your head.
- **"Shared tooling means shared blame — the vendor owns some of this."** The vendor owns the tool's general behavior; you own your use of it, the same way a spreadsheet vendor does not own your financial model's errors. Terms of service for coding assistants are explicit about this, and they are only restating the ordinary law of tools. The responsibility does not split; it sits with the person who shipped.
- **"If I have to own it completely anyway, provenance is pointless paperwork."** Backwards. Because you own it completely, you need to *know what you're owning* — which parts were generated (and therefore carry the M03 gap risk), under what license (Lesson 3), verified by whom. Provenance does not dilute your ownership; it is the instrument that lets you exercise it competently instead of blindly. Ownership without provenance is signing a document you have not read.

### Lesson 2 — Provenance: the traceable origin of an artifact

**The teaching.** **Provenance** is the recorded, traceable answer to *"where did this artifact come from?"* — who or what produced it, from what input, when, and who has since verified it. In the art world, provenance is the chain of custody that separates a genuine work from a forgery; in a supply chain, it is the record that lets you trace a contaminated part back to its batch. In software, provenance is the record that lets you answer, for any line you ship: *did a human write this, did a model generate it, was it generated-then-edited — and has anyone actually checked it?*

The crucial move of this lesson is to see what provenance **does and does not change.** It does **not** change accountability — Lesson 1 settled that; you own it regardless. What it changes is the **verification you owe**. Code you wrote by hand, you already understand (you built the model of it as you typed). Code a model generated, you understand *only as far as you have read and traced it* — and the provenance record is what stops you from lying to yourself about which is which three weeks later, when the function looks familiar and you cannot remember whether "familiar" means "I verified this" or "I skimmed this and it looked fine." A provenance record makes the M02 distinction — *legible versus merely present* — durable and auditable.

A minimally honest provenance record for a unit of code carries at least:

- **What it is** — the unit's name / identity.
- **Author of record** — `human:<name>` or `ai:<model>`; and the *origin* — `written`, `generated`, or `generated-then-edited`. (The middle-edited case is the dangerous one: it *feels* owned because you touched it, but the untouched parts still carry the generated gap.)
- **License** — the license the code is offered under, or `UNKNOWN`. `UNKNOWN` is not a blank to fill in later; it is a *blocker* (Lesson 3).
- **Verified by** — the human who read, traced, and tested it. `None` means *no human has accepted this outcome yet.* This field, not the author field, is the one accountability reads.

Notice the asymmetry the record encodes: the *author* can be a model; the *verifier* and the *accountable party* cannot. A model can generate; it cannot verify on your behalf, because verification is the act of a party who will be answerable, and the model is not one. So in the record, `author` may be `ai:...`, but `verified_by` is always a human name or it is `None` — and `None` means *not shippable*, because no one has yet accepted the outcome.

**Worked example — two identical functions, two different obligations.**

Here are two functions. They are character-for-character identical:

```python
def dedupe(items):
    seen = set()
    out = []
    for x in items:
        if x not in seen:
            seen.add(x)
            out.append(x)
    return out
```

Function A: *you* wrote it, by hand, thinking through first-appearance order as you went (the M03 `dedupe` contract). Function B: a model *generated* it from the prompt "remove duplicates, keep order." The **code** is the same, so a reviewer reading only the code owes the same read to both. But the **provenance** differs, and the difference is real: for A, you hold a mental model built during authorship — you already know it preserves first-appearance order because you *chose* that. For B, you hold nothing until you read it; "keep order" was your prompt, but the model's compliance is a claim you have not checked. The provenance record forces the honest question B demands and A does not: *have I verified the order property, or am I trusting that the prompt was obeyed?* Same code, different obligation, and only the provenance record remembers which function is which after the moment of creation has faded. Without the record, both become "that dedupe function that looks fine," and the unverified one ships on the reputation of the verified one.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"If the code is identical, the provenance doesn't matter."** The code being identical is exactly *why* provenance matters — it is the only thing that distinguishes "I understand this because I built it" from "I have not read this yet." Identical artifacts can carry non-identical *evidence*, and provenance is where that difference is recorded. Lose the record and you lose the ability to tell verified code from unverified code that looks the same.
- **"Provenance is about giving the AI credit / attribution."** It is not an authorship-credit system; it is an *accountability-support* system. The point is not to honor the model as author — it is to let a human answer "what do I still owe this artifact, and who has accepted it?" Attribution to a model is at most a byproduct; the load-bearing field is `verified_by`, which is always human.
- **"`generated-then-edited` means I've verified it."** Editing is not verifying. You may have changed the variable names and the return type while the actual defect sat untouched three lines down. The `origin` field records that you *touched* it; the `verified_by` field records that a human *checked* it. They are different facts, and conflating "I edited it so I own it" with "I verified it so it's correct" is how edited-but-unread code ships.
- **"We can reconstruct provenance later if we need it."** You cannot. The moment of creation is the only time the origin is known for free; afterward it decays into "I think I wrote that?" Provenance is cheap to record at creation and effectively impossible to reconstruct honestly under incident pressure, which is precisely when you will want it. Record it when it is free.

### Lesson 3 — Licensing and training data: the code may not be free

**The teaching.** Two separate ownership questions hide inside "can I use this code," and this lesson keeps them apart. The first is **licensing**: the code you pull in — including packages an assistant suggests, and occasionally snippets it reproduces — comes under a *license*, and licenses impose obligations that can be incompatible with how you intend to ship. The second is the **training-data question**: the model that generated "your" code learned from a corpus of human-written code under many licenses, and it can, in rare cases, reproduce a recognizable chunk of licensed code without telling you where it came from — which means the generated artifact can carry an obligation you never saw and cannot see from the code alone.

Take licensing first, because it is *decidable* — and decidability is the same tool you built in M03. The permissive licenses (MIT, BSD, ISC, Apache-2.0) let you combine and redistribute with light obligations (keep the notice). The **copyleft** licenses (the GPL/AGPL family, "strong"; MPL and LGPL, "weak") impose a reciprocal obligation: distribute a work that incorporates strong-copyleft code and *the combined work must be offered under that same copyleft license*. This is the **contamination** case: pull one GPL-3.0 dependency into a project you intend to ship under MIT, distribute the result, and you have a contradiction — the GPL demands the whole be GPL, your MIT license claims it is not. You cannot ship it as MIT. Not "it's risky"; it is a decidable violation of the terms, checkable by a table your team commits to in advance. And an `UNKNOWN` license is not neutral — you cannot clear an obligation you have not identified, so unknown is a *blocker*, not a blank.

The honest caveat, stated with teeth rather than hidden: **real license compatibility is genuinely complex** — linking type, distribution versus SaaS, dual licensing, and patent clauses all matter, and edge cases need counsel. That complexity is not a reason to leave it to the model. It is a reason to do exactly what M03 taught: encode a *simplified, decidable team policy* — a table that says, for the licenses you actually encounter, `clear` or `blocked` with no case left to opinion — and *own that policy*, escalating the genuine edge cases to a human who can. The map is not the territory; a decidable policy you own beats an unexamined guess the model made for you, every time.

The training-data question is harder and does not resolve into a keyboard check, which is why it belongs in the ethics thread and not only in a table. Verifiable ground: these models were trained on large corpora of publicly available code, much of it under licenses (including copyleft) whose terms the training and the output arguably implicate; models can and occasionally do emit near-verbatim reproductions of training examples; and whether generated output carries the license of code it was trained on is, as of this course's authoring, **unsettled** — contested in active litigation and not answered by any tool you can run. What you *can* do is bounded and real: treat a suspiciously complete, idiomatic block (a whole known algorithm, a recognizable license header, a distinctive comment) as a **provenance flag** — search for it, and if it traces to a licensed source, treat it as that-licensed code, not as free output. What you cannot do is pretend the question is settled because the code compiled.

**Worked example — a compatibility table with teeth.**

Your project ships under **MIT**. An assistant, asked to "add a fast fuzzy-matching dependency," suggests four packages with these licenses: `MIT`, `Apache-2.0`, `GPL-3.0-only`, and `RapidCorp-Eval-1.0` (a name your policy has never seen). Run the decidable policy:

| Dependency license | Category | Under an MIT project you intend to distribute | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| `MIT` | permissive | **clear** | permissive-into-permissive, keep the notice |
| `Apache-2.0` | permissive | **clear** | permissive; retain NOTICE file |
| `GPL-3.0-only` | strong-copyleft | **BLOCKED** | copyleft forces the combined work to GPL; cannot ship as MIT |
| `RapidCorp-Eval-1.0` | unknown | **BLOCKED** | unidentified obligation; cannot clear what you cannot name |

Two of the four "helpful suggestions" cannot ship. Neither is visible from the *code* — both packages may be excellent and their code may be clean. The violation lives entirely in the license metadata, which the model had no obligation to weigh and, in suggesting the packages, did not. The `GPL-3.0-only` block is the M03 lesson in a new domain: a decidable rule, committed in advance, that a plausible-looking suggestion fails on a property the happy path (does it work? yes) never checks.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"The assistant generated it, so it's my original code, license-free."** Generation is not authorship-from-nothing; the model learned from licensed code and can reproduce recognizable pieces of it. "The model made it" is a *provenance* claim, and an unverified one — it does not establish that the output is unencumbered. Idiomatic, complete blocks deserve a provenance search, not a presumption of originality.
- **"If it's on the internet / on GitHub, it's free to use."** Public visibility is not a license grant. The overwhelming majority of public repositories carry a license (often copyleft), and code with *no* license is *more* restricted, not less — absent a grant, default copyright reserves the rights to the author. "I could see it" and "I may ship it" are different facts.
- **"Copyleft is a fringe concern; permissive licenses are everywhere."** Copyleft dependencies are common enough that a single transitively-pulled GPL package is a routine way to contaminate a permissive project — and the contamination is invisible until someone runs the check. The decidable policy exists precisely because the one blocking dependency hides among dozens of clear ones.
- **"License compatibility is too complex for me to judge, so I'll trust the tool."** The complexity is real and it is an argument for a *committed simplified policy plus escalation*, not for delegation to a system with no stake in your distribution. The tool suggesting a package is not the tool clearing it; the clearing is yours, decidable for the common cases and escalated for the genuine edges — exactly the M03 total-function-vs-precondition choice, made consciously.

### Lesson 4 — Signing your name: the attestation as the act of ownership

**The teaching.** Everything converges here. To **attest** is to make a formal assertion you are answerable for — to sign. When you merge generated code, approve a PR, or tag a release, you are attesting, whether or not you think of it that way: you are asserting *this is fit to ship, and I am the party answerable for it.* This module's final claim is that **the attestation is honest exactly when it is backed by the judgment trail, and a lie exactly when it is not.** The signature does not create the ownership — Lesson 1 established that ownership attaches at acceptance regardless. What the signature does is make the ownership *visible and dated*: it is the moment the last human who could have said no, said yes, in writing.

This is why the whole course was built the way it was. The judgment trail — the decidable spec (M03), the read and trace (M02, M04), the verification that could have failed (M04), the debugged and understood defect (M05), the cleared provenance and license (M06) — is not busywork that precedes the signature. It *is* what the signature asserts is true. Sign without the trail and you have asserted something you did not check: the plausible-looking artifact, accepted under deadline, that this entire course exists to stop you from rubber-stamping. The attestation with no trail behind it is the purest form of outsourcing judgment — you have put your name on the model's guess and called it yours.

So the honest attestation gate has a shape, and it is the shape of this module's lab: **you may sign only when every unit is verified by a human and every license is cleared.** Any unverified unit, any unknown license, is a *blocker* — not a warning you may override with a shrug, but a thing that stands between you and an honest signature. You may of course choose to sign over a blocker; the point of making it a blocker is that you then own *that choice too*, explicitly, by name — which is precisely the accountability Lesson 1 said you cannot escape. The gate does not remove your freedom to ship risk. It removes your ability to ship risk *without noticing you did*.

**Worked example — what the signature actually asserts.**

A release manifest bundles three units for a deploy under an MIT project:

| Unit | Author | Origin | License | Verified by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `parse_config` | `human:sbarteau` | written | MIT | `human:sbarteau` |
| `retry_charge` | `ai:assistant` | generated | MIT | `None` |
| `format_report` | `ai:assistant` | generated-then-edited | MIT | `human:sbarteau` |

You are asked to sign the release. Read what your signature would assert, unit by unit. `parse_config`: written and verified by you — the assertion is honest. `format_report`: generated, then edited, and — crucially — `verified_by` is a human name, so someone read and checked it; the assertion is honest *if that verification was real* (the `origin` being `generated-then-edited` is a flag to confirm the untouched parts were read, not just the edits). `retry_charge`: generated, `verified_by` is `None`. **No human has accepted this outcome.** To sign the release as-is is to assert `retry_charge` is fit to ship on the basis of *nothing* — the model's authorship is not verification, and there is no verifier. The honest gate blocks the signature here and names the blocker: *`retry_charge`: unverified.* You do not fix this by editing the manifest to say `verified_by: sbarteau`; you fix it by *actually verifying it* — reading, tracing, testing it (M02–M05) — and *then* the field becomes true and the signature becomes honest. The manifest is not the work; it is the record that the work was done. Writing your name in the field without doing the reading is forging the provenance you are supposed to be keeping.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Signing is a formality — the real work is the code."** The signing is the moment ownership becomes visible and dated; it is the opposite of a formality. Treating it as a rubber stamp is exactly the failure mode — the whole apparatus of provenance and verification exists to make the signature *mean* something, and a formality-signature means nothing while carrying the full accountability of a real one.
- **"Marking it `verified` is what makes it shippable."** The *field* does not make it shippable; the *act of verification* does, and the field merely records that the act happened. Setting `verified_by` on a unit you did not read is not passing the gate — it is forging the record, which is worse than a missing record because it launders unverified code as verified. The gate is only as honest as the verifications behind its fields.
- **"If the gate is green, I've done my job."** The gate proves the *mechanism* — every field is populated, every license is in the clear column. It cannot prove your verifications were real, because "did this human actually read and trace this" is not a fact the machine holds. Green means "the record is complete and consistent"; it does not mean "the signature is honest." That last gap is yours and does not close.
- **"Owning it is a burden the tooling should reduce."** The tooling reduces the *cost of exercising* ownership — it makes provenance cheap to record and license violations cheap to catch — but it cannot and must not reduce the ownership itself, because a tool that let you own less would be a tool that let you know less about what you shipped. The goal was never to carry less responsibility. It was to carry it *competently*: with a spec, a read, a verification, a cleared license, and a signature you can defend.

```python
    author: str
    origin: str
```

```python
# solution: attest.py
from license_policy import check


class AttestationError(Exception):
    pass


def sign_off(signer, records, project_license):
    blockers = []
    for r in records:
        if r.verified_by is None:
            blockers.append(f"{r.name}: unverified — no human has accepted this outcome")
    for lic, reason in check(project_license, [r.license for r in records]):
        blockers.append(f"license {lic}: {reason}")
    if blockers:
        raise AttestationError(
            "cannot sign — " + str(len(blockers)) + " blocker(s):\n  " + "\n  ".join(blockers))
    return {
        "signer": signer,
        "project_license": project_license,
        "units": [r.name for r in records],
        "attestation": "every unit human-verified and license-cleared; signer owns the result",
    }
```

## Assessment

The assessment is **gaming-resistant by construction**: the passing bar is the *ownership you can defend and the provenance you cleared*, not a green pytest run. A learner cannot pass by getting the gate to go green, because the graded artifacts are (a) provenance and license records with an ownership judgment attached, (b) a signed — or refused — attestation defended against an adversarial "the model wrote it," and (c) the honesty of the verifications behind the signature, which no test can certify. In this module a green gate is explicitly declared *insufficient*: it proves the mechanism fired, never that the signer was entitled to sign.

### Component 1 — Provenance manifest with cleared licenses (30%)

For a supplied batch of mixed-origin units (some `written`, some `generated`, some `generated-then-edited`; mixed licenses including one copyleft and one unknown), produce the provenance manifest and run the license policy. Graded on: is every field populated *honestly* (is `verified_by` set only where a real read/trace/test is described in the accompanying judgment trail, not blanket-filled); are the copyleft and unknown licenses **flagged as blockers** with the correct distinct reasons; and is the `generated-then-edited` unit treated as *unverified-until-read* rather than owned-because-touched. **A learner who fills every `verified_by` field to make the gate green, with no per-unit judgment trail, scores below a learner who leaves two fields `None`, names them as blockers, and states what verification each still owes** — because the certified outcome is *honest accounting of what is and is not verified*, not a complete-looking record.

### Component 2 — The attestation defense (55%)

You are handed a release manifest and an **incident scenario**: a unit in the manifest caused a production failure, and a teammate's draft postmortem contains the line *"generated by the assistant; the gap was not flagged."* Time-boxed, in this order, and **the order is graded**:

1. **Decide (before defending):** for each unit, state signed / blocked and why; identify the unverified units and uncleared licenses as blockers.
2. **Rewrite the postmortem line** so it names the *decisions* (specify / review / approve) and the humans who made them, and removes the model as a party that can hold responsibility — without softening the accountability.
3. **Defend the signature (or the refusal):** answer, in writing, *"the model wrote it — why is this on you?"* by reference to the last-human-who-could-have-said-no, the provenance record, and the judgment trail behind (or missing from) `verified_by`.
4. **State the honest fix** for each blocker — verification for unverified units, escalation or removal for uncleared licenses — and explain why editing the record without doing the work is forging provenance.

Submitting a "correct" signed manifest *without* the step-2 rewrite and the step-3 defense caps this component at **40%** — because a green gate presented as proof of ownership is precisely the rubber-stamp this module exists to stop: it demonstrates the mechanism, not the judgment the signature asserts.

### Component 3 — Ownership viva (15%)

A 5-minute oral/whiteboard: handed a one-line "the AI generated this, so..." claim, complete it *honestly* out loud — separating the authorship fact (true, irrelevant to accountability) from the accountability claim (false) — and answer "at the moment this ships and fails, whose contract was it, and on what evidence did you sign?" This certifies the authorship-vs-accountability separation directly; you cannot bluff a distinction you are drawing live.

## Three-tier reading — the signature, and what it is entitled to assert

**Tier 1 — Material ground (verifiable facts).** These are checkable, not matters of taste. *Authorship and accountability are distinct fields of the same record* — provable at the keyboard: a `Provenance` with `author="ai:assistant"` and `verified_by="human:sbarteau"` returns `"human:sbarteau"` from `accountable_party()`, and the identical code with `verified_by=None` returns `None` — the author never leaks into the accountable party. *License incompatibility is decidable for the common cases* — a `GPL-3.0-only` dependency in an `MIT` project you intend to distribute is a policy violation your `check()` returns mechanically, and an `UNKNOWN` license is a blocker because an unidentified obligation cannot be cleared; both are computations, not opinions. *A dependency's license is invisible from its code* — the `GPL-3.0-only` block lives entirely in metadata, and no amount of reading the package's source reveals it. *An attestation gate is only a mechanism* — `sign_off` returning a signature proves every field is populated and every license is in the clear column, and proves *nothing* about whether the human actually read the code behind a `verified_by`; that verification is an act the machine cannot observe or enforce. And the training-data facts are ground too: these models were trained on large corpora of licensed human-written code, they can occasionally emit near-verbatim reproductions, and whether that output carries the training code's license is, as of authoring, contested in active litigation and not resolved by any tool you can run.

**Tier 2 — What the field suppresses.** Two opposite distortions, each hiding the same truth about where ownership lives. The booster distortion — "the AI is your co-author / pair programmer; it shares the work, so it shares the load" — suppresses that **a co-author who cannot be paged, cannot stand in your postmortem, and has no stake in your deploy is not sharing the accountability, only the typing.** The productivity framing counts the lines you didn't write and quietly stops counting the *decisions* you did make — to prompt, to accept, to merge — which are the entire content of ownership; it lets "the assistant wrote it" function as a mitigation in a review where it is, in fact, zero mitigation. The opposite distortion — the reflexive doomer "you can never trust or own AI code, so serious engineers avoid it" — suppresses that **ownership was never about who typed the characters; it has always been about who verified and shipped**, and a decidable spec, a real read, a verification that could have failed, a cleared license, and an honest signature make generated code exactly as ownable as any code you `pip install` and read. Both camps avoid the honest middle: the model is a maximally-fluent tool whose output you are answerable for, and the professional act is neither to pretend it co-owns the outcome nor to refuse the tool, but to *own its output competently* — with the provenance to know what you're owning, the license clearance to know you may ship it, and the judgment trail to make the signature true. One camp says the responsibility splits; the other says the tool is unusable. The person who signs an attestation backed by a real trail is doing the load-bearing work neither camp will name as work.

**Tier 3 — Sit with this (a question you cannot unknow).** You have now built the record that says a model authored a function, watched the accountable party come back as *you* — or as *no one*, when no human had verified it — and felt the pull to type your name into a `verified_by` field to make a gate go green without doing the reading it stands for. That field is the whole course compressed into one keystroke: it is true only if the judgment trail behind it is real, and forging it is indistinguishable, to the gate, from earning it. Soon you will merge a screenful a model wrote, under a deadline, and a green pipeline will invite you to sign. The pipeline cannot tell whether you read it. Your name at the bottom of the deploy will assert that you did — that this behavior is fit to ship and you are answerable for it — whether or not you built the trail that would make the assertion true. **When that code runs in production and does exactly what you signed for it to do — including the thing you never verified because the gate was green and the clock was loud — the model can truthfully say it only authored the text you accepted. So at the moment it fails: whose name is on it, what did that name assert, and if you signed on the strength of a trail you never actually walked, is the signature a record of your judgment, or a forgery of it that you will nonetheless be held to?**

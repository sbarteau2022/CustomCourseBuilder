### Lesson 1 — Weight initialization: the variance that either survives or doesn't

A `Value`-based `Neuron` computes `tanh(sum(w_i * x_i) + b)` or `relu(...)` — you built this in M01 Lab M01-C. What M01 never asked is: how should `w_i` be drawn? "Randomly" was good enough for a 3-4-4-1 network trained for 30 epochs. It is not good enough once the network gets deep, and the reason is a single piece of arithmetic you can do on paper before writing a line of code.

**The variance recursion.** Let a layer's pre-activation be `z_j = sum_i(w_ji * x_i)`, with `fan_in` inputs, weights drawn i.i.d. from a zero-mean distribution with variance `std²`, and inputs `x_i` independent of the weights with variance `Var(x)`. Then `Var(z) = fan_in * std² * Var(x)` — each of the `fan_in` independent products contributes `std² * Var(x)` to the sum's variance, and variances of independent terms add. That is the *entire* mechanism behind every initialization scheme in this lesson: it is variance bookkeeping, not folklore.

For a **ReLU** layer, half of `z`'s mass (in expectation, since `z` is roughly zero-mean) gets zeroed by the nonlinearity, and the surviving half keeps `z`'s variance conditional on being positive — the standard result (Kaiming He et al., 2015) is `Var(relu(z)) ≈ 0.5 * Var(z)`. Chain the two: each ReLU layer multiplies the running variance by

```
r = 0.5 * fan_in * std²
```

If `r > 1`, variance grows every layer — geometrically, since the recursion compounds — and if `r < 1`, it shrinks the same way. `r = 1` is the only fixed point, and solving for `std` at `r = 1` gives `std = sqrt(2 / fan_in)` — **He initialization**, derived, not quoted. For a `tanh` layer (no zeroing, but a *squashing* nonlinearity) the equivalent target is `std = sqrt(1 / fan_in)` — **Xavier/Glorot initialization** — chosen so the pre-activation variance stays near 1, the region where `tanh` is still close to linear and not yet saturated into its flat tails.

**Worked example.** `layer_stats` below runs a 10-layer, width-20 net forward only (no gradient needed to see this — it's a statement about the forward pass) at three init scales, using plain Python floats (`statistics.pstdev` measures the pooled standard deviation across a batch of 200 vectors):

```python
def layer_stats(init_std, n_layers=10, width=20, batch=200, activation="tanh", seed=202):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    acts = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(width)] for _ in range(batch)]
    stds, sat_fracs = [pooled_std(acts)], []
    for _ in range(n_layers):
        W = [[rng.gauss(0, init_std) for _ in range(width)] for _ in range(width)]
        new_acts = []
        layer_sat = 0
        for a in acts:
            z = [sum(a[i] * W[j][i] for i in range(width)) for j in range(width)]
            if activation == "tanh":
                out = [math.tanh(v) for v in z]
                layer_sat += sum(1 for v in out if abs(v) > 0.99)
            else:
                out = [max(0.0, v) for v in z]
            new_acts.append(out)
        acts = new_acts
        stds.append(pooled_std(acts))
        if activation == "tanh":
            sat_fracs.append(layer_sat / (batch * width))
    return stds, sat_fracs
```

Measured output (`width=20`, `n_layers=10`):

| Init | layer-by-layer activation std | `tanh` saturation (`\|out\|>0.99`) |
|---|---|---|
| tanh, naive `N(0,1)` (`std=1.0`) | `1.00 → 0.91 → 0.90 → 0.89 → 0.89 → 0.90 → 0.90 → 0.91 → 0.91 → 0.89 → 0.90` | **47–56% of units every layer** |
| tanh, Xavier (`std=1/√20 ≈ 0.224`) | `1.00 → 0.64 → 0.50 → 0.40 → 0.33 → 0.32 → 0.28 → 0.30 → 0.31 → 0.23 → 0.21` | **0–1% of units every layer** |
| ReLU, naive `N(0,1)` (`std=1.0`, `fan_in=20`) | `1.00 → 2.69 → 8.69 → 31.1 → 93.0 → 397 → 690 → 1.69e3 → 4.54e3 → 1.72e4 → 4.45e4` | — |
| ReLU, He (`std=√(2/20) ≈ 0.316`) | `1.00 → 0.85 → 0.87 → 0.98 → 0.93 → 1.26 → 0.69 → 0.54 → 0.45 → 0.55 → 0.45` | — |

Read the naive-`tanh` row against the theory: activation *std* looks almost stable (`~0.9` throughout) — it does **not** look broken by eyeballing that number alone. The saturation column is the tell: roughly half of every layer's units sit at `|tanh| > 0.99`, meaning `tanh`'s local slope `1 - tanh² ≈ 0` there — that's a *vanishing gradient* problem hiding behind a forward pass that looks superficially fine. The naive-ReLU row shows the opposite, more visible failure: `r = 0.5 * 20 * 1.0² = 10`, so variance multiplies by 10 and std multiplies by `√10 ≈ 3.16` every layer; after 10 layers that predicts `std ≈ 3.16¹⁰ ≈ 9.3e4`, and the measured `4.45e4` lands in the same order of magnitude — an *exploding* activation problem, visible immediately in the raw numbers.

**Named misconception 1 — "more layers just means slower training."** No: a deep net at the wrong init scale is not a slower version of a working net, it is a different, broken regime. The naive-`tanh` row above trains *nothing* usefully past the first couple of layers — roughly half its units are locked at a gradient of ~0 regardless of how long you wait or how small a learning rate you pick. Depth without initialization discipline does not degrade gracefully.

**Named misconception 2 — "small weights are always the safe default."** Refuted directly by Lab M02-A: `std=0.05` on a 12-layer ReLU net (fan_in=16) drives the activation standard deviation from `1.0` down to `2.6e-11` — every signal in the network is, for practical floating-point purposes, zero by the output layer, and the gradient flowing back through it is proportionately tiny (Lab M02-A measures this directly). "Small" is not "safe" — it is a *different* failure mode than "large," with the same root cause: `r ≠ 1`.

### Lesson 2 — Batch normalization, derived and built

Lesson 1's whole argument rests on one number, `r = 0.5 * fan_in * std²`, staying near 1 at every layer. That's fragile — it depends on getting `fan_in` and `std` right for *every* layer, and it says nothing about what happens once training starts moving the weights away from their init values. Batch normalization sidesteps the whole bookkeeping problem with a different strategy: instead of choosing an init that makes each layer's output variance land near 1, **force** it to land near 1, every forward pass, by direct computation.

**Forward pass, derived.** For a batch of `B` examples, and one feature (one neuron's pre-activation) `j`, compute across the batch:

```
mu_j    = mean_b(x[b][j])
var_j   = mean_b((x[b][j] - mu_j)²)
xhat[b][j] = (x[b][j] - mu_j) / sqrt(var_j + eps)
y[b][j]    = gamma_j * xhat[b][j] + beta_j
```

`xhat` has, by construction, zero mean and unit variance across the batch, for *every* feature, *regardless* of what scale the incoming weights produced. `eps` (typically `1e-5`) exists purely so `var_j = 0` (a genuinely constant feature, or a batch of size 1) never causes a division by zero. `gamma_j` and `beta_j` are **learned parameters**, one pair per feature, initialized to `1` and `0` — without them, every layer would be forced to have exactly unit variance and zero mean forever, which would remove the network's ability to represent a genuinely large or shifted feature when that's the right answer. Batch norm's actual claim is narrower than "normalize the activations": it's "normalize the activations, then hand the network an explicit, learnable dial to *undo* that normalization, per feature, if the loss says it should."

**Why this is exactly a `Value`-engine operation, not a new engine.** Every symbol in the forward pass above — subtraction, a mean (a sum divided by a constant), squaring, a square root (`** 0.5`), a division — is an operation the M01 `Value` class already knows how to backpropagate through. Building `BatchNorm1d` is composition, not new machinery:

```python
class BatchNorm1d:
    def __init__(self, width, eps=1e-5, momentum=0.1):
        self.width, self.eps, self.momentum = width, eps, momentum
        self.gamma = [Value(1.0) for _ in range(width)]
        self.beta  = [Value(0.0) for _ in range(width)]
        self.running_mean = [0.0] * width
        self.running_var  = [1.0] * width
        self.training = True

    def __call__(self, batch):          # batch: list of B rows, each a list of `width` Values
        B = len(batch)
        if self.training:
            means, vars_ = [], []
            for j in range(self.width):
                col = [batch[b][j] for b in range(B)]
                mu  = sum(col, Value(0.0)) * (1.0 / B)
                var = sum(((c - mu) ** 2 for c in col), Value(0.0)) * (1.0 / B)
                means.append(mu); vars_.append(var)
                self.running_mean[j] = (1 - self.momentum) * self.running_mean[j] + self.momentum * mu.data
                self.running_var[j]  = (1 - self.momentum) * self.running_var[j]  + self.momentum * var.data
        else:
            means = [Value(self.running_mean[j]) for j in range(self.width)]
            vars_ = [Value(self.running_var[j])  for j in range(self.width)]

        out = []
        for b in range(B):
            row = [(batch[b][j] - means[j]) * ((vars_[j] + self.eps) ** -0.5) * self.gamma[j] + self.beta[j]
                   for j in range(self.width)]
            out.append(row)
        return out
```

**Train vs. eval mode.** Notice `training` branches the forward pass. During training, `mu_j`/`var_j` are computed *from the current minibatch* — which is exactly what makes this layer differentiable end to end and exactly what makes it dependent on which other examples happen to be in the batch (a genuine, documented cost, revisited in Lesson 2's second misconception). At evaluation time you usually do not have a representative batch — you may be scoring one example at a time — so batch statistics would be noisy or meaningless. Instead, `BatchNorm1d` maintains `running_mean`/`running_var`, an exponential moving average of every training batch's statistics (`momentum=0.1` here means each new batch nudges the running estimate by 10%), and eval mode uses those fixed, accumulated numbers instead of recomputing from whatever batch happens to be present.

**Worked example — the same bad init from Lesson 1, rescued.** A 3-hidden-layer, width-8 network, deliberately initialized at `std=1.5` (`r = 0.5 * 8 * 1.5² = 9`, badly over 1 — the exploding regime from Lesson 1), trained with plain SGD on a small two-moons classification task:

| step | loss, bad init, **no** BatchNorm | loss, bad init, **with** BatchNorm |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2.3760 | 1.8007 |
| 15 | 0.9578 | 0.0368 |
| 40 | 0.9998 | 0.0088 |
| 79 | 0.9960 | **0.0044** |

Without normalization, the badly-initialized net never escapes a loss around chance level — it bounces (`0.96 → 1.00 → 1.00`), climbing back up past its own step-15 value before flatlining just under `1.0`, without ever committing to a meaningful downward trend, exactly the saturated/exploded-then-stuck signature Lesson 1 predicts for `r=9` compounded over 3 layers. With `BatchNorm1d` inserted after every hidden linear layer and before the ReLU, the *same* bad init trains cleanly to a loss two orders of magnitude smaller. Nothing about the initialization changed — the normalization layer simply refuses to let a bad `r` propagate past it.

**Named misconception 1 — "batch norm makes careful init unnecessary."** It makes the *specific* failure mode from Lesson 1 (compounding per-layer variance) much less sensitive to init scale — the table above demonstrates exactly that generosity. It does not make init irrelevant: extremely large weights can still saturate a layer's output *before* normalization sees it in a way that damages gradient quality even after re-standardization (`var_j` in a nearly-degenerate batch is itself a noisy statistic if the pre-normalization values already overflowed or collapsed), and it does nothing at all for the learning-rate and optimizer problems in Lessons 3 and 4. "Combine sane init with normalization" is the actual practice; "normalization replaces init discipline" is the misconception.

**Named misconception 2 — "batch norm and layer norm are interchangeable normalization tricks."** They normalize across *different axes* and that difference has teeth. `BatchNorm1d`, as built above, computes `mu_j`/`var_j` **across the batch dimension**, separately for each feature — which means its statistics depend on which other examples are in the batch, degrade at very small batch sizes (a batch of 2 gives you a genuinely unreliable variance estimate), and require the train/eval running-statistics machinery this lesson just built. **Layer normalization** (previewed here, built when it matters — the transformer module, M05) instead normalizes **across the feature dimension, per example**, independent of batch size and with no train/eval distinction needed, because every example carries its own statistics. The two are not swappable defaults; the axis you normalize over is a design decision with real consequences, and "just use batch norm" is exactly the kind of unexamined default this course's ethics thread will keep returning to.

### Lesson 3 — Optimizers beyond SGD: momentum and Adam, derived

Everything so far has assumed the update rule `p.data -= lr * p.grad` (plain SGD, the only update M01 used). Plain SGD has a specific, geometric weakness: it takes a step proportional to the *current* gradient alone, with no memory of where it has been and no per-parameter sense of scale. Both of those absences hurt on the textbook diagnostic case for this lesson — an **ill-conditioned bowl**, `f(x, y) = x² + 25y²`. Its curvature (second derivative) is `2` along `x` and `50` along `y` — a **condition number** of `50 / 2 = 25`. A single learning rate has to serve both directions at once, and that is the entire source of the difficulty.

**Why plain SGD struggles here — the stability limit.** For gradient descent on a quadratic with curvature `L`, the update is stable (does not diverge) only for `lr < 2 / L`. Along `y`, `L = 50`, so the threshold is `lr < 2/50 = 0.04`. Any `lr` large enough to make real progress along the gentle `x` direction (`L=2`, threshold `lr < 1.0`) risks blowing past the `y` threshold entirely.

**Momentum, derived from a physical analogy.** Instead of `p -= lr * grad`, accumulate a velocity: `v ← momentum * v + grad`, then `p -= lr * v`. Think of `v` as a heavy ball's velocity: gradients that keep pointing the same way (the shallow `x` direction, where progress is consistent step after step) accumulate and *reinforce* — the ball picks up speed. Gradients that keep flipping sign (the steep `y` direction, once you're oscillating around the bottom of that narrow ravine) partially cancel in the running average — the ball's oscillation gets damped rather than amplified. This is not merely "a bigger learning rate": it is a *directionally selective* filter on the raw gradient signal.

**Adam, derived as momentum plus per-parameter adaptive scaling.** Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) keeps *two* exponential moving averages per parameter: a first moment (mean of the gradient, like momentum) and a second moment (mean of the squared gradient, an estimate of that parameter's typical gradient magnitude):

```
m_t = β1 * m_{t-1} + (1 - β1) * g_t              # first moment (β1 ≈ 0.9)
v_t = β2 * v_{t-1} + (1 - β2) * g_t²             # second moment (β2 ≈ 0.999)
m̂_t = m_t / (1 - β1^t)                           # bias correction
v̂_t = v_t / (1 - β2^t)                           # bias correction
θ  -= lr * m̂_t / (sqrt(v̂_t) + eps)
```

Dividing by `sqrt(v̂_t)` rescales *each parameter's own step* by its own typical gradient magnitude — a parameter living on the steep `y` axis (large, noisy gradients) gets its step shrunk; a parameter on the shallow `x` axis (small, consistent gradients) gets its step enlarged. This is exactly what a single global learning rate cannot do, and it is exactly why Adam is comparatively insensitive to the condition number that punishes plain SGD.

**Bias correction, explained rather than just stated.** `m_0 = v_0 = 0`, so in the first few steps `m_t`/`v_t` are biased toward zero (they are exponential moving averages that have barely had any real observations mixed in yet). Dividing by `(1 - β1^t)` and `(1 - β2^t)` — both close to 0 at small `t` and close to 1 for large `t` — corrects exactly for that startup transient. Without it, the earliest updates would be artificially tiny; Lesson 4 returns to this same startup transient as the theoretical motivation for warmup.

**Worked example — the ill-conditioned bowl, raced.** All three optimizers start from the same point (`x=-1, y=1`, `loss=26`) at the *same* base learning rate the plain-SGD stability analysis flags as risky (`lr=0.05`, momentum getting the same `lr=0.05`, Adam scaled to its own conventional range at `lr=0.2`):

| step | plain SGD (`lr=0.05`) | momentum (`lr=0.05`, `β=0.9`) | Adam (`lr=0.2`) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 26.00000 | 26.00000 | 26.00000 |
| 5 | 1441.97 | 13.28688 | **0.08046** |
| 15 | 4,793,776 | 3.65212 | 1.35683 |
| 30 | 9.19e11 | 0.52044 | 0.04584 |
| 60 | 3.38e22 (diverged) | **0.00581** | 0.03112 |

Plain SGD diverges — `lr=0.05 > 2/50 = 0.04`, exactly the threshold derived above, and the loss grows without bound. Momentum, at the *identical* `lr=0.05`, converges cleanly and reaches the lowest final loss of the three. Adam makes the fastest early progress by a wide margin (`0.08` vs. momentum's `13.29` at step 5) but is *not* the best final answer here — at step 60 it sits at `0.031`, worse than momentum's `0.0058`. Hold both facts at once; the next misconception is built entirely out of this table.

**Named misconception 1 — "momentum is just a bigger learning rate."** Refuted directly by the table: plain SGD and momentum share the exact same `lr=0.05`. One diverges to `3.4e22`; the other converges to `0.0058`. The difference cannot be step size — it is the directional filtering the physical-ball analogy predicts: momentum damps the oscillating (steep, unstable) direction and reinforces the consistent (shallow, stable) one, which is a qualitatively different mechanism from scaling every step up or down uniformly.

**Named misconception 2 — "Adam always beats SGD/momentum."** Refuted directly by the same table, read to the last column: Adam wins early (step 5) and loses late (step 60) to momentum on this exact problem. Adam's per-parameter adaptive denominator, `sqrt(v̂_t) + eps`, is a *biased, noisy estimate* of local curvature — excellent for making rapid early progress across a badly-conditioned landscape, and worse than a well-tuned momentum term for precisely settling once you're close to a minimum. This asymmetry between fast-early and precise-late is documented in the optimization literature (e.g., Wilson et al., "The Marginal Value of Adaptive Gradient Methods in Machine Learning," 2017) as a genuine, empirically observed property, not a bug in this particular implementation — "just use Adam" is a defensible default, not a universal one.

### Lesson 4 — Learning-rate schedules: step decay, cosine annealing, warmup

Lesson 3 fixed the *direction* problem (which way to step) and the *scale* problem (how much per parameter). It left one dial untouched: the base learning rate itself is still a single constant across the entire run. That constant faces two opposite pressures at two different points in training.

**Late in training, under noise, a constant LR stops you from settling.** Real training doesn't see the exact gradient of the full loss — it sees a noisy estimate from a minibatch. Near a minimum, that noise is often larger than the true remaining gradient, so a fixed step size keeps the parameters *bouncing* around the minimum rather than settling into it, no matter how long you wait. **Cosine annealing** shrinks the learning rate smoothly from its base value to (near) zero over the course of training: `lr(t) = 0.5 * base_lr * (1 + cos(π * t / total_steps))`. Early on, `lr` is near `base_lr` for fast progress; late, it approaches `0`, converting the same noisy gradient signal into a smaller and smaller step, letting the parameters settle instead of orbit.

**Worked example — the same bowl, made noisy.** Add Gaussian noise (`σ=0.6`) to every gradient before the momentum update, at `lr=0.06`, to simulate minibatch stochasticity, and compare the mean loss over the *last 20* of 120 steps (the settled regime, not the initial descent):

| schedule | mean loss, last 20 steps | final-step loss |
|---|---|---|
| constant `lr=0.06` | 0.16809 | 0.12027 |
| cosine decay to 0 | 0.01487 | 0.01198 |
| cosine decay + 15-step warmup | **0.00958** | **0.00770** |

A constant learning rate under noise settles to a mean loss an order of magnitude worse than either decaying schedule — it is still bouncing at step 120, not converging. Cosine decay alone recovers most of that gap by shrinking the step size as training proceeds; adding a short warmup at the start recovers a further, smaller improvement.

**Warmup, motivated by Lesson 3's bias-correction argument.** Early in training, an adaptive optimizer's second-moment estimate `v_t` has seen very few gradients — it is a high-variance, unreliable estimate of that parameter's true typical gradient scale (this is precisely the transient bias correction was built to compensate for in Lesson 3, and bias correction only fixes the *mean* of the estimate, not its variance). A large step taken confidently in a direction estimated from an unreliable `v_t` risks a genuinely bad early update. **Warmup** — linearly ramping the learning rate up from ~0 to `base_lr` over the first `k` steps — delays taking full-sized steps until the optimizer's internal statistics have had a chance to stabilize. This reasoning (not a toy numeric demonstration — the effect is real but does not reproduce cleanly on a two-parameter toy bowl at small step counts, and this lesson will not manufacture a demo that doesn't actually show what it claims to) is documented directly in the optimization literature on Adam's early-training instability (e.g., Liu et al., "On the Variance of the Adaptive Learning Rate and Beyond" — the RAdam paper, 2020), which is exactly why warmup shows up by default in nearly every modern Adam-based training recipe, including every transformer training run you will see starting in M05.

**Named misconception 1 — "warmup is only a transformer trick."** The transformer paper (Vaswani et al., 2017) made warmup famous, but the mechanism above — an adaptive optimizer's early moment estimates being unreliable — has nothing to do with attention or sequence models specifically. It applies to any deep network trained with Adam (or any adaptive optimizer with accumulated statistics), and it also interacts with Lesson 2's `BatchNorm1d`: that layer's running statistics are *also* an exponential moving average with the same "unreliable while young" property, initialized from a handful of early batches. Warmup is a general answer to "my optimizer/normalization statistics haven't stabilized yet," not an architecture-specific trick.

**Named misconception 2 — "more aggressive decay is always the safer choice."** A schedule that decays the learning rate too early or too fast can freeze training in a mediocre basin before it has had the chance to explore — the fast-progress phase of training (visible in every table in this lesson as the first several steps) depends on the learning rate staying large enough, long enough, to actually move. A schedule is a tradeoff between exploration speed and settling precision, tuned to the problem, not a dial where "more decay" is a monotonically safer choice.

from value import Value


def make_layer(width, init_std, rng):

def run_diagnostic(init_std, n_layers=12, width=16, batch=16, seed=202):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    layers = [make_layer(width, init_std, rng) for _ in range(n_layers)]
    xs = [[Value(rng.gauss(0, 1)) for _ in range(width)] for _ in range(batch)]

# lab_b_solution.py
import math
import random
from value import Value


class Linear:
    def __init__(self, nin, nout, init_std, rng):
        self.W = [[Value(rng.gauss(0, init_std)) for _ in range(nin)] for _ in range(nout)]
        self.b = [Value(0.0) for _ in range(nout)]

    def __call__(self, x):
        return [sum((self.W[j][i] * x[i] for i in range(len(x))), self.b[j])
                for j in range(len(self.W))]

    def parameters(self):
        return [w for row in self.W for w in row] + self.b


class BatchNorm1d:
    def __init__(self, width, eps=1e-5, momentum=0.1):
        self.width, self.eps, self.momentum = width, eps, momentum
        self.gamma = [Value(1.0) for _ in range(width)]
        self.beta = [Value(0.0) for _ in range(width)]
        self.running_mean = [0.0] * width
        self.running_var = [1.0] * width
        self.training = True

    def __call__(self, batch):
        B = len(batch)
        if self.training:
            means, vars_ = [], []
            for j in range(self.width):
                col = [batch[b][j] for b in range(B)]
                mu = sum(col, Value(0.0)) * (1.0 / B)
                var = sum(((c - mu) ** 2 for c in col), Value(0.0)) * (1.0 / B)
                means.append(mu); vars_.append(var)
                self.running_mean[j] = (1 - self.momentum) * self.running_mean[j] + self.momentum * mu.data
                self.running_var[j] = (1 - self.momentum) * self.running_var[j] + self.momentum * var.data
        else:
            means = [Value(self.running_mean[j]) for j in range(self.width)]
            vars_ = [Value(self.running_var[j]) for j in range(self.width)]

        out = []
        for b in range(B):
            row = [(batch[b][j] - means[j]) * ((vars_[j] + self.eps) ** -0.5) * self.gamma[j] + self.beta[j]
                   for j in range(self.width)]
            out.append(row)
        return out

    def parameters(self):
        return self.gamma + self.beta


class MLP:
    def __init__(self, dims, init_std, use_bn, seed=1):
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        self.use_bn = use_bn
        self.lins, self.bns = [], []
        for i in range(len(dims) - 2):
            self.lins.append(Linear(dims[i], dims[i + 1], init_std, rng))
            self.bns.append(BatchNorm1d(dims[i + 1]) if use_bn else None)
        self.out = Linear(dims[-2], dims[-1], init_std, rng)

    def __call__(self, X):
        batch = [[Value(v) for v in x] for x in X]
        for lin, bn in zip(self.lins, self.bns):
            batch = [lin(row) for row in batch]
            if bn is not None:
                batch = bn(batch)
            batch = [[v.relu() for v in row] for row in batch]
        batch = [[v.tanh() for v in self.out(row)] for row in batch]
        return batch

    def parameters(self):
        ps = []
        for lin in self.lins:
            ps += lin.parameters()
        for bn in self.bns:
            if bn is not None:
                ps += bn.parameters()
        ps += self.out.parameters()
        return ps


def make_moons(n=24, noise=0.15, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n1 = n // 2
    n2 = n - n1
    X, y = [], []
    for _ in range(n1):
        t = rng.uniform(0, math.pi)
        X.append([math.cos(t) + rng.gauss(0, noise), math.sin(t) + rng.gauss(0, noise)])
        y.append(1.0)
    for _ in range(n2):
        t = rng.uniform(0, math.pi)
        X.append([1 - math.cos(t) + rng.gauss(0, noise), 1 - math.sin(t) - 0.5 + rng.gauss(0, noise)])
        y.append(-1.0)
    idx = list(range(n))
    rng.shuffle(idx)
    return [X[i] for i in idx], [y[i] for i in idx]


def train(model, X, y, lr=0.1, steps=80):
    losses = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        for p in model.parameters():
            p.grad = 0.0
        preds = model(X)
        loss = Value(0.0)
        for pred_row, target in zip(preds, y):
            loss = loss + (pred_row[0] - target) ** 2
        loss = loss * (1.0 / len(y))
        loss.backward()
        for p in model.parameters():
            p.data -= lr * p.grad
        losses.append(loss.data)
    return losses

# lab_c_solution.py
import math
import random
from value import Value


class SGD:
    def __init__(self, params, lr=0.01, momentum=0.0):
        self.params = params
        self.lr = lr
        self.momentum = momentum
        self.velocity = [0.0 for _ in params]

    def step(self):
        for i, p in enumerate(self.params):
            self.velocity[i] = self.momentum * self.velocity[i] + p.grad
            p.data -= self.lr * self.velocity[i]

    def zero_grad(self):
        for p in self.params:
            p.grad = 0.0


class Adam:
    def __init__(self, params, lr=0.001, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8):
        self.params = params
        self.lr = lr
        self.b1, self.b2 = betas
        self.eps = eps
        self.m = [0.0 for _ in params]
        self.v = [0.0 for _ in params]
        self.t = 0

    def step(self):
        self.t += 1
        for i, p in enumerate(self.params):
            g = p.grad
            self.m[i] = self.b1 * self.m[i] + (1 - self.b1) * g
            self.v[i] = self.b2 * self.v[i] + (1 - self.b2) * g * g
            m_hat = self.m[i] / (1 - self.b1 ** self.t)
            v_hat = self.v[i] / (1 - self.b2 ** self.t)
            p.data -= self.lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(v_hat) + self.eps)

    def zero_grad(self):
        for p in self.params:
            p.grad = 0.0


def cosine_with_warmup(base_lr, t, total_steps, warmup_steps=0):
    if warmup_steps > 0 and t < warmup_steps:
        return base_lr * (t + 1) / warmup_steps
    progress = min((t - warmup_steps) / max(1, total_steps - warmup_steps), 1.0)
    return 0.5 * base_lr * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

## Assessment

Maps to the course's **build milestones** component (this module's fixes are load-bearing for every subsequent milestone — a CNN or transformer trained on this engine without them will exhibit the exact same failure modes at larger scale) and the **broken-run clinics** component, which this module's labs are the direct rehearsal for.

**The unseen exam artifact.** The learner receives a training log (loss per step, no source code) from a network built on the M01/M02 machinery — some combination of init scale, `BatchNorm1d`, and an optimizer with a schedule — with exactly **one planted pathology**, drawn from this module's bug-family taxonomy:

1. **Learning rate an order of magnitude too high.** Signature: sharp early oscillation, then a flatline at a mediocre (not catastrophic) loss — dead ReLU units knocked into their zero-gradient region by an overlarge early step, after which nothing in the network can recover, since a dead ReLU unit's local gradient is exactly zero forever.
2. **A broken `BatchNorm1d` — running statistics never updated (or updated with the wrong momentum sign/scale).** Signature: training-mode loss looks fine, but a held-out eval pass (which uses `running_mean`/`running_var`) performs dramatically worse than the training loss would predict.
3. **Adam's bias correction omitted.** Signature: an anomalously slow start (`m_hat`, `v_hat` implicitly near zero for the first many steps since raw `m`, `v` are still small, undercorrected) that gradually speeds up as the moving averages accumulate real signal — distinguishable from "learning rate too low" because the *rate* of improvement visibly accelerates over the first several dozen steps rather than staying flat.
4. **Momentum coefficient far too high** (e.g., `0.999` instead of `0.9`) **at an otherwise reasonable learning rate.** Signature: large, slow oscillations that don't diverge but also don't settle — velocity accumulates faster than the loss landscape's curvature can correct it.

The learner must:

- **(a) Predict** — from the loss curve's shape alone (rate of change, presence/absence of oscillation, whether it flatlines or keeps drifting, whether eval performance diverges from training performance), name the specific bug family and state the curve evidence that rules out the other three.
- **(b) Locate** — given the source after (a) is submitted, point to the exact line or parameter responsible.
- **(c) Fix** — correct it, touching nothing else.
- **(d) Prove** — submit the fixed run's loss curve, showing the recovery, in the same format as this module's own worked examples (a before-table and an after-table, not a claim in prose).

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject: the techniques that make deep networks trainable at all, and what confidently deploying them without a settled theoretical account underneath them should cost you.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Every technique in this module was adopted into standard practice well before — sometimes years before — the field had a rigorous account of *why* it works. He initialization (2015) and Xavier initialization (2010) both have real derivations, reproduced in Lesson 1, and both derivations are documented in the field's own peer-reviewed literature, not proprietary or contested. Batch normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) was adopted essentially immediately upon publication and is standard in production architectures to this day.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** Batch normalization's *own paper* proposed a specific mechanism for why it works — "internal covariate shift," the idea that normalizing layer inputs reduces the degree to which each layer has to continuously readjust to a shifting input distribution during training. A widely cited follow-up study (Santurkar, Tsipras, Ilyas & Madry, "How Does Batch Normalization Help Optimization?", 2018) ran controlled experiments and found this explanation does not hold up — batch norm helps for reasons closer to smoothing the optimization landscape than to the covariate-shift story its own inventors proposed, and the field kept using batch norm at full confidence throughout the years between the original explanation and its correction. This is not a story about a bad technique — batch norm works, robustly, exactly as this module's worked example demonstrated. It is a story about a good technique whose *justification*, confidently stated by its own inventors and taught for years, was empirically wrong, and about how little that turned out to matter for whether practitioners kept shipping it. "We understand why this works" and "this reliably works" are different claims, and the field's own history in this exact module's subject matter is the demonstration, not an abstract worry imported from elsewhere.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** By the end of this module you will have derived every fix in it, gradchecked the ones that touch the backward pass, and watched each one turn a broken training curve into a working one, on evidence you produced yourself. That is a real, earned form of confidence — stronger than "I read that batch norm helps." But batch normalization's own inventors had exactly that kind of derivation-and-evidence confidence in *their* explanation too, for years, and were specifically wrong about the mechanism while being completely right about the outcome. If you can be right that a fix works and wrong about why, and ship it anyway because it works — which is exactly what the field did with batch norm for three years — what should that cost your confidence the next time you explain, to someone else, why one of *your* models does what it does?

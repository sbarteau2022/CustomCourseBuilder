## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Reading a loss curve like a stack trace

A loss curve is one float per step. That's the entire signal — the whole multi-million-parameter, multi-layer computation that ran between one number and the next gets compressed into a single scalar, and your job in this lesson is to get disciplined about how little that scalar actually proves. M06's assessment already showed you a curve that looked like it was recovering (`step 2: loss=0.504914 <- looks great, converging fast`) one step before it got dramatically worse — the lesson there was that a curve is a trajectory, not a verdict, and you have to read the *shape*, not the most recent number. This lesson generalizes that reading practice to a small vocabulary of shapes you'll see over and over, on real training runs, for the rest of your career.

Four archetypal shapes, in the order you'll learn to recognize them:

1. **Smooth, roughly monotone decrease.** The textbook picture. Necessary for a healthy run. Not sufficient — plenty of broken runs also produce a curve that goes down, just to the wrong place, or for the wrong reason, or more slowly than a correct run would.
2. **Divergence — growing, and increasingly volatile.** Not just "went up once." A genuinely exploding run shows the *variance* of the curve growing epoch over epoch, often with the loss itself trending upward through the noise, sometimes ending in `nan`.
3. **A plateau at a suspicious constant.** For a binary classifier trained with cross-entropy, a network that has learned nothing about its inputs — because there's nothing there to learn, or because whatever's there has been decoupled from the labels — settles at `-log(0.5) = ln(2) ≈ 0.6931`: the loss of a model that outputs 50/50 for everything. For `k`-way classification the same floor sits at `ln(k)`. This constant is not a coincidence you'll rediscover by curve-shape alone; it's the entropy of a uniform guess, and it is worth memorizing as a number, the way you memorized `ln(2)` matters to bits vs. nats back in AIE-201.
4. **Train loss fine, validation loss/accuracy wrong from the very first epoch**, not gradually the way overfitting drifts apart over many epochs. Sharp, immediate train/val divergence is a different failure mode from overfitting, and conflating the two sends you looking for "more data" or "more regularization" when the actual defect is somewhere in how train-time and eval-time computation differ.

**Worked example.** Here are the first four training-loss values from two of this module's five reference runs (both are `pytest`-verified, real PyTorch training logs you'll work with directly in Lab M07-A — nothing in this module is a hand-typed illustrative number):

```
epoch    healthy run    broken run
  0        0.7045         0.7044
  1        0.6948         0.6994
  2        0.6824         0.6934
  3        0.6713         0.6919
```

At epoch 0 these are within `0.0001` of each other — genuinely indistinguishable. By epoch 3 they've separated, but only by `0.02`, and a learner glancing at four numbers and seeing "both going down, looks fine" is not making an unreasonable read *from four numbers alone*. The full eight-epoch picture (Lesson 3, and Lab M07-A) makes the difference obvious: the healthy run keeps falling to `0.65`, the broken one asymptotes at `0.687` — a hair below `ln(2) = 0.6931` — and stays there. The lesson isn't "you should have caught it at epoch 3." The lesson is that loss curves under-report bad news early, systematically, in exactly the direction that makes a broken run look like a slow-starting healthy one, which is precisely why you don't stop at the curve — Lesson 2 gives you two more signals that catch this one at epoch 0, not epoch 7.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A smoothly-decreasing loss curve after several epochs is sufficient evidence of a correctly wired training run."** It is *consistent with* a correct run. It is also consistent with several incorrect ones, including the plateau-at-`ln(2)` run above, which decreases smoothly for its entire first half before you can see it's heading nowhere useful. Consistency with a hypothesis is not confirmation of it — the same gradcheck-era discipline from M01, restated for training runs instead of individual gradients.
- **"NaN or exploding loss means the architecture is broken."** Almost never true in practice. It is overwhelmingly a hyperparameter or training-loop defect — learning rate too high, missing gradient clipping, a bad initialization scale — layered on top of an architecture that is otherwise completely correct. Treating an exploding loss as evidence you got the model wrong sends you rewriting layers instead of checking the three or four much cheaper hypotheses first.
- **"The loss curve alone is enough to tell the four pathology families apart."** It isn't, and this lesson's worked example is the proof: a silent data bug and a merely slow-to-converge healthy run can look nearly identical for several epochs on the loss curve alone. The loss curve narrows the hypothesis space. It does not, by itself, close it — that's what Lesson 2's extra instruments are for.

### Lesson 2 — Instrumenting a black box you didn't write: grad norms and activation statistics

You are not always going to get to add print statements inside someone else's `forward()`. What you *can* always do in PyTorch — without touching a single line of the model's own source — is attach a **forward hook** to any `nn.Module` and read the **gradient** off any parameter after `.backward()` has run. Both are the same idea M01 built into you at the substrate level: `Tensor.backward()` walks the graph and writes into `.grad`; PyTorch's `nn.Parameter.grad` is that same field, on the same kind of object, just populated by a compiled engine instead of your own closures. A hook is nothing more than a callback PyTorch invokes with a module's input and output tensors at the moment they pass through — it does not require, or permit, modifying the module itself.

```python
# diagnostics.py — the grad-norm + activation-stat harness this whole module runs on
import torch

class Diagnostics:
    """Attach to a subset of a model's layers to record, once per training
    step: (1) each hooked layer's output mean/std, via a forward hook, and
    (2) each named parameter's gradient norm, read directly off .grad.

    Call .snapshot() *after* loss.backward() and *before* optimizer.step()
    or optimizer.zero_grad() -- both destroy the information .snapshot()
    needs: .step() moves the parameters that produced this gradient,
    .zero_grad() erases the gradient itself.
    """
    def __init__(self, activation_layers: dict, grad_params: dict):
        # activation_layers: {"bn1": model.bn1, "bn3": model.bn3, ...}
        # grad_params:        {"fc1": model.fc1.weight, ...}
        self.grad_params = grad_params
        self._acts = {}
        self._handles = [
            module.register_forward_hook(self._make_hook(name))
            for name, module in activation_layers.items()
        ]

    def _make_hook(self, name):
        def hook(module, inputs, output):
            self._acts[name] = (output.mean().item(), output.std().item())
        return hook

    def grad_norms(self) -> dict:
        """None means 'no gradient was computed for this parameter' --
        either requires_grad is False, or it never reached the graph this
        step (e.g. behind a stray .detach()). This is NOT the same thing
        as a gradient that was computed and happens to be near zero --
        collapsing the two into one case is the single most common way
        this diagnostic gets built wrong. See Lesson 2, Misconception 2."""
        return {
            name: (None if p.grad is None else p.grad.norm().item())
            for name, p in self.grad_params.items()
        }

    def snapshot(self) -> dict:
        row = {}
        for name, (mu, sd) in self._acts.items():
            row[f"{name}_mu"], row[f"{name}_sd"] = mu, sd
        for name, gn in self.grad_norms().items():
            row[f"gn_{name}"] = gn
        return row

    def close(self):
        for h in self._handles:
            h.remove()
```

For any layer that ends in `nn.BatchNorm1d`/`2d`, the forward-hook statistic is not an arbitrary number to eyeball — it's a check against a stated invariant. BatchNorm's entire job, at training time, is to make its output have mean ≈ 0 and std ≈ 1 across the batch, per feature. That's not a side effect you're inferring; it's the operation's contract, straight from Ioffe & Szegedy's 2015 paper. Which means `bn_mu`/`bn_sd` sitting near `(0, 1)` isn't "looks healthy" — it's the layer doing exactly what it's specified to do, and any sustained departure from it is evidence the specification is being violated somewhere, not a vibe.

**Worked example.** This module's `D_frozen_layer` reference run (full log in Lab M07-A) reports gradient norms for four weight matrices at epoch 0:

```
gn_fc1=0.0712   gn_fc2=None   gn_fc3=0.2959   gn_fc4=0.1241
```

Read this the way you'd read a stack trace: three of four layers received a nonzero, plausible-magnitude gradient. One reports `None` — not `0.0000`, `None`. That is `Diagnostics.grad_norms()` telling you, unambiguously, "this parameter's `.grad` attribute was never populated this step." There are exactly two mechanisms that produce that: `requires_grad=False` on the parameter, or the tensor never reaching the graph (a stray `.detach()`, or a forward pass that recomputes the layer's input from an unrelated tensor). Both are silent — no exception, no warning, the forward pass runs and produces plausible-looking numbers, and `loss.backward()` completes without complaint. The `None` in one specific column, held for eight straight epochs while the other three columns move, is the entire diagnosis. You do not need to see `fc2`'s definition to know something about it is wrong.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A single global gradient norm — summed or averaged across every parameter — is enough to monitor training health."** It is not, and `D_frozen_layer` is the direct counterexample: the global norm over all four layers is still comfortably nonzero, because `fc1`, `fc3`, and `fc4` are all contributing to it, and it would look completely unremarkable on a dashboard that only plots one aggregate number per step. The frozen layer is invisible at the global level and glaringly obvious at the per-layer level. Aggregating always trades away exactly the resolution you need for this kind of bug.
- **"If the gradient norm isn't NaN and isn't exploding, gradient flow is fine."** A `None` grad is a third, completely distinct failure mode from "computed and huge" or "computed and NaN" — it's "never computed at all" — and a monitoring setup that only checks `is_nan()` or `> threshold` will never catch it, because `None` fails an `is_nan()` check silently (or crashes the monitoring code outright, which is its own kind of silent if nobody's watching stderr).
- **"Forward hooks only tell you about the forward pass, so they can't help diagnose a gradient bug."** They tell you about the forward pass, and the forward pass is often where a gradient bug becomes visible *first* — Lesson 3's normalization-mistake run shows an activation-statistic anomaly at epoch 0, readable from a forward hook alone, a full several epochs before any grad-norm column looks obviously wrong. Forward-hook data and post-backward grad data triangulate; neither alone is the whole instrument.

### Lesson 3 — Four pathology families: signatures and root causes

Four bugs, one signature table. Everything in this table comes from this module's five real reference training runs — the same architecture (a 4-layer MLP with `BatchNorm1d` after each hidden `Linear`, trained with `BCEWithLogitsLoss` and momentum-SGD, on a two-class interleaved-spiral dataset), the same eight epochs, differing by exactly one planted defect each. You'll work with the full logs in Lab M07-A; this lesson is the reference key that makes them legible.

| Family | Loss signature | Grad-norm signature | Activation-stat signature | Typical root cause |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Exploding loss** | Val loss grows and swings by large ratios epoch to epoch (`2.26 → 20.75 → 8.62 → 38.44` is this module's actual `B_exploding` run); sometimes ends in `nan` | Norms spike unpredictably, sometimes 5–10× between adjacent epochs, with no stable trend | Post-BN std drifts steadily away from 1.0 as training proceeds (`bn3_sd`: `1.000 → 0.982 → 0.911 → 0.882 → 1.195 → 2.136 → 3.069 → 3.985`) | Learning rate too high for the loss landscape's local curvature; missing or insufficient gradient clipping; an initialization scale that's too large for the depth of the network (Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio, 2013) |
| **Silent data bug** | Train loss plateaus at `ln(num_classes)` (`0.6931` for binary) and barely moves from its own near-chance starting value | Completely ordinary — same shape and magnitude as a healthy run's grad norms, layer for layer | Completely ordinary — BN stats pinned at `(0, 1)`, exactly as specified | Labels and features paired wrong: a shuffle, a roll, an off-by-one join, a stale cached split, the wrong file loaded for one of the two arrays |
| **Frozen-gradient layer** | Loss still improves — other layers compensate — but plateaus higher and slower than the healthy reference (`0.7045 → 0.6623` vs. healthy's `0.7045 → 0.6498` over the same 8 epochs) | One or more parameter groups report `grad = None` on *every* step, while the rest of the network trains normally | Usually ordinary, unless the frozen layer sat upstream of one that specifically needed to adapt | `requires_grad=False` left over from a fine-tuning setup that was never unfrozen; a stray `.detach()`; an optimizer built from a parameter list that silently omitted a layer |
| **Normalization mistake** | Loss nearly flat almost immediately (`0.6925 → 0.6906` over 8 full epochs — the healthy run (`A_healthy`, `0.7045 → 0.6498`) drops roughly 29 times further in the same span); train and val loss track each other closely, which itself is a clue this isn't overfitting | Globally *smaller* than healthy, not larger — the wrong statistics distort the effective step size the optimizer takes | Post-BN mean/std sit far from `(0, 1)` starting at epoch 0, and *stay* there rather than converging toward it (`bn3_sd` pinned near `0.153` for all 8 epochs, never trending toward `1.0`) | `model.eval()` called and never switched back to `.train()` before further training steps (BatchNorm then uses its untouched, default-initialized running statistics instead of the live batch's); wrong `momentum`/`eps`; normalizing over the wrong axis (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) |

Two of these four rows deserve a second pass, because the mechanism behind the number is where the actual understanding lives.

**Why the silent-data-bug loss floor is exactly `ln(2)`, and why the grad norms look fine.** Cross-entropy loss for a binary label `y` and predicted probability `p` is `-[y·log(p) + (1-y)·log(1-p)]`. If the labels carry zero information about the inputs — because they've been shuffled, rolled, or joined against the wrong rows — the best a gradient-following optimizer can do is learn the marginal label frequency and output a constant probability near `0.5` for every input, for which the loss is exactly `-log(0.5) = ln(2) ≈ 0.6931`. Nothing about that optimization process is malfunctioning: the forward pass computes a real loss on real (if uninformative) inputs, `.backward()` computes a real gradient of that real loss, and the optimizer takes a real step downhill. Every mechanical piece of the training loop the previous two lessons taught you to check is doing exactly what it's supposed to. The bug isn't in the mechanics; it's one layer up, in what the two arrays being fed to the loss actually mean relative to each other — which is exactly why this is the family the mechanical diagnostics (grad norms, activation stats) are least equipped to catch, and why the loss-curve floor value is the load-bearing signal for this one family specifically.

**Why the normalization-mistake run's `bn1_sd` sits at `0.712`, not some more dramatic-looking number.** `nn.BatchNorm1d` maintains two separate sets of statistics: the live batch statistics it computes fresh on every call in training mode, and a running average (`running_mean`, `running_var`) it only *updates* in training mode and only *uses* in eval mode. A model stuck in `.eval()` for the entire run never updates that running average past its default initialization (`mean=0, var=1`), and normalizes every batch against those frozen, never-touched defaults instead of that batch's real statistics. The result isn't an error — `(x - 0) / sqrt(1 + eps)` is a perfectly well-defined computation — it's just not *normalization* of this particular layer's actual activation distribution, which happens to have a real standard deviation of about `0.712` relative to what the frozen default assumes. The output isn't garbage and isn't NaN; it's quietly the wrong number, produced by a completely valid tensor operation, which is the entire reason this family is the hardest of the four to catch by staring at outputs instead of checking the invariant directly.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"If the network reaches decent accuracy, the run wasn't pathological."** `D_frozen_layer`'s three live layers compensate well enough to reach `55%` accuracy on a task the healthy reference reaches `54%` on over the same eight epochs — genuinely comparable numbers, from a network missing an entire layer's worth of learning capacity. "Shippable accuracy" and "correctly wired" are different claims, and a network can clear the first bar while failing the second by a wide margin your accuracy metric will never surface.
- **"BatchNorm bugs always show up as huge numeric blowups, the same way exploding gradients do."** The normalization-mistake run's headline symptom is a training curve that's nearly *flat* — the opposite of dramatic. Most learners' first instinct on seeing a stalled-but-stable loss curve is "this is just slow, maybe it needs more epochs or a bigger learning rate," which is exactly backwards for this family and will not fix it no matter how long you wait.
- **"A frozen layer means the network can't learn anything."** `D_frozen_layer`'s train loss drops by `0.042` over eight epochs — real, if reduced, learning — because `fc1`, `fc3`, and `fc4` remain fully trainable and partially compensate for the layer that can't move. The tell isn't "the network learns nothing." It's the one column that reports `None` while every other column reports a number.

### Lesson 4 — The debugging loop for someone else's code: reproduce, isolate, hypothesize, verify

AIE-100/AIE-101 built reproduce → isolate → hypothesize → verify into you as a general debugging discipline. This lesson is that same loop, specialized for a training run whose source you have not yet seen — which changes what "isolate" means in a way that trips up almost everyone the first time.

**Reproduce.** Rerun with the exact seed and exact configuration and confirm you get the *same* curve, not a similar-looking one. This is the same determinism discipline M01's gradcheck depended on — a training run with any unpinned randomness (data shuffling, weight init, dropout) is not reproducible enough to debug, because you can't tell a genuine anomaly from ordinary run-to-run noise. If you can't reproduce the exact numbers, fix that first; everything downstream depends on it.

**Isolate.** This is the step that gets done wrong most often, and the way it gets done wrong is: opening the model's source and reading it top to bottom, looking for something that looks off. Don't. Isolate against the *diagnostic signal* Lessons 2–3 gave you — which layer's grad-norm column is anomalous, which epoch first shows an activation-stat departure from its invariant, whether the anomaly is present at epoch 0 (structural — present from the very first forward pass) or only emerges later (dynamical — something that compounds over training steps). This narrows the search space *before* you've read a single line of source, and it tells you *where in the source* to start reading once you do — which is a categorically faster search than reading top to bottom hoping something jumps out.

**Hypothesize.** Map what you isolated onto Lesson 3's table and name the specific family, plus the general mechanism you'd expect to produce it — not "something's wrong with the gradients," but "a `None` in exactly one grad-norm column, present from epoch 0, most consistent with `requires_grad=False` left set on that layer, or a `.detach()` upstream of it."

**Verify — before opening source.** Design one check that would *fail* if your hypothesis is correct and *pass* if it's wrong. This is the step most learners skip, because it feels like you're just about to get the answer for free by reading the code, so why bother — but a check designed and run *before* you've seen the source is evidence your hypothesis was actually falsifiable, not evidence you found a pattern that happened to match after you already knew the answer.

**Worked example, end to end, on `E_normalization_mistake`.**

1. *Reproduce.* Rerun with the pinned seed; confirm the eight-epoch log matches this module's reference exactly, to four decimal places.
2. *Isolate.* Scan the log column by column. `gn_fc1` through `gn_fc4` are all present and nonzero — no `None`, ruling out a frozen layer immediately. `bn1_sd` and `bn3_sd` are *already* far from `1.0` at epoch 0 (`0.7118` and `0.1531`) — the earliest possible epoch, meaning this is a structural anomaly present from the very first forward pass, not something that develops over training. That timing — anomalous from step zero, not compounding — rules out exploding loss (which starts clean and drifts) and rules out the silent-data-bug family (whose BN stats stay perfectly pinned at `(0,1)` the entire run, since that bug never touches the model or its normalization at all).
3. *Hypothesize.* Post-BN activation statistics wrong from the first forward pass, with grad flow otherwise intact, matches the normalization-mistake row specifically: BatchNorm is computing *a* valid normalization, just against the wrong statistics — most likely because the model is in `.eval()` mode during what should be a training step, so it's using its untouched, default-initialized running stats instead of the batch's real ones.
4. *Verify, before opening source.* Write one targeted check: assert `model.training is True` at the exact point `loss.backward()` is called inside the training loop. This check would fail under the "stuck in eval" hypothesis and pass under every other hypothesis in Lesson 3's table — including a plain hyperparameter mistake, which would leave `model.training` untouched. It requires instrumenting the *running* loop, not reading the model's `forward()` definition at all.

Only after that check comes back failing (confirming the hypothesis) do you open source to find the exact line — which, once you know what you're looking for, is usually seconds, not minutes.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"Isolating the bug means opening the model source and reading it line by line."** That is the single habit this module exists to break. Isolating happens against the diagnostic signal first — Lessons 2 and 3 built the instruments specifically so you'd have somewhere else to look. Reading source is the *last* step of the loop (folded into Locate, in the Labs and Assessment below), not a replacement for the first three.
- **"One diagnostic result that's consistent with your hypothesis is enough to confirm it."** Consistency isn't sufficiency. "The loss went up" is consistent with exploding loss *and* with an unrelated, mild, LR-too-high-but-still-eventually-converging run that would resolve on its own with more epochs — a check that only confirms consistency with your favored hypothesis, without also being capable of contradicting it, hasn't actually tested anything. The worked example's Verify step was chosen specifically because it *fails* under the hypothesis and *passes* under every alternative in the table — the same specificity requirement M06's rubric demanded of a "targeted test," restated for training runs.
- **"Lowering the learning rate until the curve looks smoother counts as isolating an exploding-loss bug."** This is the exact anti-pattern M06's CLAUDE.md-style rubric named directly: a fix-shaped action taken before anything was located, that happens to reduce the symptom without ever confirming — or even stating — a mechanism. It is reachable by luck on an exploding-loss run and will do nothing at all for the other three families, which is precisely why "isolate" has to happen before "fix," not be skipped in favor of it.

```python
# architecture.py — the model and task every log in this module was trained on
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def make_spirals(n=800, noise=0.15, seed=0):
    """Two interleaved spirals, one per class -- a 2D task simple enough to
    plot, hard enough that a linear model can't solve it, matching M03's
    ring-vs-cross choice of 'small, cleanly separable, nothing exotic
    required' so any failure to learn it is on the training run, not the task."""
    rng = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    n_per = n // 2
    t = np.linspace(0.3, 4 * np.pi, n_per)
    r = t
    x1 = np.stack([r * np.cos(t), r * np.sin(t)], axis=1)
    x2 = np.stack([-r * np.cos(t), -r * np.sin(t)], axis=1)
    X = np.concatenate([x1, x2], axis=0) + rng.randn(n_per * 2, 2) * noise
    y = np.concatenate([np.zeros(n_per), np.ones(n_per)])
    idx = rng.permutation(len(X))
    X, y = X[idx], y[idx]
    X = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
    return X.astype(np.float32), y.astype(np.float32)

def load_spiral_data():
    """Train split (seed=0) and validation split (seed=1) -- two
    independently generated draws from the same distribution, not a
    held-out slice of one draw, so a data-pairing bug in the train split
    doesn't accidentally also corrupt validation."""
    Xtr, ytr = make_spirals(800, seed=0)
    Xval, yval = make_spirals(200, seed=1)
    return (torch.tensor(Xtr), torch.tensor(ytr),
            torch.tensor(Xval), torch.tensor(yval))

class SpiralMLP(nn.Module):
    """fc1(2->32) -> bn1 -> relu -> fc2(32->32) -> bn2 -> relu ->
    fc3(32->16) -> bn3 -> relu -> fc4(16->1). This is the exact architecture
    behind every log in this module -- the same four gn_fc* columns, the
    same bn1/bn3 activation-stat columns, in every lab and the Assessment."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(2, 32);  self.bn1 = nn.BatchNorm1d(32)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(32, 32); self.bn2 = nn.BatchNorm1d(32)
        self.fc3 = nn.Linear(32, 16); self.bn3 = nn.BatchNorm1d(16)
        self.fc4 = nn.Linear(16, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = torch.relu(self.bn1(self.fc1(x)))
        x = torch.relu(self.bn2(self.fc2(x)))
        x = torch.relu(self.bn3(self.fc3(x)))
        return self.fc4(x).squeeze(-1)
```

### Lab M07-A — Diagnose from curves alone

*Predict (on paper, before touching code).* You are given five complete eight-epoch training logs below — `train_loss`, `val_loss`, `val_acc`, four per-layer gradient norms, and four `BatchNorm` activation statistics, per epoch. You are given **no source code** for any of the five runs. For each of the five, write down: (1) which of `exploding`, `silent_data_bug`, `frozen_layer`, `normalization_mistake`, or `healthy` you believe it is; (2) the single earliest column and epoch where the log first shows evidence that rules out at least two of the other four possibilities — not just evidence "consistent with" your answer, evidence that actively excludes alternatives, per Lesson 4's Verify discipline.


```python
# lab_a_solution.py
import math

def classify_run(log: list[dict], num_classes: int = 2) -> str:
    grad_cols = ["gn_fc1", "gn_fc2", "gn_fc3", "gn_fc4"]

    for col in grad_cols:
        if all(row[col] is None for row in log):
            return "frozen_layer"

    bn_sd_off = [abs(row["bn3_sd"] - 1.0) > 0.2 for row in log]
    if sum(bn_sd_off) >= len(log) - 1:
        return "normalization_mistake"

    half = len(log) // 2
    first_vloss = log[0]["val_loss"]
    back_half_vloss = [row["val_loss"] for row in log[half:]]
    back_mean = sum(back_half_vloss) / len(back_half_vloss)
    front_min = min(r["val_loss"] for r in log[:half] if r["val_loss"] > 0)
    vloss_ratio = max(back_half_vloss) / front_min
    if back_mean > 3 * first_vloss or vloss_ratio > 5:
        return "exploding"

    chance_loss = math.log(num_classes)
    last_train_loss = log[-1]["train_loss"]
    last_val_acc = log[-1]["val_acc"]
    train_loss_drop = log[0]["train_loss"] - log[-1]["train_loss"]
    if (abs(last_train_loss - chance_loss) < 0.05
            and abs(last_val_acc - 1.0 / num_classes) < 0.08
            and train_loss_drop < 0.03):
        return "silent_data_bug"

    return "healthy"
```

```python
# lab_b_solution.py
import torch
from diagnostics import Diagnostics

class RecordingDiagnostics(Diagnostics):
    def __init__(self, activation_layers: dict, grad_params: dict):
        super().__init__(activation_layers, grad_params)
        self.history: list[dict] = []

    def step(self, epoch: int):
        row = {"epoch": epoch, **self.snapshot()}
        self.history.append(row)
        return row
```

```python
# test_lab_b.py
import math

import torch.nn as nn
from lab_b_solution import RecordingDiagnostics

torch.manual_seed(0)

class TinyMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(4, 8); self.bn1 = nn.BatchNorm1d(8)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(8, 1)
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.fc2(torch.relu(self.bn1(self.fc1(x))))

    model = TinyMLP()
    diag = RecordingDiagnostics(
        activation_layers={"bn1": model.bn1},
        grad_params={"fc1": model.fc1.weight, "fc2": model.fc2.weight},
    )
    x = torch.randn(16, 4)
    y = torch.randn(16, 1)
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    row = diag.step(epoch=0)
    manual_norm = model.fc1.weight.grad.norm().item()
    assert math.isclose(row["gn_fc1"], manual_norm, rel_tol=1e-9)

    model = TinyMLP()
    model.fc2.weight.requires_grad_(False)
    diag = RecordingDiagnostics(
        activation_layers={"bn1": model.bn1},
        grad_params={"fc1": model.fc1.weight, "fc2": model.fc2.weight},
    )
    x = torch.randn(16, 4)
    y = torch.randn(16, 1)
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    row = diag.step(epoch=0)

        model.zero_grad()
```

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def _train_n_epochs(model, opt, lossfn, Xtr, ytr, n=8, force_train_mode=True):
    log = []
    for epoch in range(n):
        if force_train_mode:
            model.train()
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = lossfn(model(Xtr), ytr)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        log.append(loss.item())

    # computed in train() mode has std~1 -- that will PASS even on the
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — what it means to have proven a training run is *mechanically* sound, and what that proof does and doesn't tell you about the model it produces.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Every failure family this module built is a documented, named, widely-replicated phenomenon in the literature, not a pedagogical invention. Exploding (and vanishing) gradients in deep and recurrent networks, along with gradient clipping as the standard mitigation, are characterized directly in Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio's "On the difficulty of training recurrent neural networks" (ICML 2013). BatchNorm's dependence on train/eval-mode statistics — and the specific failure mode this module's normalization-mistake family plants — follows directly from the mechanism specified in Ioffe & Szegedy's original Batch Normalization paper (ICML 2015): the layer's contract is a claim about *training-time* batch statistics, and eval-mode substitutes a different, separately-tracked statistic by design, which is exactly the substitution this module's bug exploits by accident. Silent data-pairing errors — the family Lab M07-A and this module's Assessment both center — are documented at production scale, not just as a toy-scale curiosity: Sambasivan et al.'s "Everyone wants to do the model work, not the data work" (CHI 2021) surveys ML practitioners across industry finding data-quality and data-pipeline defects to be pervasive, systemically under-invested-in, and specifically hard to detect because a data-pairing bug produces a model that *trains successfully* — no crash, no obviously-wrong number, exactly this module's silent-data-bug signature, at real scale, not a constructed classroom example.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "The training run completed without errors, the loss went down, and the tests pass" is treated, in a huge amount of day-to-day ML engineering practice, as sufficient evidence to ship — and this module just spent five labs proving that exact bar is clearable by at least three of its four broken families. A frozen layer clears it. A silent data bug clears it, and clears a "loss went down" bar too, provided nobody checks *how far* down or *toward what floor*. A model stuck normalizing against stale statistics for its first several epochs, if the bug is later fixed mid-training-run (a very real production pattern — a config change deployed between checkpoints) can clear it while still having learned something quietly worse than intended for the epochs it ran broken. The field's own postmortem culture — incident reviews, "what we learned" retrospectives from ML teams at companies large and small — repeatedly documents this same category of failure being caught only after a downstream harm surfaced, not during training, because training-time monitoring across the industry disproportionately watches the single number (loss, or accuracy) that this module's four families were specifically chosen to demonstrate is the least discriminating signal available. The gap isn't that the diagnostics in this module are secret or hard to build — you built all of them, in under twenty hours, from a stated specification. The gap is that watching them is not yet the default, and "it trained, the number went down" keeps functioning as a social permission slip to stop looking, in exactly the way this module's own rubrics were built to refuse.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You can now take a training run and prove, mechanically, that it is sound — every layer receives a real gradient, every normalization layer's invariant holds, the loss is heading somewhere other than a chance-level floor for a real reason. That is a genuine, checkable, gradcheck-grade claim, and everything in this module was built to let you make it honestly instead of by vibes. Here is what that proof does not cover, and does not get smaller as you get better at this: a training run can pass every diagnostic in this module — clean grad norms, clean activation statistics, a loss curve that heads decisively away from chance for a real, checkable reason — and still learn a function that does something in deployment nobody who watched those diagnostics predicted, wanted, or would have signed off on if they'd seen it coming. Mechanical soundness is a claim about *how* the model got where it's going. It is not a claim about *where that turned out to be*. M06 left you with a question about architecture and behavior; this module hands you the tools to prove the training *process* was correct, and deliberately does not extend those tools to prove the training *outcome* was the one you wanted. So: once you can show, on a diff, on a log, on a gradcheck report, that nothing was wrong with how a model was trained — at what point does a surprising, unwanted capability stop being a bug you failed to catch, and start being simply what a correctly-run training process, on real data, with real labels, was always going to produce? You do not get to answer this by building a sixth diagnostic. Sit with why not.

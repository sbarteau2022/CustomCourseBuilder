## Module overview

Every module before this one had a `y`. Linear regression (M02) and trees (M03) were both bets
that minimizing error against a *known, labeled* target would generalize. This module takes the
labels away. K-means and PCA never see a `y` — they only ever see `X` — which means the question
"did it work?" stops being "did it predict the label correctly" and becomes something harder to
pin down: "did it find real structure, or did it find *a* structure, confidently, whether or not
one was there?" That question is the spine of this module, and it does not go away once you can
call `KMeans().fit(X)`. It gets easier to ignore.

You will build two algorithms from raw NumPy, each reusing machinery you already have. **K-means**
(Lloyd's algorithm) is an alternating minimization of within-cluster sum of squared distances —
the assignment step and the update step are each, individually, an exact minimization you can
derive with calculus you already know from M02's normal equations. **PCA** is eigendecomposition
of a covariance matrix, which is the *same* symmetric-matrix machinery AIE-103-M02 built for the
SVD, pointed at a new object (the covariance of centered data instead of a raw data matrix). By
the end of this module you will watch k-means converge, iteration by iteration, on a 2D toy set
you can plot by hand first and verify in code second. You will predict how many principal
components explain 95% of a dataset's variance *before* you compute the scree plot that confirms
it — because if you understand what PCA is measuring, that number is not a mystery you wait to
be told, it is a claim you can defend in advance and be wrong about, which is the entire AI-PRIMM
loop this course has run since M01.

The payoff this module is built around: **PCA's low-rank approximation is the exact mechanical
ancestor of the word "embedding."** AIE-103-M02 taught you that the rows of `U_k Σ_k` from a
truncated SVD are vectors placing similar rows near each other in a learned space. PCA is that
same object, computed from the covariance eigenvectors instead of the raw SVD, with one added
ingredient — centering — that changes what "similar" means from "similar in raw magnitude" to
"similar in how they vary from the average." Starting in AIE-202 and AIE-301, `model.encode(x)`
will hand you a vector and call it an embedding, produced by a wildly more powerful nonlinear
procedure than anything in this module. But it is still, structurally, a vector per item in a
space built by keeping the directions with a lot of signal and discarding the directions with
little — and after this module, you will know that claim is checkable, because you built the
linear version of it yourself and watched the reconstruction error land exactly where the dropped
eigenvalues said it would.

The descent: define what k-means is actually minimizing before running it (Lesson 1), watch that
minimization fail in specific, predictable ways (Lesson 2), reuse AIE-103-M02's eigenvector
machinery on a new matrix (Lesson 3), and turn "how many components do I keep" from a guess into
a prediction you check (Lesson 4). Re-ascent: you can read `PCA(n_components=0.95)` and know
structurally what condition it is solving for, and you can read a customer-segmentation deck's
"we found 4 natural clusters" and know to ask what the inertia curve looked like at k=3 and k=5
before you believe the 4.

### Lesson 1 — K-means as an optimization problem: Lloyd's algorithm and the objective it actually minimizes

**Teaching text**

Start with the object being minimized, because "k-means groups similar points" is exactly the
kind of informal sentence M01 warned you about. Given data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ and a
target cluster count $k$, k-means searches for cluster assignments $c_i \in \{1,\dots,k\}$ and
centroids $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k$ that minimize the **within-cluster sum of squared distances**
(also called **inertia**):

$$J(c, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lVert x_i - \mu_{c_i} \rVert_2^2$$

This is the only thing k-means is trying to do. It is not trying to find "natural groups," it is
not trying to match any ground truth (there is none — this is unsupervised, there is no `y` to
check against), and it has no way of knowing whether $J$ being small means the clusters are
*meaningful*. It only knows how to make $J$ small. Holding that distinction — the objective
function versus what a human wants the objective function to mean — is the discipline this whole
module is testing.

$J$ is jointly non-convex in $(c, \mu)$ and minimizing it exactly is NP-hard. **Lloyd's algorithm**
does not solve it exactly; it alternates between two sub-problems that *are* each exactly
solvable, and repeats until neither improves:

1. **Assignment step** (hold $\mu$ fixed, minimize over $c$): for each point, the assignment that
   minimizes its own squared distance to *some* centroid is obviously "assign it to the nearest
   centroid" — this is a per-point, independent, exact minimization with no approximation in it.
2. **Update step** (hold $c$ fixed, minimize over $\mu$): for cluster $j$, minimize
   $\sum_{i: c_i = j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2$ over $\mu_j$. Take the derivative and set it to
   zero — **the same move as M02 Lesson 1's normal equations**:
   $$\frac{d}{d\mu_j}\sum_{i:c_i=j}\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2 = -2\sum_{i:c_i=j}(x_i-\mu_j) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mu_j = \frac{1}{|\{i:c_i=j\}|}\sum_{i:c_i=j}x_i$$
   The centroid that minimizes squared distance to its assigned points is exactly their mean.
   This is not a heuristic choice of "use the average as the center" — it is the closed-form
   solution to an exact sub-problem, arrived at the identical way you derived OLS's coefficients.

Because each step is an *exact* minimization of $J$ over its half of the variables with the other
half fixed, $J$ can never increase from one step to the next — it is monotonically non-increasing,
bounded below by zero, and therefore guaranteed to converge to a fixed point in a finite number of
steps (there are only finitely many ways to partition $n$ points into $k$ groups, so the sequence
of assignments cannot cycle without repeating, and $J$ strictly decreases at every assignment
change). What that guarantee does **not** say is which fixed point — that is Lesson 2's subject,
in full.

**Worked example — six points, converging by hand from a deliberately bad start**

$$P_1=(1,1),\; P_2=(1,2),\; P_3=(2,1),\; P_4=(8,8),\; P_5=(9,9),\; P_6=(9,8)$$

Two true clusters are visually obvious: $\{P_1,P_2,P_3\}$ near the origin, $\{P_4,P_5,P_6\}$ far
away. Initialize deliberately badly: $\mu_1 = P_1 = (1,1)$, $\mu_2 = P_2 = (1,2)$ — **both seeds
drawn from the same true cluster**, a case Lesson 2 will show is not always this forgiving.

*Iteration 1 — assignment.* Squared distances to $(\mu_1,\mu_2)$:

| Point | to $\mu_1=(1,1)$ | to $\mu_2=(1,2)$ | assigned |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| $P_1$ | 0 | 1 | $\mu_1$ |
| $P_2$ | 1 | 0 | $\mu_2$ |
| $P_3$ | 1 | 2 | $\mu_1$ |
| $P_4$ | 98 | 85 | $\mu_2$ |
| $P_5$ | 128 | 113 | $\mu_2$ |
| $P_6$ | 113 | 100 | $\mu_2$ |

Update: $\mu_1 = \text{mean}(P_1,P_3) = (1.5, 1)$, $\mu_2 = \text{mean}(P_2,P_4,P_5,P_6) = (6.75, 6.75)$.
$J$ under the *pre-update* centroids (the assignment cost that produced this update) is
$0+0+1+85+113+100 = 299.0$.

*Iteration 2 — assignment.* Squared distances to $(\mu_1,\mu_2)=((1.5,1),(6.75,6.75))$:

| Point | to $\mu_1$ | to $\mu_2$ | assigned |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| $P_1$ | 0.25 | 66.125 | $\mu_1$ |
| $P_2$ | 1.25 | 55.625 | $\mu_1$ |
| $P_3$ | 0.25 | 55.625 | $\mu_1$ |
| $P_4$ | 91.25 | 3.125 | $\mu_2$ |
| $P_5$ | 120.25 | 10.125 | $\mu_2$ |
| $P_6$ | 105.25 | 6.625 | $\mu_2$ |

The clusters have now snapped to the correct partition — $\{P_1,P_2,P_3\}$ and $\{P_4,P_5,P_6\}$
— **despite starting with two centroids drawn from the same true cluster.** $J$ at this step is
$0.25+1.25+0.25+3.125+10.125+6.625 = 21.625$, already far below 299.0. Update:
$\mu_1=(4/3,4/3)\approx(1.333,1.333)$, $\mu_2=(26/3,25/3)\approx(8.667,8.333)$.

*Iteration 3 — assignment.* Every point's nearest centroid is unchanged from iteration 2 (check
$P_1$: $0.222 < 112.6$; check $P_6$: $0.222 < 103.2$; the margins are now enormous). Assignments
are identical to the previous step, so the update step reproduces the same centroids — **Lloyd's
algorithm has converged.** Final inertia:
$0.222+0.556+0.556+0.556+0.556+0.222 = 2.667$ (exactly $8/3$).

$J$ fell $299.0 \to 21.625 \to 2.667$ — monotonically, exactly as the derivation promised — and
converged in two assignment steps. This particular bad initialization recovered the correct
partition; do not generalize from that yet. Lesson 2 shows the same mechanism land somewhere
wrong roughly 30% of the time under a worse kind of bad luck, on a real dataset, with real
numbers.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"K-means finds the clusters that are really there."** K-means finds the partition into $k$
  groups that minimizes $J$ — a specific, spherical, equal-weighted notion of "compact group." If
  the real structure in the data isn't shaped like $k$ roughly round, roughly equal-density blobs,
  k-means will still confidently return $k$ round-ish partitions; it has no mechanism for
  reporting "these don't look like real clusters." Lesson 2's elongated-cluster example makes this
  concrete and measurable, not just a caveat.
- **"Monotonically decreasing $J$ means k-means converges to the global minimum."** Monotone
  non-increase guarantees convergence to *a* fixed point of the alternating scheme — a *local*
  minimum of $J$ with respect to single-point reassignments and centroid recomputation. The
  overall problem is NP-hard; nothing about Lloyd's algorithm's convergence proof touches
  *global* optimality. This is the single most consequential misconception in the module, and
  Lesson 2 is built specifically to break it with numbers.
- **"The centroid update `mean(cluster points)` is just an intuitive summary statistic."** It is
  the exact closed-form minimizer of squared distance for that sub-problem, derived by the
  identical calculus as M02's OLS coefficients. It is not a design choice among several
  reasonable options — change the distance metric (e.g., to L1) and the optimal "center" is no
  longer the mean, it's the coordinate-wise median (k-medians), which is a genuinely different
  algorithm with a genuinely different update rule, not a synonym.
- **"An empty cluster (zero points assigned) is a bug in my implementation."** It's a real,
  expected edge case: if no point is closer to centroid $j$ than to every other centroid, $j$'s
  mean is undefined by the formula above. Production implementations handle this explicitly
  (commonly: freeze the centroid in place, or reseed it at the point with the largest current
  assignment cost) — Lab 1's starter code requires you to make this choice deliberately rather
  than let a silent `NaN` decide it for you.

### Lesson 2 — Initialization, k-means++, and choosing k: where k-means quietly fails

**Teaching text**

Lesson 1 proved $J$ decreases monotonically to *a* fixed point. This lesson shows, with numbers,
that "a fixed point" can be a bad one, how often, and what to do about it — plus a second,
structurally different failure that no initialization strategy fixes.

**Failure 1: bad initialization finds a bad local optimum.** Random initialization — pick $k$
data points uniformly at random as starting centroids — can place two starting centroids in the
same true cluster and none in another, the same setup Lesson 1's hand example survived. It does
not always survive. On a 300-point, 3-cluster synthetic dataset (three roughly-round blobs
centered at $(-4,-2)$, $(4,4)$, $(0,-6)$, generated with `rng.normal(loc=center, scale=0.9)`), a
sweep of 10 different random-init seeds against 10 different **k-means++** seeds gives:

| Seed | Random init inertia | Random init result | k-means++ inertia | k-means++ result |
|---:|---:|---|---:|---|
| 0 | 2145.36 | **bad local optimum** | 517.07 | correct partition |
| 1 | 2133.84 | **bad local optimum** | 517.07 | correct partition |
| 2 | 517.07 | correct partition | 517.07 | correct partition |
| 3 | 517.07 | correct partition | 2133.45 | **bad local optimum** |
| 4–9 | 517.07 (all six) | correct partition | 517.07 (all six) | correct partition |

Random init lands in the bad optimum (inertia ≈ 2135–2145, roughly **4× worse** than the correct
partition's 517.07) on **3 of 10 seeds (30%)**. **K-means++** — the seeding strategy that picks
each new centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest
already-chosen centroid, actively spreading the initial guesses apart instead of leaving it to
chance — lands in the bad optimum on **1 of 10 seeds (10%)**. K-means++ does not eliminate the
risk (seed 3 fails under k-means++ too); it changes the odds, and by a lot, at essentially the
same computational cost as random init. This is why `sklearn.cluster.KMeans`'s default is
`init="k-means++"`, not `"random"` — and why production code almost always runs several
independent initializations (`n_init`) and keeps the lowest-inertia result, rather than trusting
any single run.

**Failure 2: the isotropic-cluster assumption, which no initialization strategy fixes.** Because
$J$ measures *squared Euclidean distance to a centroid*, k-means implicitly assumes clusters are
roughly round (isotropic) and comparably sized — its notion of "compact" is a ball around a
center. Two long, thin, parallel, diagonally-oriented clusters positioned close together (each
generated as a 1D Gaussian smeared along a rotated axis, the short axis nearly noiseless) are a
textbook violation: a human looking at a scatter plot sees two obvious elongated groups, but
k-means, evaluated against the true generating label, achieves only **63.3% agreement** — barely
better than assigning points by a coin flip weighted toward the majority group. This is not a
convergence failure; k-means converges cleanly and confidently to a wrong-shaped partition,
because a ball-shaped notion of "cluster" cannot represent an elongated one no matter how good the
starting centroids are. **Better initialization cannot fix a wrong assumption about cluster
shape** — that is the load-bearing distinction between the two failures in this lesson.

**Choosing $k$ without a $y$ to check against.** Unlike M02–M03, there is no held-out label
accuracy to validate against. The standard heuristic is the **elbow method**: plot inertia against
$k$ and look for the point where adding another cluster stops buying much reduction. On the
3-blob dataset:

| $k$ | inertia (best of 5 k-means++ restarts) | drop from previous $k$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 8821.77 | — |
| 2 | 2189.38 | 6632.39 |
| 3 | 517.07 | **1672.31** |
| 4 | 452.89 | 64.18 |
| 5 | 387.62 | 65.27 |
| 6 | 331.69 | 55.93 |

The drop from $k{=}2\to3$ (1672.31) dwarfs every subsequent drop (64–65 each) — a sharp elbow at
$k=3$, matching the true generating structure. This *worked* here because the true structure is
three well-separated, roughly-round clusters. The elbow method is a heuristic reading of a curve,
not a proof of correctness, and Lesson 4's ethics tier returns to exactly this point: "the elbow
says 3" is a claim about where a slope changes, not a claim about how many groups of people
actually exist.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"K-means++ guarantees the global optimum."** It reduces the *probability* of landing in a bad
  local optimum (30% → 10% in the sweep above); it does not eliminate it, as seed 3 shows even
  under k-means++. "Better odds" and "guaranteed" are different claims, and only one of them is
  true here.
- **"If k-means doesn't find the right shape of cluster, it just does a slightly worse job — it
  degrades gracefully."** 63.3% agreement on the elongated-cluster data is not "slightly worse" —
  it is close to arbitrary, and k-means reports it with exactly the same unworried confidence
  (clean convergence, a perfectly well-defined inertia value) as a case where it's 100% correct.
  Nothing in the algorithm's output signals "this dataset's shape doesn't fit my assumptions."
- **"The elbow method finds the 'true' number of clusters."** It finds where the inertia-vs-$k$
  curve bends, under a specific reader's judgment of what counts as a bend. Real data rarely
  produces as clean an elbow as three well-separated synthetic blobs; a data analyst deciding
  "the elbow is at $k{=}4$" versus "the elbow is at $k{=}5$" is making a judgment call the curve
  does not make for them, and different analysts looking at the same curve can and do disagree.
- **"Running k-means once with a fixed random seed is reproducible, so it's reliable."** It is
  reproducible (same seed, same result, always) — that is not the same claim as reliable (same
  result as a *different* seed would give, or the same result as the "correct" partition). A
  single fixed seed can silently be seed 0 or seed 1 from the table above.

### Lesson 3 — PCA via eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix

**Teaching text**

AIE-103-M02 Lesson 3 built the SVD of a raw data matrix $A$: form $A^TA$, find its eigenvectors,
and read off the axes $A$ cares about most. This lesson reuses that *exact* machinery — same
symmetric matrix, same eigenvector-eigenvalue mechanics — pointed at a different object, with one
change that matters more than it looks: **PCA operates on centered data.**

Given $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, first subtract the column means: $X_c = X - \bar{x}$, where
$\bar{x}$ is the $p$-dimensional mean vector broadcast across all $n$ rows. The **sample
covariance matrix** is:

$$C = \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^TX_c \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$$

This is the same shape of object as AIE-103-M02's $A^TA$ (both are $p \times p$ and symmetric),
and the same guarantees apply: $C$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, so its eigenvalues are
real and non-negative and its eigenvectors are orthogonal — the exact fact AIE-103-M02 used to
guarantee the SVD's right singular vectors were a clean orthonormal basis. Solve
$Cv_j = \lambda_j v_j$, sort by $\lambda_j$ descending, and the eigenvectors $v_1, v_2, \dots$ are
the **principal components** — the orthogonal axes along which the (centered) data varies the
most, the second-most, and so on. The **explained variance ratio** of component $j$ is
$\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i$: exactly the same "fraction of total energy" framing AIE-103-M02
used for singular values, because it is the same quantity under a different name — Lesson 4 makes
that identity explicit.

**Why centering is not optional.** Skip it, and the "principal component" of the raw, uncentered
data is dominated by *where the data sits relative to the origin*, not by how it *varies*. A cloud
of points tightly clustered far from the origin has almost all of its raw sum-of-squares explained
by "distance from $(0,0)$," which tells you nothing about the cloud's internal shape. Centering
removes that location information so the covariance genuinely measures spread, exactly the way M02
never regularized the intercept: the mean (or the intercept) is a location parameter, and location
is not the thing this procedure is trying to describe.

**Worked example — PCA on four points, entirely by hand.**

$$X = \begin{bmatrix}3&4\\4&3\\1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 1 — center.** $\bar{x} = (2.5, 2.5)$.
$$X_c = \begin{bmatrix}0.5&1.5\\1.5&0.5\\-1.5&-0.5\\-0.5&-1.5\end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 2 — covariance** ($n-1=3$):
$$C = \frac{1}{3}X_c^TX_c = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5/3&1\\1&5/3\end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 3 — eigenvalues.** $C$ has the form $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}$ with $a=5/3$,
$b=1$ — the same structural shape as AIE-103-M02's `[[2,1],[1,2]]` example, and it has the same
shortcut: eigenvalues are $a+b$ and $a-b$, with eigenvectors $[1,1]/\sqrt2$ and $[1,-1]/\sqrt2$
respectively (verify: $C[1,1]^T = [a+b,a+b]^T = (a+b)[1,1]^T$).

$$\lambda_1 = 5/3+1 = 8/3 \approx 2.667, \qquad \lambda_2 = 5/3-1 = 2/3 \approx 0.667$$

**Step 4 — explained variance ratio.** Total variance $= \text{tr}(C) = 5/3+5/3 = 10/3$.
$$\text{PC1 ratio} = \frac{8/3}{10/3} = 0.800 \;(80\%), \qquad \text{PC2 ratio} = \frac{2/3}{10/3}=0.200\;(20\%)$$

This makes geometric sense on inspection: the four centered points $(0.5,1.5),(1.5,0.5),
(-1.5,-0.5),(-0.5,-1.5)$ lie almost exactly along the $[1,1]$ diagonal direction, with a much
smaller spread along $[1,-1]$ — PCA found the diagonal, quantitatively, from arithmetic alone.

**Cross-check via SVD.** Following AIE-103-M02 Lesson 3, $X_c$'s singular values are
$\sigma_1=2.828$, $\sigma_2=1.414$, and the identity connecting the two decompositions is
$\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)$: $2.828^2/3 = 8/3 = \lambda_1$ ✓, $1.414^2/3=2/3=\lambda_2$ ✓ —
`np.linalg.eigh(cov)` and `np.linalg.svd(Xc)` are computing the same underlying structure through
two different, exactly reconcilable routes, matching to `1e-10`.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"PCA finds the direction where the data has the biggest raw values."** It finds the direction
  of maximum *variance after centering*. Skipping the centering step doesn't produce "PCA with
  slightly different numbers" — it produces a decomposition dominated by where the data sits
  relative to the origin, not how it spreads, which is a different and usually useless quantity.
  This is the single most common from-scratch PCA bug, and it does not crash — it silently returns
  a plausible-looking wrong answer, the same failure shape as M02's use-after-free.
- **"PCA and SVD are basically the same algorithm, so it doesn't matter which one you run."** They
  compute reconcilable quantities ($\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)$, as shown above) — but only if
  you feed the SVD the *centered* data. Running `np.linalg.svd` on the raw, uncentered $X$ instead
  of $X_c$ silently computes something else entirely: the singular vectors of $X^TX$, not of the
  covariance of $X$. "PCA via SVD" specifically means "SVD of the centered matrix" — the algorithm
  name doesn't carry that requirement, the discipline does.
- **"A principal component's sign is a meaningful part of the answer."** $Cv=\lambda v$ is equally
  satisfied by $v$ and $-v$ — the *axis* PCA finds is meaningful, its arbitrarily-chosen positive
  direction is not. Different implementations (and even the same implementation on a different
  BLAS backend) can and do flip signs; comparing your from-scratch components to `sklearn.decomposition.PCA`'s
  `components_` requires aligning signs first, or every comparison will spuriously "fail."
- **"You can extract as many principal components as you have features, always."** For centered
  data, the covariance matrix's rank is at most $\min(n-1, p)$ — with only $n$ centered points,
  the components live in at most an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace (the centering itself removes one
  degree of freedom), so a dataset with $n=4$ points can have at most 3 genuinely nonzero
  eigenvalues no matter how large $p$ is. This is a real constraint worth checking, not a
  misconception to un-learn, but it surprises learners who expect $p$ components by default.

### Lesson 4 — Low-rank approximation, the predict-before-scree discipline, and PCA as the ancestor of "embedding"

**Teaching text**

AIE-103-M02 Lesson 4 proved the Eckart–Young theorem: truncating an SVD to its top $k$ terms gives
the *provably best* rank-$k$ approximation, with an error you can compute in advance from the
dropped singular values, $\lVert A-A_k\rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2}$. Because
$\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)$ (Lesson 3), that same guarantee restates directly in PCA's language:
truncating to the top $k$ principal components is the best possible rank-$k$ linear approximation
of the centered data, and its reconstruction error is exactly predictable from the eigenvalues you
threw away:

$$\lVert X_c - X_{c,k}\rVert_F = \sqrt{(n-1)\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j}$$

On the four-point worked example, dropping the second component ($\lambda_2=2/3$) predicts
reconstruction error $\sqrt{3\cdot 2/3}=\sqrt{2}\approx1.414$ — and the *measured* Frobenius norm
of the actual reconstruction error is $1.4142135...$, matching to floating-point noise. This is
not a coincidence to marvel at; it is the theorem doing exactly what it says, and it is the reason
this module asks you to **predict $k$ before you look at the scree plot that confirms it** — the
prediction is not a guess, it is a claim about eigenvalues you can defend with a mechanism.

**The predict-before-scree discipline, concretely.** Suppose you're told a dataset has 8 measured
features, believed (from domain knowledge, or from how the data was generated) to be driven by
roughly 3 underlying factors plus per-feature measurement noise. Before computing anything, the
prediction is: *most of the variance should concentrate in about 3 components, because 3 is how
many real directions of variation the data-generating process has* — a per-feature noise term
adds a little variance in every direction, but a shared underlying factor adds correlated variance
along one specific direction, and correlated variance is what PCA's top components chase. Running
the actual eigendecomposition on such a dataset (8 features built from 3 latent factors, moderate
per-feature noise, $n{=}200$) gives:

| $k$ | eigenvalue | explained variance ratio | cumulative |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 14.953 | 60.12% | 60.12% |
| 2 | 6.421 | 25.82% | 85.94% |
| 3 | 2.762 | 11.10% | **97.05%** |
| 4 | 0.182 | 0.73% | 97.78% |
| 5 | 0.158 | 0.64% | 98.41% |
| 6 | 0.139 | 0.56% | 98.97% |
| 7 | 0.134 | 0.54% | 99.51% |
| 8 | 0.122 | 0.49% | 100.00% |

Cumulative variance crosses 95% at **exactly $k=3$** (97.05%), and the eigenvalue table shows a
sharp cliff between component 3 (2.762) and component 4 (0.182) — a 15× drop, versus the gentle
~10–15% decreases among components 4–8. That cliff, not the raw "95%" threshold, is the more
robust signal in real data: it's the point where "structured, shared variance" ends and
"per-feature noise floor" begins, and a learner who predicted "about 3" from the data-generating
story, before ever looking at this table, has demonstrated understanding a learner who only reads
`cumsum(explained_variance_ratio_) >= 0.95` off a plot has not.

**From low-rank approximation to embedding.** Keep the top $k$ components and project each row:
$z_i = X_{c,i} \cdot [v_1,\dots,v_k]$. Each $z_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is a $k$-dimensional coordinate
for example $i$ in the space spanned by the top-$k$ principal axes — this is **exactly** AIE-103-M02
Lesson 4's "the rows of $U_k\Sigma_k$ are an embedding" idea, restated for covariance eigenvectors
instead of raw SVD, with the added property that distances and angles between the $z_i$ now
reflect *deviation from the population average* rather than raw magnitude, because centering built
that property in from Lesson 3. Two rows with similar $z_i$ vectors are similar in the ways the
data varies the most — which is the honest, checkable content behind the sentence "this embedding
places similar items near each other." Starting in AIE-202 (neural embeddings from a trained
network) and AIE-301 (retrieval over those embeddings), the vectors will come from a vastly more
powerful nonlinear procedure trained on vastly more data — but the object is the same *kind* of
object: coordinates in a space built by keeping high-signal directions and discarding low-signal
ones. The word stops being a black box exactly here, because you built the linear special case of
it with your own eigendecomposition and watched its error land where the theorem said it would.

**Two limits worth knowing before you trust this in production.**

*Scale sensitivity.* PCA maximizes *raw* variance, and raw variance is unit-dependent. Combine a
feature measured in meters (height, std $\approx 0.1$) with a feature measured in dollars (income,
std $\approx 15{,}000$) without standardizing first, and PCA reports **explained variance ratio
$[1.000, 0.000]$** with PC1 loading almost entirely on income (loading $\approx 1.0$) and almost
none on height (loading $\approx 10^{-7}$) — not because income is more "important," but because
its raw numeric scale is roughly 100,000× larger. Standardize both features first (zero mean, unit
variance) and the same two genuinely-comparable-importance features split much more evenly:
explained variance ratio $[0.508, 0.492]$, loadings $[0.707, 0.707]$ on PC1 — an almost perfectly
even mix, which is what "comparably important, differently scaled" should actually look like. This
is the exact same lesson as M02's regularization-and-feature-scale warning, reapplied: whenever an
algorithm is defined in terms of "the direction/coefficient of largest magnitude," check whether
your units are doing the deciding before the data is.

*The linearity limit.* PCA can only ever propose *linear* combinations of the original features as
its new axes. Data on a genuinely nonlinear manifold — 200 points sampled around a circle
($\cos\theta,\sin\theta$) plus small noise, whose *true* intrinsic structure is one number (the
angle $\theta$) — splits its variance almost evenly across both linear PCA axes:
**52.6% / 47.4%**, not the lopsided "one dominant component" pattern a genuinely 1-dimensional
structure might lead you to expect. Keeping only PC1 reconstructs the circle as a flattened line,
losing nearly half the structure. PCA didn't fail to find a 1-dimensional pattern because the
pattern wasn't there — it failed because the pattern isn't *linear*, and no amount of eigenvector
math can express "walk around a circle" as "move along a fixed straight direction." This is a real
boundary of the method, not a bug to fix by adding more components — it's the seed of why AIE-202's
neural embeddings, which can learn nonlinear projections, exist at all.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"95% variance explained means I've kept 95% of what matters."** It means you've kept 95% of
  the *linear variance*, measured in whatever units your features happened to be in when you ran
  PCA. Skewed feature scales can inflate one feature's apparent "importance" to the point of
  swallowing 100% of the variance ratio on its own (the height/income example) — the 95% number is
  only meaningful once you've established that the units it's measured in are the ones you
  actually care about.
- **"If PCA can compress my data to $k$ dimensions with low reconstruction error, the data's
  'true' dimensionality is $k$."** Low linear reconstruction error means the data lies *close to
  a $k$-dimensional linear subspace* — a narrower and stronger claim than "intrinsic
  dimensionality." The circle example has one true intrinsic dimension (an angle) and yet needs
  both linear PCA components to reconstruct reasonably, because the manifold curves.
- **"A steep drop in the scree plot always means you've found the 'right' number of components."**
  It means you've found a point where the eigenvalues change regime sharply — which is a genuinely
  useful signal, and the 8-feature/3-latent-factor example shows it lining up cleanly with the true
  generative structure. But real, messy data rarely hands you a 15× cliff; a gentler, more
  ambiguous decline is common, and reading a "right" $k$ off it is a judgment call, exactly as
  Lesson 2 flagged for the k-means elbow.
- **"PCA and choosing $k$ for k-means are unrelated problems."** They rhyme deliberately: both ask
  "how many directions/groups does this unlabeled data really have," both answer it with a curve
  (inertia-vs-$k$, eigenvalue-vs-component) read by a human for an elbow or a cliff, and both
  curves can decline smoothly with no clean answer at all. Unsupervised learning does not remove
  the judgment call M01 built the whole course around — it just moves where the judgment call has
  to happen.

```python
import numpy as np

def kmeans_plusplus_init(X: np.ndarray, k: int, rng: np.random.Generator) -> np.ndarray:
    n, p = X.shape
    centroids = np.empty((k, p))
    first = rng.integers(n)
    centroids[0] = X[first]
    closest_d2 = ((X - centroids[0]) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
    for i in range(1, k):
        probs = closest_d2 / closest_d2.sum()
        idx = rng.choice(n, p=probs)
        centroids[i] = X[idx]
        new_d2 = ((X - centroids[i]) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
        closest_d2 = np.minimum(closest_d2, new_d2)
    return centroids

def lloyds_kmeans(X, k, init_centroids, max_iters=300, tol=1e-6):
    centroids = init_centroids.copy().astype(float)
    n = X.shape[0]
    labels = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
    for it in range(max_iters):
        d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - centroids[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
        labels = d2.argmin(axis=1)
        new_centroids = centroids.copy()
        for j in range(k):
            mask = labels == j
            if mask.any():
                new_centroids[j] = X[mask].mean(axis=0)
            # else: empty cluster — keep previous centroid in place
        shift = np.linalg.norm(new_centroids - centroids)
        centroids = new_centroids
        if shift < tol:
            break
    d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - centroids[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
    labels = d2.argmin(axis=1)
    inertia = d2[np.arange(n), labels].sum()
    return centroids, labels, float(inertia), it + 1
```

```python
def random_init(X, k, rng):

def best_of_n_restarts(X, k, init_fn, n_restarts, base_seed=0):
    best = None
    for i in range(n_restarts):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(base_seed + i)
        init = init_fn(X, k, rng)
        centroids, labels, inertia, _ = lloyds_kmeans(X, k, init)
        if best is None or inertia < best[2]:
```

```python
import numpy as np

def fit_pca(X, n_components=None):
    n, p = X.shape
    if n_components is None:
        n_components = p
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    Xc = X - mean
    cov = (Xc.T @ Xc) / (n - 1)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)          # ascending
    order = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]
    eigvals_sorted = eigvals[order]
    eigvecs_sorted = eigvecs[:, order]
    total = eigvals_sorted.sum()
    components = eigvecs_sorted[:, :n_components].T
    explained_variance = eigvals_sorted[:n_components]
    explained_variance_ratio = eigvals_sorted[:n_components] / total
    return mean, components, explained_variance, explained_variance_ratio

def transform(X, mean, components):
    return (X - mean) @ components.T

def reconstruct(Z, mean, components):
    return mean + Z @ components

def n_components_for_variance(explained_variance_ratio, threshold=0.95):
    cumulative = np.cumsum(explained_variance_ratio)
    return int(np.searchsorted(cumulative, threshold) + 1)
```

## Elle pacing notes

**The wall: "k-means always converges, so why did I get a different answer on two runs?"**
Learners confuse two different claims — "Lloyd's algorithm converges" (true: $J$ monotonically
decreases to *a* fixed point, every time, no exceptions) and "k-means converges to *the* answer"
(false: which fixed point depends on initialization). The tell is a learner re-running their own
code with `np.random.default_rng()` (no seed) and being confused that results differ.
**Reroute:** have them fix the seed and run twice — identical results, proving the algorithm
itself is deterministic given its inputs — then change only the seed and run again, to isolate
initialization, not the algorithm, as the source of variation. This is quick (under 10 minutes)
and the confusion evaporates once they've isolated the variable themselves rather than being told.

**The wall: the elongated-cluster failure reads as "I did the standardization wrong," not as
"k-means genuinely cannot represent this shape."** Because Lesson 2's earlier content
(initialization) is fixable with a better algorithm choice (k-means++), learners default to
assuming the elongated-cluster failure (63.3% agreement) is *also* fixable with a better technique
— they'll try standardizing features, trying more restarts, tuning `tol`, none of which move the
number meaningfully, because the problem isn't numerical, it's structural (k-means' notion of
"cluster" is a ball). **Reroute:** after ~15 minutes of technique-tuning with no improvement, have
them plot the elongated data directly and trace, by eye, which points a *ball* centered anywhere
reasonable would misclassify — the visual argument (a circle drawn around any point on a long
thin cluster necessarily includes points from the *other* thin cluster if they're close and
parallel) makes the structural nature of the limit obvious in a way the accuracy number alone does
not.

not a productive struggle to sit inside — the sign-ambiguity fact is stated plainly in the lesson,

## Three-tier reading — the average that a centroid or a principal axis is

**Tier 1 — material ground.** K-means centroids and PCA's top principal components are both,
mechanically, weighted averages over whoever supplied the training data: a centroid is literally
the arithmetic mean of its assigned points, and a principal axis is the direction of maximum
variance across the *entire* training population, weighted by how many points sit in each region.
This is not an interpretation — it falls directly out of the calculus in Lessons 1 and 3. These
two techniques underpin real, deployed systems: customer-segmentation models built on k-means,
"typical profile" embeddings built on PCA or its descendants, and anomaly-detection systems that
flag whatever has the highest reconstruction error against a PCA-derived "normal" subspace. That
these systems are averages over a training population, by construction, is a verifiable
mathematical fact, not a critique of the method.

**Tier 2 — what "explains 95% of the variance" suppresses.** The Assessment's disaggregation
requirement is Tier 2 made checkable in your own code, not an add-on ethics reading. A population-
weighted average — which is exactly what inertia, covariance, and reconstruction error all are —
mathematically privileges whoever is numerous. If 92% of your training population shares a set of
correlated patterns and 8% varies along a genuinely different axis, PCA's top components will
overwhelmingly track the 92%'s structure, because that is what maximizing *aggregate* variance
does — it is not a bug, not an oversight, it is the literal optimization target working correctly.
The minority's data isn't ignored, exactly — it's still there, contributing to the total variance
— but it contributes a small, diluted amount to axes chosen to explain the majority, so the
minority ends up with *systematically higher reconstruction error* not because anything is wrong
with their data, but because the axes were never fit to explain it well in the first place. The
same logic applies to k-means: a minority subpopulation shaped differently from the majority
either gets absorbed into whichever majority-shaped cluster is nearest, or — if it's distinct
enough — becomes its own small cluster whose centroid, an average of a much smaller and often
noisier sample, is a shakier summary than the majority clusters' centroids, without the system
reporting that difference in reliability anywhere.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** You ship a customer-segmentation model built on k-means, or a "typical
usage pattern" embedding space built on PCA, trained on whoever was in your data — which is
whoever used the product, applied for the loan, showed up for the health screening, before you
started. It performs beautifully on the aggregate metrics: low inertia, 95% variance explained,
clean scree plot, clean elbow. Somewhere in the tail of that population is a subgroup — maybe
defined by something you tracked, maybe by something you never collected a column for — whose
patterns genuinely differ from the majority's, and whose reconstruction error or distance-to-
centroid is, by the plain mechanics of the algorithm, going to run higher than everyone else's.
Someone downstream — a product manager reading a segment dashboard, a clinician reading a "risk
distance from normal profile" score, a loan officer reading "this application doesn't fit our
typical customer profile" — sees that higher number and has to decide what it means. Your model
cannot tell them whether it means "this person's situation is genuinely unusual" or "this person's
kind of pattern was never well-represented in the data these axes were built from" — those two
readings look numerically identical from inside the algorithm, and only one of them is a fact
about the person. Before you ship the aggregate number, who is in the part of your training
population too small to have shaped the axes, and what happens to them the day their distance
from the centroid gets read as a fact about who they are rather than a fact about who was in the
room when the axes were drawn?

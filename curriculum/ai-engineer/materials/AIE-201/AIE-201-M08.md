## Module overview

Every module before this one answered a version of "how do I build this well." This module answers
a question none of them could: **should you build it at all, and if so, how much of it?** It is the
module the rest of the course has been quietly building toward — M01 gave you the vocabulary to say
a problem has no learnable signal; M02 and M03 gave you a ladder of models of increasing complexity
to climb; M05 gave you the honest, leak-free comparison a climb like that depends on; M06 gave you
the discipline to distrust a number that looks too good. This module assembles those four pieces
into a single, gaming-resistant procedure — the **complexity ladder** — and adds the one ingredient
none of the previous modules needed: **the arithmetic of whether the win is worth building.**

The claim this module defends, with evidence three separate times, is uncomfortable on purpose:
**a model can be genuinely, measurably better than the alternative and still be the wrong thing to
build.** Not because the model is broken. Not because the evaluation leaked. Because the
alternative — a lookup table, a rule someone already wrote, a program that hands a human the raw
numbers, or literally nothing — was cheap, auditable, and already close enough, and closing the
remaining gap costs more to build and keep alive than the gap is worth. Seven modules of this
course have trained you to reach for the more expressive tool. This module trains the muscle every
one of those modules assumed you'd eventually need: **the discipline to put the tool down and show
your work for why.**

The descent, one more time, downward through the assumption instead of the algorithm: price what a
model actually costs across its lifetime, not just what it costs to train, and watch a fully
maintained gradient-boosting pipeline lose to an eight-row table it couldn't beat by a
statistically real margin (Lesson 1). Meet the lookup table and the hand-written rule as first-class
competitors — not a strawman for the model to beat, but frequently the rung that wins, and see
exactly why an 18,900-node random forest can spend all that capacity rediscovering thirty-two
numbers a human already knew (Lesson 2). Meet the hardest case: a model with real, non-noise signal,
solving a genuinely actionable problem, that still shouldn't be built, because the arithmetic of a
capacity constraint and a low base rate says so — and watch that arithmetic done wrong, silently,
be indistinguishable from a great pitch deck (Lesson 3). Then formalize the whole procedure as a
ladder with a stopping rule, so "keep it simple" stops being a slogan you agree with in the abstract
and becomes a test you actually ran, with a p-value and a confidence interval attached to the
decision to stop (Lesson 4). Re-ascent: you can sit in front of a roadmap that assumes "we're
building the ML version of this" and, before writing a line of training code, produce the two
numbers — the paired significance test and the maintenance-adjusted breakeven — that tell you and
everyone in the room whether that assumption survives contact with evidence.

This is also the course's most unusual assessed outcome, named in the syllabus by design: you will
be graded on the rigor of an argument, not on which side of "build it" you land on. A learner who
always says "don't build it" fails this module exactly as fast as a learner who always says "ship
the fancy model" — both are pattern-matching a vibe, not running the ladder. The scenario bank this
module's assessment draws from contains cases where the correct, evidence-backed answer is "yes,
build the complex model, and here is the number that says so." Rigor is the only thing being
scored.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The true cost of ML: the ledger nobody puts in the notebook

**Teaching text**

Every module before this one reported a model's quality with a single number — accuracy, $R^2$,
inertia — and stopped there, because the module's job was to teach you to compute that number
honestly. This lesson adds the number every one of those modules left out: **what does getting that
number cost, across the model's entire deployed life, not just the training run that produced it?**

For a tabular model of the kind this course builds, training compute is very often the *cheapest*
line on the ledger — logistic regression and gradient boosting on 5,000–500,000 rows train in
seconds to minutes on a laptop. The lifecycle cost lives almost entirely somewhere else:

| Ledger line | What it actually is | Who pays it, and when |
|---|---|---|
| Data collection & labeling | Getting the training signal into a table at all (recall AIE-201's ethics thread: labeled data is bought labor) | Up front, and again every time the label definition drifts |
| Feature pipeline engineering | Code that must reproduce, bit-for-bit, whatever transform training assumed — in production, at inference time, under load | Built once, **owned forever** |
| Validation & the evaluation contract | Everything M05 required — an honest split, a real baseline, leakage checks | Every model version, every retrain |
| Deployment infrastructure | Serving the model where a decision needs it, with a latency budget | Ongoing infra cost, whether or not the model earns its keep that month |
| Drift monitoring | Detecting the M06 failure modes — the population moving out from under a model validated on yesterday's data | Ongoing, and silent when skipped |
| Retraining cadence | A schedule, a pipeline, a person who owns "did this retrain actually help or just add noise" | Recurring, and easy to under-budget |
| Incident response / on-call | Someone's pager, the day the model's output is visibly wrong in front of a customer | Unbounded, and the least-forecast line on this table |
| Interpretability / audit debt | The cost of explaining a decision to a regulator, an auditor, or an angry customer who wants a reason | Contingent, catastrophic when it comes due unbudgeted |

A rule-based system or a lookup table pays almost none of these recurring costs. It is written once,
reviewed once like any other code change, and — critically — **it does not silently go stale the
way a fitted model's parameters do**, because it encodes a decision a person made on purpose rather
than a distribution's shape at one moment in time. Its "retraining" is a deliberate edit someone
signs off on, not a scheduled job that can quietly start doing the wrong thing. The comparison this
lesson insists on is therefore never "which one scores higher on the held-out set." It is: **does
the nominal score gap clear the lifetime cost gap** — a bar this lesson will call the
**maintenance-adjusted margin**, to distinguish it from the raw, nominal gap a leaderboard shows you.

**Worked example — an 8-row table vs. a fully maintained gradient-boosting pipeline**

A logistics team wants to flag parcels likely to arrive late. The engineered features are `carrier`
(4 carriers), `is_weekend` (a derived flag from `day_of_week`), `distance_km`, and `weight_kg` — the
last two included because they were cheap to compute, on the plausible-sounding theory that farther
and heavier parcels run later, on `n = 6,000` parcels (`rng = np.random.default_rng(208)`), split
70/30. Four rungs were fit, in order of rising complexity — a majority-class baseline, a lookup
table keyed on `(carrier, is_weekend)`, a regularized logistic regression on all four features, and
a gradient-boosting classifier on all four features:

| Rung | What it is | Test accuracy | Test AUC | Test F1 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | Majority-class baseline (always predict "not late") | 0.5739 | — | — |
| 1 | **Lookup table**, 8 cells: `(carrier, is_weekend) → rate ≥ 0.5` | 0.6689 | 0.7275 | 0.5512 |
| 2 | Logistic regression, all 4 features, standardized | 0.6700 | 0.7241 | 0.5619 |
| 3 | Gradient boosting, all 4 features | 0.6678 | 0.7264 | 0.5795 |

The eight-row table (four carriers × weekend-or-not) is, on this test set, **not beaten at all** by
a fully trained, hyperparameter-defaulted gradient-boosting classifier fed two extra continuous
features the table never sees — it edges GBM out by a hair (0.6689 vs. 0.6678). A paired bootstrap
over 5,000 resamples of the test set (the method Lesson 4 formalizes) puts the GBM-minus-lookup gap's
95% CI at **(−0.0122, 0.0100)**, squarely straddling zero: `p(GBM beats lookup) = 0.409`. The
distance and weight columns a data scientist would reflexively feed to a boosting model turned out to
carry no information the table wasn't already capturing through carrier and weekend status alone —
you cannot tell that from the feature list; you can only find it out by running the ladder. Meanwhile
the eight-row table costs a human ten seconds to read end to end, needs no feature pipeline beyond
two `groupby` keys already sitting in the source data, cannot drift the way a fitted model's
coefficients can (its "parameters" *are* the business rule, chosen on purpose), and produces a
prediction a customer-service rep can explain to an angry customer without hand-waving about SHAP
values. **The maintenance-adjusted margin here is not "is the gap positive" — it's "is the gap large
enough, and certain enough, to justify owning a retraining pipeline, a monitoring dashboard, and an
on-call rotation for it."** By that bar, this gap loses badly, before you even reach for the
boosting library.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A more sophisticated model always captures more value."** The table above is the refutation in
  one row: gradient boosting, given two additional continuous features the lookup table never sees,
  does not beat the table at all on this test set (0.6678 vs. 0.6689) — a paired bootstrap can't even
  tell the two apart in gradient boosting's favor. More model capacity, and more features, extracted
  zero additional signal here, because the signal was never more than four carrier effects and a
  weekend flag — capacity spent chasing noise that isn't there is not "trying harder," it's
  overfitting a training run's specific draw.
- **"If it doesn't cost extra GPU-hours, it's free."** For tabular problems at this course's scale,
  training compute is almost never the dominant cost. The ledger above is dominated by pipeline
  ownership, monitoring, and incident response — costs that exist whether or not a single extra
  minute of training time was spent, and that a lookup table mostly does not incur at all.
- **"The accuracy number is the whole ROI case."** Accuracy (or AUC, or $R^2$) measures whether the
  model is *right more often*. It says nothing about what a correct prediction is worth, what a
  wrong one costs, or how many predictions you can even act on — the subject of Lesson 3.

### Lesson 2 — Rule-based systems and lookup tables: when memorizing beats learning

**Teaching text**

A lookup table is not a strawman baseline invented to make models look good by comparison. It is a
legitimate estimator in its own right — the discrete analogue of pure memorization, with zero bias
inside any cell it has enough data to estimate and zero learned generalization outside the keys it
has seen. It **dominates** a fitted model under three conditions, and this course has already given
you the vocabulary for all three:

1. **The input space is small, finite, or can be usefully bucketed** — a handful of categorical
   keys whose cross-product is a few dozen to a few thousand cells, not an open-ended continuous
   space.
2. **The relationship is close to deterministic given those keys** — most of the outcome variance
   lives *between* cells (which carrier, which zone), not within one, so a per-cell mean or rate is
   close to the true generating function, not a noisy point estimate of it.
3. **Each cell has enough rows to estimate its rate or mean stably** — this is the same bias–variance
   trade AIE-201-M01 built by hand, just stated over cells instead of over model families: too many
   cells relative to your data and the lookup table starts overfitting exactly the way an
   unregularized, fully grown decision tree does (a fully grown tree *is*, in the limit, a lookup
   table over its leaves).

That last equivalence is the whole lesson in one sentence: **a decision tree deep enough to split on
every combination of your categorical keys is learning the lookup table — just less directly, at
far greater representational cost, and without a human ever getting to read the rule it landed on.**
When conditions 1–3 hold, an ensemble of such trees is not learning something a table couldn't
represent; it is spending enormous capacity *rediscovering* a table that already existed in the data
and could have been written down directly.

Rule-based systems earn a second, independent argument beyond "the domain is small": a rule that
encodes a **known invariant** (a regulatory threshold, a physical constraint, a business policy
someone deliberately chose) can be *more* robust to the distribution shift AIE-201-M06 spent a
lesson cataloging than a fitted model is. A model's parameters encode the shape of a training
distribution at one point in time; when the population moves, those parameters silently stop
matching reality while still producing confident-looking output. A rule that encodes "flag any
shipment where declared value exceeds $10,000" doesn't care whether the population of shippers
changed — the invariant it's checking didn't move, because it was never a statistical property of
training data to begin with.

**Worked example — 32 cells matching an 18,900-node forest**

A shipping team wants to predict `delivery_days` and knows, from how the network is actually
provisioned, that transit time is a function of exactly two things: which of 8 origin zones a
parcel ships from, and which of 4 carriers handles it — 32 zone–carrier combinations, each with its
own typical transit time plus ordinary day-to-day noise (`σ = 1.0` day, `n = 5,000`,
`rng = np.random.default_rng(311)`, 70/30 split). Three estimators were fit: a lookup table (each
cell's mean transit time, computed on train only), a 300-tree random forest on one-hot zone and
carrier columns, and a gradient-boosting regressor on the same columns.

| Estimator | Test RMSE (days) | Test MAE (days) | "Parameters" |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lookup table (32 group means) | 0.9888 | 0.7900 | **32** numbers |
| Random forest, 300 trees | 0.9891 | 0.7902 | **18,900** tree nodes |
| Gradient boosting | 0.9883 | 0.7898 | (hundreds of shallow trees) |

The data was generated with an irreducible noise floor of `σ = 1.0` day — meaning **≈0.99 is
essentially the best any estimator can do**, model or table, because the remaining error is not
signal anyone left on the table; it's noise no estimator can remove. The random forest's 18,900
nodes across 300 trees produce an RMSE **0.0003 days worse** than the 32-row table — a gap of about
22 seconds on a multi-day transit estimate. The bootstrap CI on that gap (0.00002 to 0.00051) is
entirely on the "table wins" side, so with enough test data even a two-hundred-and-thirty-microsecond
difference in a bootstrap resample average can register as "statistically real" — and Lesson 4's
second bar (does the gap clear the maintenance-adjusted margin) is exactly the check that stops
anyone from acting on it: 22 seconds of transit-time precision is not worth a forest and an inference
service, full stop, whether or not a bootstrap can detect it. Gradient boosting lands 0.0005 days
*better* than the table, with a CI (−0.0012, 0.0001) that does straddle zero — not even reliably
better. Every rung of complexity above the lookup table bought essentially nothing here, because the
table already *was* the correct function — the forest spent 590× as many parameters re-deriving the
same 32 numbers a `groupby().mean()` produces in one line, with none of the auditability: a reviewer
can read a 32-row CSV in a code review and catch a wrong number; no reviewer is reading 18,900 split
conditions.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A lookup table can't generalize, so it's not real ML."** It generalizes perfectly well when the
  domain of keys is genuinely closed — 8 zones and 4 carriers is a fixed, known set, not a sample
  from a larger population you'll later see new members of. Its real failure mode is different from
  underfitting: a *novel key* (a ninth zone) it has zero rows for. That's a design constraint to
  plan around (a fallback rate), not evidence the whole approach is illegitimate.
- **"Trees basically are lookup tables, so there's no real difference."** Approximately true for
  low-cardinality categorical domains — which is exactly the point, not a dismissal of it: when a
  fitted ensemble converges to behavior indistinguishable from a table a human could write, prefer
  the version that's version-controlled as a diffable CSV, auditable line by line, and doesn't need
  a serialized model artifact and an inference server to answer one lookup.
- **"Preferring rules over models is what people who don't understand ML do."** It's the reverse:
  correctly identifying that a fitted model's added flexibility is buying nothing above a much
  cheaper alternative requires *more* judgment than defaulting to the biggest model available — it
  requires having built the ladder in Lesson 4 and read its result honestly.

### Lesson 3 — Don't build it: the cases where no model clears the bar

**Teaching text**

Lessons 1 and 2 both ended with "build the cheaper thing instead." This lesson covers the harder,
more uncomfortable case: sometimes the honest answer is **build nothing**, and the model that
"works" — with real, non-noise, statistically genuine signal — is still the wrong thing to ship.
Four independent conditions each, alone, justify "don't build it"; a real business case can fail on
more than one at once, but any single one is sufficient:

1. **Near-irreducible noise.** The learner's very first tool in this course, AIE-201-M01's
   generalization framing, applies directly here: if the best achievable AUC is barely above 0.5, or
   the best achievable $R^2$ is barely above 0, there is close to no learnable function connecting
   the features you have to the outcome you want. Building *anything* — simple or fancy — is fitting
   to noise, and a lookup table will overfit exactly as fast as a forest will.
2. **No actionability.** Even a hypothetically perfect prediction changes nothing about what anyone
   downstream does differently. A forecast nobody acts on is a report, not a decision system, and
   its entire cost ledger (Lesson 1) is pure loss.
3. **Negative expected value even with genuine, non-trivial lift.** This is the case this lesson
   spends its worked example on, because it is the one the first two conditions don't warn you
   about: the model has real signal, the decision is genuinely actionable, and the arithmetic of a
   **capacity constraint** (you can only act on $K$ cases) crossed with a **low base rate** (the
   thing you're predicting is rare) still produces a program that loses money, because the total
   value available to capture is capped below what the program costs to run.
4. **The error distribution's harm to a subgroup swamps the aggregate benefit.** This is where the
   course's ethics thread lands with teeth: an average metric that looks fine can hide a failure
   mode concentrated entirely on people the aggregate number was never built to represent. A model
   whose overall accuracy is excellent and whose false-negative rate on one subgroup is three times
   the majority-group rate is not "mostly right" — ask who is standing in that tail before any
   deployment conversation, not after.

Condition 3 is the one to internalize hardest, because it is the one that survives every check the
first two conditions catch. The model can pass a rigorous M05-style evaluation, show a real,
statistically significant lift over baseline, and *still* not be worth building — and the only way
to know is to do the arithmetic, in dollars, at the actual operating point the business will run it
at.

**Worked example — a churn-outreach program with a real model and a capacity constraint**

A support team wants to call customers who are about to churn before they churn. The pool is 20,000
customers, refreshed daily; the true churn rate is a known, low **1.06%** (this quarter's actual
number, not a fitted estimate); the team has **K = 50** outreach slots per day; a successful save is
worth **$450** in retained lifetime value; a call converts a true would-be-churner into staying
**25%** of the time it reaches one; each call costs **$6** in agent time; a vendor quotes
**$95,000/year** ($380/operating day across 250 days) to build and run the full ML scoring
platform — feature pipeline, retraining, monitoring, on-call.

A logistic regression on tenure, recent support-ticket count, monthly spend, and contract type
achieves **AUC = 0.7326** on a held-out pool — genuine, non-trivial signal, not noise (rules out
condition 1). Calling a flagged customer is a real, executable action (rules out condition 2).
Because a single day's 50 calls only contain a handful of true churners (the held-out pool has 82 in
8,000 rows), precision@50 on any one day is noisy; the team simulated **300 bootstrap-resampled
"days"** and averaged (the same Monte Carlo discipline M06 required of any rare-class metric):

| Targeting policy | Mean precision@50 | SD across days | Daily retention value | Daily cost | **Net daily** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Random 50 (no targeting) | 0.0090 | 0.0133 | $50.63 | $300 (calls only) | **−$249.38** |
| Existing free heuristic (rank by recent ticket count) | 0.0422 | 0.0287 | $237.38 | $300 (calls only) | **−$62.62** |
| **ML model (full platform)** | **0.0885** | 0.0400 | $498.00 | $680 (calls + $380 platform) | **−$182.00** |

The model roughly *doubles* precision over the existing free heuristic (8.85% vs. 4.22%) and nearly
*ten-fold*s it over random targeting — a real, meaningful lift, exactly the kind of number a
project readout would headline. And the fully-costed program is still net negative, worse than the
heuristic it would replace: **building the platform loses an extra $29,844/year** compared to
leaving the free heuristic in place, because the model's operational value gain over the heuristic
(≈$261/day) doesn't cover the platform's $380/day fixed cost. Solve for the breakeven: the platform
would need to cost **$260.62/day ($65,156/year) or less** to pay for itself against the heuristic it
replaces — a real number the team can take back to the vendor, or use to scope a cheaper, simpler
scoring approach (a hand-maintained scorecard recomputed monthly, no real-time pipeline) that might
capture most of the same lift for a fraction of the quoted cost. **"The model works" and "build the
model" were never the same claim — only the second one needed this arithmetic.**

**Named misconceptions**

- **"If the model beats the baseline, ship it."** Beating a baseline is necessary and was never
  sufficient — it says nothing about whether the decision it's slotted into is worth automating,
  what a correct prediction is worth, or what capacity exists to act on it.
- **"A low base rate just means the model needs to be better."** Sometimes true — but a fixed
  capacity constraint ($K$ slots) puts a *ceiling* on how much value is even available to capture,
  independent of model quality. Check the ceiling (breakeven precision, given $K$ and the payoffs)
  before assuming a better model would fix the arithmetic; here, doubling precision again to ~0.18
  would still only add another ~$260/day, not close the remaining gap by much more.
- **"A good AUC means deploy it."** AUC summarizes ranking quality across every possible threshold;
  the business operates at exactly **one** threshold — or one capacity constraint. A model with
  excellent AUC can still deliver a precision@K that doesn't clear the cost floor at the one
  operating point that matters, exactly as shown above.

### Lesson 4 — The complexity ladder: Occam's razor as a procedure with evidence, not a slogan

**Teaching text**

"Prefer the simpler model" is advice everyone in this field claims to follow and almost no one runs
as an actual procedure. This lesson turns it into one, assembling pieces from every prior lesson and
module into a fixed sequence with a stopping rule attached — the same procedure every worked example
in this module has already been quietly running:

1. **Define the ladder before touching data.** Rung 0: a trivial baseline (majority class / mean).
   Rung 1: a lookup table or a hand-written domain rule, where the domain supports one. Rung 2: a
   simple parametric model — linear or regularized logistic regression, from AIE-201-M02. Rung 3: a
   tree ensemble or boosting model, from AIE-201-M03. Fixing the ladder *before* looking at results
   is the ladder's own Predict step — it exists precisely to prevent the next failure mode.
2. **Climb one rung at a time, on the same held-out test set, with a paired test.** Compare each
   rung to the *previous* rung — never to rung 0 alone, never to an unrelated leaderboard number —
   using a **paired** bootstrap (or McNemar's test, for classification) on the *same* test
   observations. Pairing matters because it directly measures the gap you care about (did this
   specific set of predictions change for the better) instead of comparing two noisy summary
   numbers as if they were independent draws — exactly the mistake that makes a small nominal gap
   look meaningful until you check its confidence interval.
3. **Stop at the first rung a later rung fails to clear on both bars.** A later rung only survives
   if it beats the previous rung with (a) statistical significance on the paired test, **and** (b) a
   margin that clears the maintenance-adjusted TCO delta from Lesson 1. Fail either bar and the
   climb stops — report the earlier, simpler rung as the recommendation, with the failed
   comparison's CI as the evidence for why.
4. **Guard against multiple comparisons.** If you instead try many model families and
   hyperparameter settings and report whichever one happened to win, you've reintroduced the exact
   failure AIE-201-M06 warned about with repeated validation-set peeking: trying enough things
   guarantees one looks good by luck alone. The ladder is climbed with its rungs fixed in advance,
   not selected after seeing which one flatters the result.

**Worked example — climbing the late-delivery ladder to its honest stopping point**

Reusing Lesson 1's late-delivery data end to end, with the paired-bootstrap procedure made explicit:

| Comparison | Δ accuracy (later − earlier) | $p(\Delta \le 0)$ | 95% CI | Climb? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rung 1 (lookup) − Rung 0 (majority) | **+0.0950** | **< 0.001** | (0.0694, 0.1211) | **Yes — clear win** |
| Rung 2 (logreg) − Rung 1 (lookup) | +0.0011 | 0.389 | (−0.0044, 0.0067) | **No — fails significance** |
| Rung 3 (GBM) − Rung 2 (logreg) | −0.0022 | 0.657 | (−0.0144, 0.0106) | **No — fails significance** |
| *(direct check)* Rung 3 (GBM) − Rung 1 (lookup) | −0.0011 | 0.591 | (−0.0122, 0.0100) | **No — confirms stopping point** |

The ladder climbs exactly once — rung 0 to rung 1 is not close, $p < 0.001$, and the lookup table is
trivially cheap to justify against a baseline that is wrong on 43% of parcels. Every rung after that
fails to clear significance, and the nominal "gains" barely exist at all — rung 2 adds a nominal
0.11 points over rung 1, and rung 3 is nominally *worse* than rung 2 by 0.22 points, both comfortably
inside noise. Nothing past rung 1 clears Lesson 1's maintenance-adjusted margin, because nothing past
rung 1 clears zero first. **The honest report is: ship the lookup table, stop at rung 1, and state
the CI** — not "the lookup table is definitively best," but "this test set's power cannot
distinguish rung 1 from rungs 2 or 3, and rung 1 is far cheaper to own," which is a falsifiable,
revisitable claim a future team with a larger test set could overturn with evidence, not a permanent
verdict.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"You always start from the simplest model and stop as early as possible."** No — you define the
  full ladder and its stopping rule *before* running it, then climb it honestly and report wherever
  it actually stops, even if that's rung 3. A learner who always stops at rung 1 regardless of the
  data is gaming a bias toward simplicity, not running the procedure; this module's assessment
  includes a scenario constructed so that the honest stopping point is rung 3, precisely to catch
  this.
- **"A ladder that stops at rung 1 proves rung 1 is 'the best model.'** It proves rung 1 is the
  most complexity *this test set's statistical power can justify*. Report the CI, not just the
  verdict — a wider CI on more data might later detect rung 2's small nominal edge as real, and the
  next team should be able to see that possibility in your report, not just your conclusion.
- **"Statistical significance alone settles it."** A statistically significant but small gain can
  still fail to clear the maintenance-adjusted margin from Lesson 1. The ladder's stopping rule
  requires *both* bars — significance and TCO-adjusted margin — precisely because either one alone
  can be gamed (a huge test set makes even trivial gaps "significant"; ignoring cost makes any
  positive gap look worth taking).

### Lab M08-A — Build the complexity ladder and defend a stopping point

Uses the late-delivery dataset from Lessons 1 and 4: `carrier` (4 levels), `is_weekend`,
`distance_km`, `weight_kg` predicting `late` (binary), `n = 6,000`.

**Predict**

    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    cut = int(frac_train * len(df))
    return df.iloc[idx[:cut]].copy(), df.iloc[idx[cut:]].copy()


def rung0_majority(train: pd.DataFrame, test: pd.DataFrame) -> np.ndarray:
    majority = train["late"].mode()[0]
    return np.full(len(test), majority)


def rung1_lookup_table(train: pd.DataFrame, test: pd.DataFrame) -> np.ndarray:
    lut = train.groupby(["carrier", "is_weekend"])["late"].mean()
    fallback = train["late"].mean()
    keys = list(zip(test["carrier"], test["is_weekend"]))
    rates = np.array([lut.get(k, fallback) for k in keys])
    return (rates >= 0.5).astype(int)


def _encode(train: pd.DataFrame, test: pd.DataFrame):
    cols = ["is_weekend", "distance_km", "weight_kg"]
    train_oh = pd.get_dummies(train[["carrier"] + cols], columns=["carrier"], drop_first=True)
    test_oh = pd.get_dummies(test[["carrier"] + cols], columns=["carrier"], drop_first=True)
    test_oh = test_oh.reindex(columns=train_oh.columns, fill_value=0)
    scaler = StandardScaler().fit(train_oh[["distance_km", "weight_kg"]])
    train_oh[["distance_km", "weight_kg"]] = scaler.transform(train_oh[["distance_km", "weight_kg"]])
    test_oh[["distance_km", "weight_kg"]] = scaler.transform(test_oh[["distance_km", "weight_kg"]])
    return train_oh, test_oh


def rung2_logistic_regression(train: pd.DataFrame, test: pd.DataFrame) -> np.ndarray:
    train_oh, test_oh = _encode(train, test)
    model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(train_oh, train["late"])
    return model.predict(test_oh)


def rung3_gradient_boosting(train: pd.DataFrame, test: pd.DataFrame) -> np.ndarray:
    train_oh, test_oh = _encode(train, test)
    model = GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=208).fit(train_oh, train["late"])
    return model.predict(test_oh)


def paired_bootstrap_pvalue(y_true, pred_a, pred_b, n_boot: int = 5000, seed: int = 0):
    y_true = np.asarray(y_true)
    correct_a = (np.asarray(pred_a) == y_true).astype(int)
    correct_b = (np.asarray(pred_b) == y_true).astype(int)
    obs_diff = correct_b.mean() - correct_a.mean()
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = len(y_true)
    diffs = np.empty(n_boot)
    for i in range(n_boot):
        sample = rng.integers(0, n, size=n)
        diffs[i] = correct_b[sample].mean() - correct_a[sample].mean()
    p_le_zero = float(np.mean(diffs <= 0))
    ci_lo, ci_hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
    return float(obs_diff), p_le_zero, (float(ci_lo), float(ci_hi))

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor, GradientBoostingRegressor

    }
    zone_choice = rng.choice(zones, size=n)
    carrier_choice = rng.choice(carriers, size=n)
    mu = np.array([combo_means[(z, c)] for z, c in zip(zone_choice, carrier_choice)])

        diffs[i] = np.sqrt(err_b[s].mean()) - np.sqrt(err_a[s].mean())
    observed = np.sqrt(err_b.mean()) - np.sqrt(err_a.mean())
    ci = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])

```python
# solution.py — Lab M08-C
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

K = 50
LTV_SAVED = 450.0
SAVE_RATE_IF_CALLED = 0.25
COST_PER_CALL = 6.0
OPERATING_DAYS_PER_YEAR = 250
ML_SYSTEM_ANNUAL_COST = 95000.0
ML_SYSTEM_DAILY_COST = ML_SYSTEM_ANNUAL_COST / OPERATING_DAYS_PER_YEAR


def make_churn_pool(seed: int = 417, n: int = 20000) -> pd.DataFrame:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    tenure_months = rng.gamma(shape=3.0, scale=8.0, size=n)
    recent_tickets = rng.poisson(lam=0.4, size=n)
    monthly_spend = rng.gamma(shape=4.0, scale=20.0, size=n)
    contract_m2m = rng.binomial(1, 0.35, size=n)
    logit = (-4.5 - 0.03 * tenure_months + 0.65 * recent_tickets
             - 0.004 * monthly_spend + 1.3 * contract_m2m)
    p_churn = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))
    churned = rng.binomial(1, p_churn)
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "tenure_months": tenure_months, "recent_tickets": recent_tickets,
        "monthly_spend": monthly_spend, "contract_m2m": contract_m2m, "churned": churned,
    })


def split(df: pd.DataFrame, seed: int = 417, frac_train: float = 0.6):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    idx = rng.permutation(len(df))
    cut = int(frac_train * len(df))
    return df.iloc[idx[:cut]].copy(), df.iloc[idx[cut:]].copy()


def fit_model(train: pd.DataFrame) -> LogisticRegression:
    features = ["tenure_months", "recent_tickets", "monthly_spend", "contract_m2m"]
    return LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(train[features], train["churned"])


def simulate_precision_at_k(test: pd.DataFrame, score_col, k: int = K,
                             n_days: int = 300, seed: int = 21,
                             tiebreak_cols=None, ascending=None) -> np.ndarray:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = len(test)
    precisions = np.empty(n_days)
    for i in range(n_days):
        sample_idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=n)
        day = test.iloc[sample_idx]
        if score_col == "__random__":
            pick_idx = rng.choice(len(day), size=k, replace=False)
            top = day.iloc[pick_idx]
        elif tiebreak_cols:
            top = day.sort_values(tiebreak_cols, ascending=ascending).head(k)
        else:
            top = day.sort_values(score_col, ascending=False).head(k)
        precisions[i] = top["churned"].mean()
    return precisions


def daily_value(precision: float, k: int = K, include_ml_cost: bool = False):
    expected_true = precision * k
    value = expected_true * SAVE_RATE_IF_CALLED * LTV_SAVED
    cost = k * COST_PER_CALL
    if include_ml_cost:
        cost += ML_SYSTEM_DAILY_COST
    return value - cost, value, cost


def breakeven_daily_ml_cost(model_precision: float, heuristic_net_daily: float, k: int = K) -> float:
    _, value, calls_cost = daily_value(model_precision, k=k, include_ml_cost=False)
    # net = value - calls_cost - ml_cost >= heuristic_net_daily  =>  ml_cost <= value - calls_cost - heuristic_net_daily
    return (value - calls_cost) - heuristic_net_daily
```

## Assessment

This module's certification artifact is the course's signature move, named in the syllabus: **the
learner is graded on the rigor of the argument, never on which verdict it reaches.** A learner who
reflexively recommends "don't build it" for every scenario fails exactly as fast as one who
reflexively recommends the fanciest model — both are pattern-matching, not running the procedure
this module built.

**The task.** Each learner is handed one packet, drawn from a fixed scenario bank, containing: a
short business narrative, a real (synthetic but seeded) dataset, and the operating constraints that
matter (a capacity limit, a per-error cost, a quoted build/maintain cost where relevant). The
scenario bank includes packets on both sides of the ladder's honest stopping point on purpose:

- Packets where the honest stopping point is **rung 1 or "don't build"** (as in every worked
  example and lab in this module).
- At least one packet — e.g., a credit-default risk score on a large, feature-rich portfolio with
  genuinely nonlinear interactions — where the honest, evidence-backed stopping point is **rung 3**:
  a gradient-boosted model clears logistic regression by several AUC points with $p < 0.001$, and
  the economics show the additional defaults caught are worth far more than the platform's TCO. A
  learner who ships "the lookup table" on this packet without running the ladder fails it on the
  evidence, not on the verdict.

In two hours, the learner produces a short **Recommendation Memo** plus the code that generated its
evidence: a written Predict (before touching the data), the executed complexity ladder with paired
significance tests, an explicit cost/actionability/capacity analysis where relevant, a stated
verdict, and a **named condition that would change the verdict.**

**Rubric**

| Dimension | Weight | 4 — Exemplary | 3 — Proficient | 2 — Developing | 1 — Insufficient |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| **Predict, written before data contact** | 15% | Specific, falsifiable predictions for ranking, significance, and stopping point, all timestamped/ordered before analysis | Predictions present and mostly specific but vague on one sub-claim | Predictions generic ("the simple one will probably win") or added after seeing results | No Predict step, or clearly written post hoc |
| **Complexity ladder executed correctly** | 25% | All applicable rungs built, each adjacent pair compared with a correctly implemented **paired** test, CIs reported | Ladder built and paired-tested but one comparison uses an unpaired or informal check | Ladder built but rungs compared only by eyeballing raw numbers, no significance test | No ladder — a single model was built and declared sufficient or insufficient by assertion |
| **Cost / actionability / capacity case** | 25% | TCO, actionability, and (where relevant) a capacity-constrained breakeven are all computed with traceable numbers; correctly identifies which of Lesson 3's four conditions apply | Cost case present and mostly correct but misses one relevant condition (e.g., ignores capacity constraint) | Cost mentioned qualitatively ("this would be expensive to maintain") with no computation | No cost/actionability reasoning — verdict rests on accuracy numbers alone |
| **Verdict matches the evidence produced** | 20% | Verdict (build / build simpler / don't build) follows directly and only from the learner's own computed numbers, including on packets where the correct verdict is "build the complex model" | Verdict matches evidence but the memo doesn't clearly connect which number drove it | Verdict is defensible but the memo shows signs of having been decided first and evidence arranged to fit | Verdict contradicts the learner's own computed numbers, or is a default answer applied regardless of the packet |
| **Named condition that would change the verdict** | 15% | A specific, quantified sensitivity (e.g., "if call-success rate exceeds 34%, the platform breaks even") tied to a real input in the model | A named condition, correctly directional but not quantified | A vague hedge ("more data might change this") with no mechanism | No stated condition, or the verdict is presented as unconditionally certain |

**Designed to defeat**

1. **Reflexive contrarianism.** Always answering "don't build it" fails outright on the scenario
   bank's rung-3-justified packet — a learner who never climbs the ladder past rung 1 has no
   evidence for *that* packet's correct verdict and loses the "verdict matches evidence" and
   "ladder executed correctly" rows regardless of how confident the memo sounds.
2. **Eyeballing instead of testing.** A memo that shows a bar chart with two close bars and says "so
   they're basically the same" without a paired test and a CI scores at most a 2 on "ladder executed
   correctly" — the rubric requires the actual statistic, not a description of what one might show.
3. **Invented economics.** Every dollar figure in the cost case must trace to either a number given
   in the scenario packet or a number the learner's own code computed from the data (a precision, a
   node count, an RMSE). A plausible-sounding but untraceable number ("maintenance would probably
   cost about $50k/year") scores a 1 on that row even if the eventual verdict happens to be correct
   — the grade is on the trail, not the destination.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime, specific to this module's unusual demand: after seven modules of
being trained to reach for the more powerful tool, this module asks the learner to argue for putting
it down — and to do that with the same rigor, not less.

- **Wall — identity resistance to recommending simplicity.** After M02–M07, many learners have
  internalized "better model = better answer" as an unstated value, not just a skill; being asked to
  argue *for* the lookup table can read as a demotion of everything just learned. **Reroute:** run
  the assessment's rung-3-justified scenario (or an equivalent) *first*, so the learner feels the
  "the correct evidence-backed verdict is the fancy model" case land before tackling a case where
  the honest answer is "don't build" — this establishes the module isn't anti-ML, it's pro-evidence,
  before asking for the harder verdict.
- **Wall — overcorrection into reflexive "don't build it."** The opposite failure, usually appearing
  right after the first wall resolves: a learner who has just internalized "sometimes the model
  isn't worth it" starts applying that conclusion by default, skipping the ladder. This reads as
  fluent but is exactly the gaming pattern the rubric is built to catch. **Reroute:** hand back the
  memo with one question — "which rung did you actually run the paired test against?" — before any
  content correction; usually the gap is procedural, not conceptual.
- **Wall — reading a bootstrap CI that straddles zero as "the rungs are equal."** A common
  misreading: "the CI includes zero" gets treated as proof of no difference, rather than as
  insufficient evidence of a difference (absence of evidence, not evidence of absence). **Reroute:**
  a short paper exercise — given a CI and a sample size, what *additional* test-set size would be
  needed to detect a hypothetical true gap of a stated size — grounds the distinction concretely
  before returning to the lab.
- **Wall — silent unit errors in the cost ledger.** Conflating daily and annual costs, or forgetting
  to divide a quoted annual platform price by operating days before comparing it to a per-day value
  figure, flips a verdict without throwing any error — structurally the same failure mode M05 taught
  ("a leak doesn't crash, it just quietly lies"). **Reroute:** require a unit-labeled line-by-line
  ledger (every number tagged `/day` or `/year` explicitly) before trusting any total; this alone
  catches most of these errors on inspection.
- **Signal — productive struggle.** A learner who says "I predicted the lookup table would win, and
  it did, but I don't fully understand why the 18,900-node forest didn't do at least a little
  better" is exactly on track — that's Lesson 2's point landing, not a gap in understanding. Log
  `struggle-productive`, not `blocked`.
- **Signal — blocked struggle.** A learner who cannot articulate *which number* in their own memo
  drove the verdict, even after the memo is written, has produced a fluent document without a
  judgment trail. This is the pattern the assessment is built to catch, and it should be surfaced
  before the exam, not discovered at grading. **Reroute:** have them explain the memo aloud,
  pointing at the specific table cell or CI that changed their mind at each rung.
- **Accelerate.** A learner who nails Lab A's stopping-point prediction *and* correctly predicts Lab
  B's "the forest won't beat the table by more than 0.05 days" before running any code has
  internalized the ladder's logic well enough to skip re-deriving Lab C's ROI calculator from
  scratch — hand them a partially completed one and let them spend the saved time entirely on the
  "what would change my mind" sensitivity analysis, which is where the real judgment lives.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — the judgment to not build, and what it costs the person who
makes that call — not the course-level ethics thread restated.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** The capacity-constrained, low-base-rate arithmetic in Lesson 3 is
  not a hypothetical: outreach programs, fraud-triage queues, and content-moderation escalation
  systems are routinely capacity-constrained in exactly this shape, and the breakeven-precision
  calculation this module teaches is a standard, computable check that most organizations do not run
  before building. This is a documented, mechanical fact about how these systems fail economically,
  not an opinion about whether ML is good or bad.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "AI can solve this" pitches suppress that shipping the
  impressive system is frequently rewarded independent of whether the arithmetic supports it — a
  data scientist's promotion case and a vendor's contract renewal both credit "we built the ML
  platform" more reliably than "we correctly showed the platform wouldn't pay for itself," so the
  organizational incentive gradient runs opposite to this module's discipline more often than
  either side of the AI-hype debate likes to admit. And the opposite failure is just as real:
  blanket "AI is overhyped" critiques suppress the packets — like this module's own rung-3 scenario —
  where the complex model's gain is genuine, large, and worth the cost, and where walking away on
  principle would be the wrong call, not the responsible one. Neither boosterism nor blanket
  skepticism runs the ladder; only the ladder does.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** At some point in your career you will be the only person in the room
  who ran the number showing the platform doesn't pay for itself — while the roadmap, the vendor
  contract already under negotiation, and your manager's OKR all assume it does. The arithmetic is
  not in question; the room's appetite for it is. What do you owe the room when the honest
  recommendation is the unwelcome one, and what do you owe the version of yourself who built this
  module's discipline specifically so you'd be able to make that call?

## Module overview

M05 built the wall between what a model is fit on and what it is graded on, and gave you the tools
to catch a breach: a leaked column, a bad split, a baseline you never checked. This module starts
from the other side of that wall, standing on solid ground: assume the split is honest, the
baseline is fair, nothing leaked. The number in front of you is real. **It can still lie to you —
not by being wrong, but by being true and misleading at the same time.** That is a stranger, harder
claim than "the pipeline has a bug," and it is the one this module spends sixteen hours making
concrete, four separate times, with four different mechanisms, none of which involve broken code.

M05 already named leakage as one entry in this catalog and handed you the tool for it — "a score
that's too good is itself evidence" is a muscle you already built there, not new material. This
module does not reopen leakage. It completes the remaining four ways an honestly-computed number
can still deceive you: **Simpson's paradox** (Lesson 1) — an aggregate comparison reverses sign the
moment you stratify it, because the groups you're comparing were never balanced the way the
aggregate silently assumed. **Base-rate neglect** (Lesson 2) — a test or classifier's own accuracy
figures (sensitivity, specificity) are real and correctly computed, and still tell you almost
nothing about what a single positive result means, once you account for how rare the thing you're
looking for actually is. **Distribution shift** (Lesson 3) — the exact same "drift" M05 pointed
toward by name — a model validated honestly, on a genuinely held-out slice of the same population
it was trained on, collapses the moment it meets a population it was never shown. **The rare-class
accuracy illusion** (Lesson 4) — the same base-rate mechanism from Lesson 2, now applied not to a
single test result but to an entire trained classifier's headline metric, where "accuracy" turns
out to measure almost nothing when the thing you care about is rare.

Every lesson in this module follows the same discipline the course has run since M01: you are
handed something that looks finished — a table, a diagnosis, a trained model, a validation report
— and your job is not to build it, it is to **audit** it, using only the data, before being told
what (if anything) is wrong. This is a deliberate inversion of every lab in M02–M05, where you built
the estimator. Here, the estimator is a given. What you build instead are the four diagnostic
functions — a confound-reversal detector, a Bayes-correct PPV/NPV calculator, a train-vs-deployment
slice comparator, and a threshold-aware confusion-matrix auditor — that a working ML engineer
actually reaches for when a number looks a little too clean. Those functions, not a new estimator,
are this module's from-scratch artifact.

The descent: watch a real, historically documented aggregate reverse sign under stratification and
derive *why*, algebraically, from a weighted-average identity (Lesson 1); watch a "90% accurate"
test become almost worthless the moment you multiply by how rare the condition actually is, and
learn that the false-positive flood, not the sensitivity headline, is what actually governs trust in
a positive result (Lesson 2); train a model honestly on one population, validate it honestly on a
held-out slice of *that same population*, and watch it fail on a different population it was never
shown, with no bug anywhere in the code (Lesson 3); and watch a classifier's "98% accurate" get
exposed as mathematically indistinguishable from a classifier that never predicts anything at all
(Lesson 4). Re-ascent: you can read "94% accurate" or "we found four segments" or "validated on held
out data" the way M05 taught you to read "leak-free" — not as a green light, but as an incomplete
sentence, and you now know exactly which four questions finish it.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Simpson's paradox: when the aggregate and every subgroup disagree

**Teaching text**

An aggregate rate is a weighted average. That is the entire mechanism behind this lesson, and it is
worth writing out in full before looking at any data, because the paradox is not a statistical
oddity — it is this identity, applied to a case where the weights themselves carry information:

$$\text{rate}_{\text{overall}} = \sum_{s} w_s \cdot \text{rate}_s, \qquad w_s = \frac{n_s}{\sum_{s'} n_{s'}}$$

where $s$ ranges over strata (departments, hospitals, time periods — any way of splitting the
population into groups) and $w_s$ is the share of that group's total sample living in stratum $s$.
**Simpson's paradox occurs when two groups being compared have different weight vectors $w_s$
across the *same* strata, and the strata differ systematically in their own rates.** If group A's
population is concentrated in high-rate strata and group B's is concentrated in low-rate strata,
A's aggregate can exceed B's aggregate even if B's rate is equal to or higher than A's rate in
*every single stratum* — because the aggregate is not comparing "how each group does," it is
comparing two *differently weighted* averages of stratum rates, and weighting is doing real,
number-moving work the aggregate number never discloses.

**Worked example — six-department admissions, the classic case (after Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell,
1975, *Science* 187).** The table below reproduces the six-largest-department subset from the
widely cited 1973 UC Berkeley graduate admissions analysis — admit counts rounded to the nearest
whole applicant from the originally published per-department rates.

| Dept | Male applicants | Male admitted | Male rate | Female applicants | Female admitted | Female rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 825 | 512 | 62.06% | 108 | 89 | 82.41% |
| B | 560 | 353 | 63.04% | 25 | 17 | 68.00% |
| C | 325 | 120 | 36.92% | 593 | 202 | 34.06% |
| D | 417 | 138 | 33.09% | 375 | 131 | 34.93% |
| E | 191 | 53 | 27.75% | 393 | 94 | 23.92% |
| F | 373 | 22 | 5.90% | 341 | 24 | 7.04% |
| **Total** | **2691** | **1198** | **44.52%** | **1835** | **557** | **30.35%** |

Read only the totals row and the conclusion looks unambiguous: men were admitted at 44.52%, women
at 30.35% — a 14-point gap. Now read the department rows: women were admitted at an equal or higher
rate than men in **four of six departments** (A, B, D, F) — in A by a full 20 points. Men out-admit
women in only two departments (C, E), and by smaller margins (2.9 and 3.8 points) than several of
the departments running the other way. **The aggregate says "men favored." A 4-out-of-6 majority of
the departments says "women favored, if anything."** Both statements are computed correctly from
the same rows of data. Neither is a mistake.

The mechanism is the weight vector. Compute what share of each gender's *total* applicant pool went
to each department:

| Dept | Overall admit rate | Male applicant share | Female applicant share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 64.4% | 30.7% | 5.9% |
| B | 63.2% | 20.8% | 1.4% |
| C | 35.1% | 12.1% | 32.3% |
| D | 34.0% | 15.5% | 20.4% |
| E | 25.2% | 7.1% | 21.4% |
| F | 6.4% | 13.9% | 18.6% |

Men concentrated over half their applications (30.7% + 20.8% = 51.5%) in departments A and B — the
two *easiest* departments to get into (64.4% and 63.2% overall admit rates). Women concentrated
nearly three-quarters of their applications (32.3% + 20.4% + 21.4% = 74.1%) in departments C, D, and
E — mid-to-competitive departments. Department choice, not department-level bias, is doing almost
all the work in the aggregate gap: women weren't admitted less often *within* departments (they
mostly weren't) — they disproportionately applied to the departments that admit *everyone*, of
every gender, less often. The aggregate number silently averages over a confound (department) that
correlates with both the outcome (admit rate) and the group (gender), and reporting only the
aggregate erases the one piece of information — the weight vector — that explains the whole
reversal.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"Simpson's paradox is a rare statistical curiosity, unlikely to come up in real data."** It
  requires only two ordinary conditions: group composition differs across a stratifier, and the
  stratifier correlates with the outcome. Hiring funnels stratified by department or level,
  clinical trials stratified by disease severity, and A/B tests stratified by traffic source
  reproduce this pattern routinely — the six-department table above is a real, published example,
  not a constructed toy.
- **"Once you see the reversal, you now know which number — aggregate or per-stratum — is 'the
  truth.'"** Neither is automatically the right comparison; which one answers your actual question
  is a *causal* choice, not a statistical one. "Are individual departments biased against women who
  apply to them?" is answered by the per-department rates. "Are women, in aggregate, less likely to
  end up admitted to graduate school at this university?" is answered by the aggregate rate — and
  is *also true* even though no department is biased, because department choice itself is not
  independent of gender in this data. Reporting only one of the two numbers, without naming which
  question it answers, is the actual failure mode.
- **"If both groups do about as well in most sub-groups, the aggregate has to reflect that,
  roughly."** The weighted-average identity says otherwise, exactly, and the six-department table is
  the counterexample: a 4-of-6 stratum-level advantage for women coexists with a 14-point aggregate
  disadvantage. There is no informal intuition that reliably predicts this from "most strata agree"
  — you have to compute the weights.
- **"A machine learning model fit on this data would discover the confound automatically."** Only if
  the confounding variable (department) is actually a feature the model sees. A model trained on
  `gender → admitted` alone, with no department column, cannot discover or correct for a confound it
  was never shown; it will faithfully learn the aggregate 14-point relationship and report it with
  full confidence, because from inside the model, department was never a variable at all. Omitting a
  confounding feature is a modeling decision, not a fact about the world, and it is invisible in the
  model's own reported performance.

### Lesson 2 — Base-rate neglect: what a positive result actually means

**Teaching text**

A screening test (or a classifier's positive prediction) is described by two numbers that never
mention how common the thing being detected is: **sensitivity** ($P(\text{test+} \mid \text{condition})$,
the true positive rate) and **specificity** ($P(\text{test-} \mid \text{no condition})$, the true
negative rate). The number almost everyone actually wants when they see a positive result is a third
quantity — **positive predictive value**, $P(\text{condition} \mid \text{test+})$ — and Bayes'
theorem is unambiguous that this third number depends on a fourth quantity neither sensitivity nor
specificity contains: the **prevalence**, $P(\text{condition})$, the base rate.

$$\text{PPV} = P(\text{condition}\mid\text{test+}) = \frac{P(\text{test+}\mid\text{condition})\,P(\text{condition})}{P(\text{test+}\mid\text{condition})\,P(\text{condition}) + P(\text{test+}\mid\neg\text{condition})\,P(\neg\text{condition})} = \frac{\text{sens}\cdot\text{prev}}{\text{sens}\cdot\text{prev} + (1-\text{spec})(1-\text{prev})}$$

**Base-rate neglect** is the specific, well-documented error of reading sensitivity or "accuracy" as
if it were PPV — treating "the test is 90% accurate" as "a positive result is 90% likely to be
correct." Those are different claims, and for a rare condition, the gap between them is not a
rounding error — it can be the difference between "almost certainly true" and "more likely wrong
than right."

**Worked example.** A screening test has sensitivity 90%, specificity 90%, applied to a population
where the condition's true prevalence is 1%. Take a population of $N=10{,}000$ and build the
confusion matrix directly from the rates:

| | Condition present (100) | Condition absent (9,900) |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Test positive** | TP = 90 | FP = 990 |
| **Test negative** | FN = 10 | TN = 8,910 |

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{90}{90+990} = \frac{90}{1080} = 8.33\%, \qquad \text{NPV} = \frac{8910}{8910+10} = 99.89\%$$

A test correctly described as "90% accurate" in both directions produces a positive result that is
**right only about one time in twelve.** The other eleven of every twelve positive results are false
alarms — not because the test is broken, but because the condition is rare enough that the 10% false
positive rate, applied to the enormous pool of 9,900 healthy people, manufactures far more false
positives (990) than the 90% true positive rate finds real cases (90) in the tiny pool of 100
diseased people. **The false-positive flood from the large healthy population dominates the small
number of true positives from the rare condition — this is the entire mechanism, and it is pure
arithmetic, not a property of any specific test.**

This has a sharp, checkable consequence: **specificity, not sensitivity, is what governs PPV when
prevalence is low**, because specificity controls the size of that flood ($FP = (1-\text{spec})
\times \text{healthy count}$, and the healthy count dominates the population). Hold sensitivity
fixed at 90% and raise specificity from 90% to 99%:

$$\text{FP} = 9900 \times (1-0.99) = 99, \qquad \text{PPV} = \frac{90}{90+99} = \frac{90}{189} = 47.62\%$$

A 9-point improvement in specificity **almost sextupled** PPV (8.33% → 47.62%), while the identical
9-point improvement applied to sensitivity instead would only ever move TP from 90 toward 99 — a
change that cannot, by itself, come close to that swing, because TP was never the dominant term in
the denominator. The full picture, sweeping prevalence at fixed sensitivity/specificity = 90%/90%:

| Prevalence | 0.1% | 1% | 5% | 10% | 30% | 50% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| PPV | 0.89% | 8.33% | 32.14% | 50.00% | 79.41% | 90.00% |

The same "90% accurate" test means "almost certainly a false alarm" at 0.1% prevalence and "probably
correct" at 50% prevalence — **prevalence is not a footnote to this calculation, it is the dominant
input**, and reading "90% accurate" without asking "accurate against what background rate" is
exactly the neglect this lesson names.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A 90% accurate test means a positive result has a 90% chance of being right."** This conflates
  sensitivity/specificity with PPV. The worked example's 8.33% is the direct, arithmetic refutation:
  the same "90% accurate" description is compatible with a positive result that's right one time in
  twelve, once the base rate is this low.
- **"'Accuracy' is a single number that fully describes how useful a test is."** Sensitivity and
  specificity can each individually be 90% — genuinely good numbers — and still combine, at low
  prevalence, into a test whose positive results are mostly noise. A single blended "accuracy"
  figure hides exactly which of the two error types is dominating, and at low prevalence, false
  positives dominate by construction.
- **"Improving sensitivity is the most impactful lever for a rare-condition screening test."** The
  9-point specificity improvement moved PPV nearly 6×; a symmetric sensitivity improvement could not
  have, because sensitivity only ever adjusts the numerator's TP term, while specificity adjusts the
  FP term that is competing against a much larger healthy population. For rare conditions, chasing
  specificity is usually the higher-leverage fix — this is a real, checkable asymmetry, not a rule
  of thumb.
- **"Base-rate neglect is a human cognitive bias — it doesn't affect code."** It affects any
  automated system that reports a raw model score (or applies a fixed decision threshold) without
  accounting for the deployment population's actual positive rate. Lesson 4 is this exact mechanism,
  wearing a different name, applied to a trained classifier instead of a medical test — the neglect
  is encoded the moment someone reads "98% accurate" off a model card without asking what fraction
  of the deployment population is actually positive.

### Lesson 3 — Distribution shift: honestly validated, still wrong

**Teaching text**

M05 established the evaluation contract: fit on train, grade on a genuinely held-out set, and the
reported score is a trustworthy estimate of performance **on the distribution that held-out set was
drawn from.** That clause is easy to drop and impossible to ignore once you've seen it fail. A model
can pass M05's entire contract — no leakage, an honest split, a fair baseline — and still collapse
in production, because the held-out set and the deployment population are not always the same
distribution, even when both are "real, unseen data" in the informal sense a validation report
usually means.

Three distinct mechanisms travel under the umbrella term "distribution shift," and they are not
interchangeable:

- **Covariate shift** — $P(X)$ changes between train and deployment, but the true relationship
  $P(Y\mid X)$ does not. A model that has correctly learned $P(Y\mid X)$ can, in principle, still
  perform well under pure covariate shift — it just sees a different mix of inputs.
- **Label shift** — $P(Y)$ changes (the base rate of the outcome itself shifts) while $P(X\mid Y)$
  stays fixed.
- **Concept drift** — $P(Y\mid X)$ itself changes: the *relationship* a model learned is no longer
  true in the new population. This is the mechanism that breaks a correctly-fit model outright, and
  no amount of "the model generalizes well" from validation protects against it, because validation
  only ever tests against the training distribution's own $P(Y\mid X)$.

**Worked example — a churn model trained on a non-representative slice.** A team has churn labels
only for customers in their first six months (the cohort with fast enough turnover to have observed
outcomes) and trains a model on that slice alone — a realistic, common data-availability constraint,
not a mistake. Two features: `onboarding_score` (0–1, how well the customer's initial setup went)
and `recent_price_increase` (0/1). The **true** generating relationship is different in the two
tenure populations: for new customers, churn is driven mostly by onboarding quality, and price
increases are rare (5% incidence) and weakly connected to churn in that population. For tenured
customers (6+ months), onboarding's effect has washed out — everyone is long past onboarding — and
churn is now driven mostly by whether they experienced a recent price increase (30% incidence, a
strong effect). This is concept drift: the *coefficient*, not just the input mix, genuinely differs
between populations.

A logistic regression trained only on 4,000 new-customer rows and validated on 1,000 **held-out
new-customer rows** — a completely honest M05-style split, no leakage — reports:

| | Accuracy | AUC | True churn rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Validation (new customers, held out) | 69.1% | 0.738 | 46.2% |

This looks like a reasonable, shippable churn model. Fitted coefficients:
`onboarding_score = -2.96`, `recent_price_increase = +0.55`. The team ships it to score the *entire*
customer base, which is overwhelmingly tenured. On 1,000 tenured customers:

| | Accuracy | AUC | True churn rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Deployed on tenured customers | **55.3%** | **0.607** | 31.2% |
| *Always predict "no churn" (trivial baseline)* | *68.8%* | *—* | *—* |

**The deployed model is worse than doing nothing.** A baseline that never even looks at a single
feature — always guess "no churn" — beats it by 13.5 accuracy points. Nothing crashed. The
validation number (69.1%, honestly computed, no leakage) was completely real, and it measured
exactly what it claimed to measure: performance on new customers. It was never a claim about
tenured customers, and the moment it was read as a general "this model works" statement, it started
lying by omission. The mechanism is visible in the coefficient: the model learned
`recent_price_increase = +0.55` because in its *training* population that feature was rare (5%
incidence) and only weakly predictive — it had almost no signal to learn a stronger relationship
from. In the tenured population, the *true* effect is roughly 4–5× stronger, and the model, having
never seen enough of that signal to learn it, systematically underweights the single feature that
now matters most.

The fix is not "collect more data" in the naive sense — more new-customer data would sharpen the
model's estimate of the new-customer relationship and do nothing for the tenured relationship it has
never observed. Retraining on a representative blend of both slices (2,000 new + 2,000 tenured)
changes the picture entirely: `recent_price_increase`'s coefficient rises to `+1.49` (nearly 3× its
old value), and evaluated on the same tenured deployment slice: accuracy **78.1%**, AUC **0.767**,
churn recall **66.3%** (versus 58.7% for the original model) — beating the 68.8% baseline instead of
losing to it. **The data had to represent the deployment population; more of the same non-
representative data could never have fixed this.**

**Named misconceptions**

- **"High, stable validation accuracy across folds means the model is ready to deploy."** Validation
  — even honest, leak-free, M05-compliant validation — only ever certifies performance on the
  distribution the training data came from. It certifies nothing about a population that
  distribution never included, no matter how many folds you check it on.
- **"A model that scores below a trivial baseline must have a bug."** Not necessarily. The 55.3%
  model has no bug — it is the exact, correct output of fitting logistic regression to the data it
  was given. It is wrong not because the code is wrong, but because the population it now faces is
  not the population it learned from. "Worse than baseline" is real, informative evidence about
  distribution shift, not automatically evidence of broken code.
- **"If the inputs look different, the relationship must have changed too" (conflating covariate
  shift with concept drift).** These are separable claims. Pure covariate shift — different mix of
  inputs, same underlying relationship — does not necessarily break a model that learned the true
  relationship. The churn example has real concept drift (the coefficient itself is different), which
  is why it breaks; a model facing only covariate shift with a stable relationship can, in principle,
  hold up far better, and telling the two apart requires checking whether $P(Y\mid X)$ actually
  changed, not just whether $P(X)$ did.
- **"More training data from the same source will fix distribution shift."** More new-customer rows
  would only ever sharpen an estimate of a relationship that was never the one needed. The fix
  demonstrated above is representative data, not more data — a distinction with a specific, measured
  consequence (78.1% vs. 55.3%) once you actually run both.

### Lesson 4 — The rare-class accuracy illusion

**Teaching text**

Lesson 2 showed that a test's own headline numbers can radically mislead once the base rate is
factored in. This lesson applies the identical arithmetic to a trained classifier's single most
commonly reported metric: **accuracy.** For a binary classifier facing a positive rate $p$, a
classifier that predicts "negative" for every single row — one that has learned literally nothing —
achieves:

$$\text{accuracy}_{\text{trivial}} = 1 - p$$

**Any trained model must clear this number just to prove it learned something at all**, and for a
rare positive class, $1-p$ is already very close to 100%. Worse: because standard training
procedures minimize an *aggregate* loss (log-loss, averaged over every row, exactly the
population-weighted-average mechanism from M04's ethics reading), a model trained on heavily
imbalanced data can and does converge toward always predicting the majority class — not as a bug,
but because that region of parameter space genuinely has low *aggregate* loss when the minority
class is rare enough. The training objective and the accuracy metric share the same blind spot, for
the same underlying reason.

**Worked example — a fraud classifier, fully honest, fully useless.** A logistic regression is
trained on 14,000 transactions (2% true fraud rate) and evaluated on a genuinely held-out,
stratified test set of 6,000 transactions containing 110 fraud cases (1.83%). The model reports:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Accuracy | **98.17%** |
| Precision (fraud) | 0.000 |
| Recall (fraud) | 0.000 |
| F1 (fraud) | 0.000 |

Confusion matrix at the default 0.5 threshold:

| | Predicted not-fraud | Predicted fraud |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Actually not-fraud** | 5,890 | 0 |
| **Actually fraud** | 110 | 0 |

The model predicts **"not fraud" for every single one of the 6,000 test transactions**, including
every one of the 110 fraud cases. Its accuracy — 98.17% — is not approximately equal to the trivial
always-negative baseline's accuracy. It is **exactly, bit-for-bit equal to it**, because the
model's predictions *are*, element for element, identical to the trivial baseline's predictions.
`np.array_equal(model_predictions, baseline_predictions)` returns `True`. A dashboard reporting
"98.17% accuracy — ship it" is not wrong about the number. It is silent about the fact that the
number was achieved by learning to do nothing.

Sweeping the decision threshold down from the default 0.5 recovers real information the accuracy
number was hiding:

| Threshold | Precision | Recall | F1 | Accuracy |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.50 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 98.2% |
| 0.30 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 98.2% |
| 0.20 | 0.111 | 0.009 | 0.017 | 98.1% |
| 0.10 | 0.225 | 0.209 | 0.217 | 97.2% |
| 0.05 | 0.120 | 0.309 | 0.173 | 94.6% |

At threshold 0.05, accuracy has *fallen* nearly four points, to 94.6% — and recall on the class that
actually matters has risen from 0% to 30.9%, catching roughly one in three fraud cases the original
"98.17% accurate" model caught zero of. **A lower accuracy number here represents a strictly more
useful fraud detector.** Accuracy going down is not automatically bad news; it depends entirely on
which errors the number is trading against which, and accuracy — by design — cannot express that
trade, because it weights a missed fraud case and a false fraud alarm identically, when in almost
every real deployment they cost wildly different amounts.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"98% accuracy means the model is doing its job."** The worked example is the direct
  counterexample: 98.17% accuracy, achieved by a model that flags zero fraud cases, mathematically
  indistinguishable from a model that was never trained.
- **"If a model beats the trivial baseline's accuracy at all, it has learned something about the
  rare class."** A model can nudge accuracy fractionally above $1-p$ by correctly reclassifying a
  handful of easy majority-class points while still having near-zero recall on the class that
  matters — beating the baseline on accuracy is necessary but nowhere near sufficient evidence of
  minority-class competence; check precision/recall/F1 for the class you actually care about
  directly.
- **"The right fix for class imbalance is to rebalance the training data (oversampling, SMOTE, class
  weights) — end of story."** Rebalancing changes what the *training* loss optimizes for, and can
  genuinely help. It does not answer a separate, still-required question: which metric do you report
  and which threshold do you deploy at? Even a perfectly rebalanced model still needs
  precision/recall/F1-based evaluation and a deliberately chosen operating threshold — "I rebalanced
  the data" and "I evaluate correctly" are two different fixes, and doing only the first while still
  reporting accuracy leaves this lesson's exact trap in place.
- **"A model with lower accuracy than another model is strictly worse."** The threshold sweep is the
  direct, measured counterexample: accuracy fell from 98.2% to 94.6% while fraud recall rose from 0%
  to 30.9%. Whether that trade is "better" or "worse" depends entirely on what the deployment needs
  to catch and what each error type costs — a question accuracy is structurally unable to answer,
  because it treats every misclassification as equally bad regardless of which class it belongs to.

## Labs

Every lab hands you something that reports a clean, unremarkable number — a comparison, a screening
claim, a validated model, a shipped classifier — and your Predict step commits, in writing, to a
falsifiable claim about what a proper audit will find, **before** you run the diagnostic that finds
it. This is the whole discipline of the module in miniature, run four times.

### Lab 1 — A confound-reversal detector, verified on a real dataset and a synthetic control

**Predict** (on paper, before touching code)

1. You will be shown a second, structurally similar dataset before writing any detector: a
   two-treatment (`A`, `B`) success-rate comparison, stratified by case severity (`small`,

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def build_admissions_table() -> pd.DataFrame:
    depts = ["A", "B", "C", "D", "E", "F"]
    male_app  = np.array([825, 560, 325, 417, 191, 373])
    male_rate = np.array([0.62, 0.63, 0.37, 0.33, 0.28, 0.06])
    female_app  = np.array([108, 25, 593, 375, 393, 341])
    female_rate = np.array([0.82, 0.68, 0.34, 0.35, 0.24, 0.07])
    male_admit = np.round(male_app * male_rate).astype(int)
    female_admit = np.round(female_app * female_rate).astype(int)

    rows = []
    for i, d in enumerate(depts):
        rows.append({"dept": d, "group": "male", "applicants": male_app[i], "admitted": male_admit[i]})
        rows.append({"dept": d, "group": "female", "applicants": female_app[i], "admitted": female_admit[i]})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def overall_rate(df: pd.DataFrame, group: str) -> float:
    sub = df[df["group"] == group]
    return sub["admitted"].sum() / sub["applicants"].sum()

def stratified_rates(df: pd.DataFrame, group: str) -> pd.Series:
    sub = df[df["group"] == group].set_index("dept")
    return sub["admitted"] / sub["applicants"]

def detect_confound_reversal(df: pd.DataFrame, group_a: str, group_b: str) -> dict:
    overall_a, overall_b = overall_rate(df, group_a), overall_rate(df, group_b)
    overall_winner = group_a if overall_a > overall_b else group_b

    rates_a = stratified_rates(df, group_a)
    rates_b = stratified_rates(df, group_b)
    strata_winners = {
        stratum: (group_a if rates_a[stratum] >= rates_b[stratum] else group_b)
        for stratum in rates_a.index
    }
    n_a_wins = sum(1 for w in strata_winners.values() if w == group_a)
    n_b_wins = len(strata_winners) - n_a_wins
    strata_majority_winner = group_a if n_a_wins > n_b_wins else group_b

    return {
        "overall_rate_a": overall_a, "overall_rate_b": overall_b,
        "overall_winner": overall_winner,
        "strata_winners": strata_winners,
        "strata_majority_winner": strata_majority_winner,
        "n_strata_a_wins": n_a_wins, "n_strata_b_wins": n_b_wins,
        "is_reversal": overall_winner != strata_majority_winner,
    }
```

```python
def confusion_from_rates(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, n=10000):
    diseased = n * prevalence
    healthy = n * (1 - prevalence)
    return {
        "TP": diseased * sensitivity,
        "FN": diseased * (1 - sensitivity),
        "FP": healthy * (1 - specificity),
        "TN": healthy * specificity,
    }

def ppv(cm):
    return cm["TP"] / (cm["TP"] + cm["FP"])

def npv(cm):
    return cm["TN"] / (cm["TN"] + cm["FN"])

def sensitivity_from_confusion(cm):
    return cm["TP"] / (cm["TP"] + cm["FN"])

def specificity_from_confusion(cm):
    return cm["TN"] / (cm["TN"] + cm["FP"])

def ppv_table(prevalences, sensitivity, specificity):
    return {
        p: ppv(confusion_from_rates(p, sensitivity, specificity))
        for p in prevalences
    }
```

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, roc_auc_score, precision_score, recall_score

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

def make_customers(n, tenure_group, rng):
    onboarding_score = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
    if tenure_group == "new":
        recent_price_increase = rng.binomial(1, 0.05, n).astype(float)
        logit = 3.0 * (0.5 - onboarding_score) + 0.3 * recent_price_increase - 0.2
    elif tenure_group == "tenured":
        recent_price_increase = rng.binomial(1, 0.30, n).astype(float)
        logit = 0.2 * (0.5 - onboarding_score) + 2.5 * recent_price_increase - 1.6
    else:
        raise ValueError(tenure_group)
    churn = rng.binomial(1, sigmoid(logit))
    X = np.column_stack([onboarding_score, recent_price_increase])
    return X, churn

def build_lab3_data(seed=201):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X_train, y_train = make_customers(4000, "new", rng)
    X_val, y_val = make_customers(1000, "new", rng)
    X_deploy, y_deploy = make_customers(1000, "tenured", rng)
    model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_train, y_train)
    return model, (X_train, y_train), (X_val, y_val), (X_deploy, y_deploy)

def build_representative_comparison(seed=201):
    """Same rng STREAM as build_lab3_data (train/val/deploy drawn first, identically),
    then continues it to build a representative NEW+TENURED blend and fit a second
    model -- so the deploy slice and the new-customer-only model are byte-for-byte
    the same as build_lab3_data's, and only the retraining data differs."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X_train, y_train = make_customers(4000, "new", rng)
    X_val, y_val = make_customers(1000, "new", rng)
    X_deploy, y_deploy = make_customers(1000, "tenured", rng)
    base_model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_train, y_train)

    X_mix_new, y_mix_new = make_customers(2000, "new", rng)
    X_mix_ten, y_mix_ten = make_customers(2000, "tenured", rng)
    X_mix = np.vstack([X_mix_new, X_mix_ten])
    y_mix = np.concatenate([y_mix_new, y_mix_ten])
    mix_model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_mix, y_mix)

    return base_model, mix_model, (X_deploy, y_deploy)

def evaluate_on_slice(model, X, y):
    pred = model.predict(X)
    proba = model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
    return {
        "accuracy": accuracy_score(y, pred),
        "auc": roc_auc_score(y, proba),
        "precision": precision_score(y, pred, zero_division=0),
        "recall": recall_score(y, pred, zero_division=0),
    }

def baseline_accuracy(y):
    majority_class = 1 if np.mean(y) > 0.5 else 0
    return float(np.mean(y == majority_class))

def feature_shift_report(X_train, X_deploy, feature_names):
    report = {}
    for i, name in enumerate(feature_names):
        report[name] = {
            "train_mean": float(X_train[:, i].mean()),
            "train_std": float(X_train[:, i].std()),
            "deploy_mean": float(X_deploy[:, i].mean()),
            "deploy_std": float(X_deploy[:, i].std()),
        }
    return report
```

Solution (`lab4_solution.py`):

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

def build_lab4_data(seed=201, n=20000, p_fraud=0.02):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    is_fraud = rng.binomial(1, p_fraud, n)
    transaction_amount_z = rng.normal(0, 1, n) + is_fraud * 0.6
    odd_hour = rng.binomial(1, 0.05 + is_fraud * 0.25, n)
    X = np.column_stack([transaction_amount_z, odd_hour])
    y = is_fraud
    X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
        X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=seed, stratify=y
    )
    model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_train, y_train)
    return model, (X_train, y_train), (X_test, y_test)

def confusion_counts(y_true, y_pred):
    y_true = np.asarray(y_true); y_pred = np.asarray(y_pred)
    return {
        "TN": int(np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 0))),
        "FP": int(np.sum((y_true == 0) & (y_pred == 1))),
        "FN": int(np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 0))),
        "TP": int(np.sum((y_true == 1) & (y_pred == 1))),
    }

def precision_recall_f1(y_true, y_pred):
    cm = confusion_counts(y_true, y_pred)
    precision = cm["TP"] / (cm["TP"] + cm["FP"]) if (cm["TP"] + cm["FP"]) > 0 else 0.0
    recall = cm["TP"] / (cm["TP"] + cm["FN"]) if (cm["TP"] + cm["FN"]) > 0 else 0.0
    f1 = (2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall)) if (precision + recall) > 0 else 0.0
    return {"precision": precision, "recall": recall, "f1": f1}

def baseline_predictions(y_true):
    return np.zeros_like(np.asarray(y_true))

def identical_to_baseline(pred, baseline_pred):
    return bool(np.array_equal(np.asarray(pred), np.asarray(baseline_pred)))

def threshold_sweep(model, X, y, thresholds):
    proba = model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
    y = np.asarray(y)
    results = {}
    for t in thresholds:
        pred = (proba >= t).astype(int)
        prf = precision_recall_f1(y, pred)
        prf["accuracy"] = float(np.mean(pred == y))
        results[t] = prf
    return results
```

**The wall: "which number is 'the truth,' the aggregate or the per-department rate?"** Learners want

**The wall: "precision/recall/F1 being exactly 0.0 must be a bug in my metric code" (Lab 4).**

## Three-tier reading — the number that is true and still lying

**Tier 1 — material ground.** Every number in this module is real arithmetic, verifiable by anyone
who runs the code: 44.52% vs. 30.35% is the correct pooled rate from the department table; 8.33% is
the correct PPV from Bayes' theorem given those inputs; 98.17% is the correct accuracy of a model
that predicts "not fraud" for every row. None of these numbers are wrong, miscalculated, or the
product of a bug. Real deployed systems — hiring funnels, medical screening programs, churn models,
fraud detectors — report numbers exactly this shape, exactly this correctly computed, every day. That
these numbers can still mislead is not a critique of arithmetic; it is a fact about what a single
summary statistic can and cannot carry, and it is checkable, not a matter of opinion.

**Tier 2 — what "the number checks out" suppresses.** A validated, leak-free, honestly-computed
metric (M05's entire contract, satisfied) still only ever describes performance averaged over
whatever population and whatever stratification you chose to report it at. This module's four
lessons are four different ways that averaging quietly discards the information that would have
changed the decision: which department someone applied to, how rare the condition actually is, which
population the model will actually meet, which class the "accuracy" number is secretly indifferent
to. This is the same mechanism M04's Tier 2 named for PCA and k-means — a population-weighted
average privileges whoever is numerous or whichever comparison is easiest to compute — now shown
four more times, in settings that have nothing to do with clustering or eigenvectors, because it is
not a property of any one algorithm. It is a property of averaging itself, and averaging is nearly
every metric anyone reports.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** Every failure mode in this module produced a model or a claim that was,
by its own reported number, fine. Nothing crashed. No test failed. The dashboard was green, the
model card said "validated," the accuracy was in the nineties. The only reason any of the four ever
got caught in this module is that a specific diagnostic — a stratified-rate comparison, a Bayes-
correct PPV, a slice-vs-slice audit, a threshold-aware confusion matrix — was already known, already
built, and someone deliberately ran it. You now have four such diagnostics. The failure catalog is
not exhaustive; it was never claimed to be. Somewhere in a system you ship is a failure mode this
module didn't name, hiding behind a number that is, technically, true — on a stratification nobody
thought to check, for a subgroup nobody thought to disaggregate, using a metric nobody thought to
question this quarter, because the deadline was real and the dashboard was green. What is the
question you are not yet in the habit of asking, and who is on the other side of the number the day
it turns out you needed to?

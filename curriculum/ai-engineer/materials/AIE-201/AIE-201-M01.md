## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Learning is a bet, not an understanding

**Teaching text.**

Start with the sentence everyone says: "the model learns the pattern." Descend beneath it. What actually happens is narrower and more mechanical: you pick a family of functions, you pick a loss that scores how wrong a function is on a specific finite set of examples, and you search the family for the function that scores lowest on *that set*. Nothing in this process ever touches "the pattern" in any general sense — it touches the **training set**, which is a finite, particular, already-collected sample.

Formalize it, because the informal version is where the trouble hides. Assume there is some true, unknown joint distribution $P(x, y)$ over inputs and labels — the "world" the model will eventually be judged against. What you actually want is a function $f$ that minimizes the **true risk**:

$$R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\big[\,\ell(f(x), y)\,\big]$$

You cannot compute this. You don't have $P$; you have a finite sample $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ drawn from it. So you minimize a stand-in, the **empirical risk**:

$$\hat{R}(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(x_i), y_i)$$

**Learning is the bet that the $f$ minimizing $\hat{R}$ also does well at minimizing $R$.** That bet is not automatically good. It is good exactly to the degree that two conditions hold: the training sample is drawn i.i.d. from the same distribution the model will face at deployment, and the sample is large/representative enough that $\hat{R}$ doesn't diverge wildly from $R$ for the functions your search considers. Every failure mode this course spends sixteen hours cataloging in M06 — leakage, drift, Simpson's paradox — is a way one of those two conditions quietly breaks while $\hat{R}$ keeps looking fine.

This is why "the model learns the pattern" is a dangerous sentence: it smuggles in the claim that the bet has already been checked and won. It hasn't. All training ever proves is that $\hat{R}$ went down. Whether $R$ went down with it is an empirical question you have to go answer separately — which is exactly what a held-out set is for, and exactly what this module's labs make you watch fail.

**Worked example.**

Ground truth (known to us, hidden from the fitting procedure): $f^*(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$ for $x \in [0,1]$, observed with Gaussian noise, $\sigma = 0.2$. This is a stand-in for "the world" — a real relationship plus irreducible measurement noise, exactly the shape of a real regression problem, just small enough to hold in your head.

Draw a training sample of $n=12$ points. Fit two polynomials to it by least squares (raw coefficients via the normal equations — no named algorithm, just "the line/curve through the points that minimizes squared error," which you already have the machinery for from AIE-103's optimization work): one of degree 2, one of degree 8.

| Model | Training MSE | Population MSE (2,000 fresh points, same generator) |
|---|---:|---:|
| degree 2 | 0.1622 | 0.2559 |
| degree 8 | 0.0013 | 1.5119 |

Read this straight, not through the training column. On paper — on the only evidence available at fit time — the degree-8 model is roughly **125× better** than the degree-2 model. Ship both, and in the world they're actually deployed against, the degree-8 model is roughly **6× worse**. The training column didn't lie, technically — it correctly reported the empirical risk of each model. What it could not tell you, and what nothing about the training process can ever tell you by itself, is whether empirical risk and true risk moved together. Here they moved in opposite directions. That's the whole module in one table.

**Named misconceptions.**

- **"Lower training error means a better model."** This is the single most consequential wrong belief in applied ML, and it is wrong precisely when it matters most — when a model has enough capacity to memorize idiosyncrasies of the sample rather than structure of the world. Training error is not even measuring the right quantity; it's a biased-downward estimate of true risk, and the bias gets worse as capacity goes up (Lesson 2 makes this exact).
- **"The model understands/learns the concept."** Reification. A fit model is a function selected to score well on a fixed finite objective. Whether that function's behavior generalizes to inputs it never saw is a separate, falsifiable claim — not a property that comes bundled with "training completed successfully." A program that merely runs, or merely drives training loss down, is never sufficient evidence of anything.
- **"More training always helps."** More *optimization* on the same empirical risk, with the same model family and the same sample, drives $\hat{R}$ down — and, past some point, drives $R$ up. "Training more" and "generalizing better" are different axes that happen to move together only in a specific, checkable regime (Lesson 2 names it).

### Lesson 2 — Capacity and the bias–variance decomposition

**Teaching text.**

**Capacity** is informally the size/richness of the function family your search is allowed to consider — how wiggly a curve it can draw, how many distinct patterns of behavior it can represent. A degree-1 polynomial has almost none: it can only be a line. A degree-20 polynomial has a great deal: it can wiggle through any 21 points exactly. Polynomial degree is a literal, turnable capacity knob, which is exactly why the labs use it — later in the course, tree depth, number of neighbors, and regularization strength will all turn out to be the same knob wearing a different name.

The mechanism connecting capacity to the table in Lesson 1 has an exact name and an exact algebraic form: the **bias–variance decomposition**. For squared-error loss, at any fixed input $x$, treating the training set itself as the random quantity (a new draw of $n$ points would give a slightly different fit $\hat{f}$ each time):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f^*(x)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}$$

Read each term as a question about *what would happen if you re-ran the whole training process on a fresh sample from the same world, over and over*:

- **Bias** — even averaged over infinitely many training sets, is the family's best-case fit systematically off from the truth? Low-capacity models can't represent the true shape no matter how much or what data they see; that's bias, and more data does not fix it.
- **Variance** — how much does the fit swing from one training sample to the next? High-capacity models chase the specific noise in whichever sample they happened to get, so a re-draw produces a visibly different function; that's variance, and it *is* fixed by more data (Lesson 4), or by constraining capacity.
- **Irreducible noise ($\sigma^2$)** — the floor. No model, no matter how well specified, beats this, because it's the actual randomness in $y$ given $x$. Chasing error below $\sigma^2$ is chasing noise, not signal — this is what overfitting *is*, stated in one sentence.

**Worked example.**

Same generator as Lesson 1 ($f^*(x)=\sin(2\pi x)$, $\sigma=0.2$, so the irreducible floor is $\sigma^2 = 0.04$). This time isolate bias and variance directly by simulation: redraw the training set (n=20) 500 times, refit at a fixed degree each time, and look at how the 500 resulting curves behave at a grid of test points.

| Degree | Bias² | Variance | Noise floor | Sum | Regime |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 0.1482 | 0.0244 | 0.04 | 0.213 | **underfit** — bias dominates |
| 3 | 0.0028 | 0.0101 | 0.04 | 0.053 | **near the sweet spot** — both small, close to the floor |
| 7 | 0.0014 | 1.9131 | 0.04 | 1.955 | **overfit** — variance dominates, by two orders of magnitude |

The "Sum" column is not decorative — it's the decomposition's promise, checked: it should equal (and does, up to simulation noise) the average population MSE you'd measure by the Lesson-1 method. Degree 3's total (0.053) sits barely above the $\sigma^2=0.04$ floor — there is very little room left to improve without more capacity *and* more data together (a preview of M02's regularization and M05's honest baselines). Degree 7's variance term is nearly 200× its bias term: the family can represent the truth just fine, but with only 20 points to pin down 8 coefficients, which particular curve it lands on is almost pure noise-chasing.

**Named misconceptions.**

- **"Bias here means the model is unfair."** No — this is *statistical* bias: a mismatch between a family's best achievable average fit and the truth. It is a property of a model family versus a data-generating process, computable with no reference to people or protected attributes. Model **fairness** is a different, real, important concept this course's ethics thread returns to — but conflating the two vocabulary words is a fast way to either trivialize fairness ("just reduce bias, i.e. use a bigger model") or miss it entirely ("my bias² is low, so my model is fair"). Neither follows.
- **"Zero bias and zero variance is the achievable goal."** It is not achievable for any finite sample and any imperfect-but-useful model family; the two terms trade off against each other as capacity moves, and the honest goal is minimizing their *sum*, not driving either one individually to zero. A model with literally zero bias and zero variance would have to already know $f^*$ exactly — there'd be nothing left to learn.
- **"More capacity is strictly safer — worst case it just fits the data as well as a simpler model would."** False, and the degree-7 row is the counterexample: the extra capacity did not sit idle when unneeded, it actively found noise to fit, and variance exploded. Capacity is not a one-directional dial you can over-set for free.

### Lesson 3 — No free lunch: why every algorithm bets on *something*

**Teaching text.**

Lesson 2 leaves an obvious question hanging: why not just always pick the capacity that minimizes the bias–variance sum for the problem at hand? Because you don't know, in advance, how much capacity the true function needs — and there is a real theorem (Wolpert's No Free Lunch theorem for supervised learning, stated informally here; the full statement belongs in a learning-theory course, not this one) explaining why no algorithm can dodge that ignorance in general: **averaged uniformly over every possible target function a learner could face, every algorithm — including random guessing — has the same expected performance.** An algorithm that beats random guessing on the problems you actually care about is not smarter in some absolute sense; it is *matched* to the structure those problems happen to have, and it would do correspondingly worse on problems without that structure.

The structure a learning algorithm assumes the world has is its **inductive bias**. Restricting a fit to low-degree polynomials assumes the truth is smooth and slowly-varying. Nearest-neighbor prediction assumes nearby inputs have similar outputs. A convolutional filter (AIE-202, not yet) assumes that translating a pattern in an image doesn't change what it is. None of these assumptions are free, and none are universally correct — each is a bet about what kind of world you're in, encoded before a single data point is seen.

This reframes Lesson 2 rather than replacing it: capacity is *one* axis of inductive bias (how flexible a function is allowed to be), but the *shape* of the bias matters independently of how much of it there is. A polynomial family and a Fourier-series family at the same capacity encode different assumptions about the target and will succeed or fail on different problems, even at matched flexibility.

**Worked example.**

Two different ground-truth functions, same noise level ($\sigma=0.15$), same sample size ($n=40$): a **smooth** target, one cycle over $[0,1]$, and a **wiggly** target, three cycles over the same interval. Fit both with a low-capacity (degree 3) and a higher-capacity (degree 9) polynomial family.

| Target | Degree | Train MSE | Population MSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| smooth (1 cycle) | 3 | 0.0166 | 0.0307 |
| smooth (1 cycle) | 9 | 0.0134 | 0.0278 |
| wiggly (3 cycles) | 3 | 0.3478 | 0.4645 |
| wiggly (3 cycles) | 9 | 0.0177 | 0.0526 |

On the smooth target, degree 3 and degree 9 are basically tied (0.0307 vs. 0.0278) — the low-capacity model's inductive bias already matches the truth, so extra capacity buys almost nothing. On the wiggly target, degree 3 is catastrophically underfit — a cubic *cannot* represent three oscillations, no amount of data fixes that, it's pure bias — while degree 9 recovers most of the gap (0.4645 → 0.0526, an 8.8× improvement). **There is no row in this table where one fixed choice of capacity wins outright across both problems.** That's no-free-lunch, made numeric rather than abstract: the "right" model is a function of what the world you're fitting actually looks like, which you generally don't know until after you've tried to fit it.

**Named misconceptions.**

- **"A sufficiently powerful/flexible model has no inductive bias, so it's always the safe default."** Every model family has an inductive bias — "flexible" is itself a bias (toward assuming the world needs that much flexibility), and Lesson 2's degree-7 variance blowup shows it is not a free or safe default. Even the largest neural architectures in AIE-202 encode strong biases (locality, compositionality, invariances) — they don't escape this theorem, they win by having biases that happen to match the problems the field throws at them constantly.
- **"No free lunch means all algorithms are equally good in practice, so algorithm choice doesn't matter."** The theorem is about a uniform average over *all conceivable* problems, a set real-world problems are not a representative sample of. In practice, matching inductive bias to actual problem structure is most of what separates a working system from a broken one — this theorem explains *why* that matching step is unavoidable, it does not make it unnecessary.
- **"If my model has high capacity, I should always use it, since it can 'fall back' to something simple if needed."** A high-capacity family fit by minimizing empirical risk does not fall back to simple behavior automatically — Lesson 2's degree-7 row shows it will actively use spare capacity to fit noise unless something (more data, a smaller family, or regularization — M02) stops it.

### Lesson 4 — Reading a learning curve

**Teaching text.**

Everything so far has been diagnosed after the fact, by comparing training error to a population estimate you don't get at deployment time. In practice you have exactly one finite sample. The tool that recovers the Lesson 1–3 story from that single sample is the **learning curve**: plot training error and *validation* error (error on a held-out slice of your own data, never touched during fitting) as a function of some axis, and read the shape.

There are two axis choices, and they diagnose different things:

1. **Error vs. capacity** (degree, tree depth, etc., data size fixed). Training error falls roughly monotonically as capacity rises — more capacity can only get better at fitting the specific points it's shown. Validation error falls, bottoms out, then rises: the classic U-shape. The bottom is where bias and variance roughly balance; left of it you're underfit, right of it you're overfit. This is Lab B, and it is the direct empirical instrument for everything Lesson 2 stated algebraically.
2. **Error vs. training-set size** (capacity fixed). This is the diagnostic that tells you *which fix to reach for*. Fix a high-capacity model (degree 9) and grow $n$:

   | $n$ | Train MSE | Population MSE | Gap |
   |---:|---:|---:|---:|
   | 12 | 0.0081 | 10.9102 | 10.9020 |
   | 20 | 0.0085 | 1.3919 | 1.3834 |
   | 50 | 0.0278 | 0.0477 | 0.0199 |
   | 100 | 0.0338 | 0.0460 | 0.0122 |
   | 300 | 0.0380 | 0.0400 | 0.0020 |

   The gap collapses by four orders of magnitude as $n$ grows, and population error converges toward the $\sigma^2=0.04$ floor — with *capacity held fixed the entire time*. This is the operational meaning of "more data reduces variance, not bias": if your problem is a large train/validation gap with both curves still trending toward each other, more data is the fix, and buying a bigger model would only make it worse. If instead both curves have flattened *together*, high and close, more data won't help — that's bias, and the fix is more capacity (or better features — M07) not more rows.

This module doesn't yet teach the honest, leakage-free way to build the validation split itself — that contract, with its own failure modes, is M05's entire subject. For now: validation data means data the fitting procedure never saw, full stop, and mixing that up even slightly (predict the specific way in Lab C) breaks everything above without changing a single number in the training column.

**Named misconceptions.**

- **"A jagged, noisy validation curve means my code has a bug."** Not necessarily — Lab B's own curve jumps around at high degree (the design matrix becomes ill-conditioned as degree rises, adding numerical noise on top of the bias–variance signal). Real curves, especially with small $n$, are noisier than the clean textbook U. Learn to read the *trend*, not each individual point.
- **"If training error is still falling, I should keep adding capacity."** Training error almost always keeps falling — that curve alone never tells you to stop. The validation curve is the one doing the actual work; training error only tells you the optimizer is functioning.
- **"The n-axis curve and the capacity-axis curve are two ways of looking at the same information."** They diagnose different, non-interchangeable problems — high-variance regimes respond to more data and don't respond to more capacity; high-bias regimes respond to more capacity and largely don't respond to more data. Reaching for the wrong lever (more data for a bias problem, more capacity for a variance problem) burns real cost for near-zero improvement, and this is the single most common wasted-effort pattern in applied ML.

```python
"""AIE-201-M01 Lab A — solution reference."""
import numpy as np


def true_function(x):
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)


def make_dataset(n, noise_std, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, size=n)
    y = true_function(x) + rng.normal(0, noise_std, size=n)
    return x, y


def design_matrix(x, degree):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    return np.vstack([x ** i for i in range(degree + 1)]).T


def fit_poly(x, y, degree):
    X = design_matrix(x, degree)
    w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
    return w


def predict_poly(x, w):
    degree = len(w) - 1
    X = design_matrix(x, degree)
    return X @ w


def mse(y_true, y_pred):
    y_true = np.asarray(y_true, dtype=float)
    y_pred = np.asarray(y_pred, dtype=float)
    return float(np.mean((y_true - y_pred) ** 2))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    x_train, y_train = make_dataset(15, 0.2, seed=0)
    x_pop, y_pop = make_dataset(2000, 0.2, seed=1)
    print(f"{'degree':>6} {'train_mse':>10} {'pop_mse':>10}")
    for degree in (1, 3, 9):
        w = fit_poly(x_train, y_train, degree)
        tr = mse(y_train, predict_poly(x_train, w))
        pop = mse(y_pop, predict_poly(x_pop, w))
        print(f"{degree:6d} {tr:10.4f} {pop:10.4f}")
```

```python
def learning_curve(x_train, y_train, x_val, y_val, degrees):
    train_errs, val_errs = [], []
    for degree in degrees:
        w = fit_poly(x_train, y_train, degree)
        train_errs.append(mse(y_train, predict_poly(x_train, w)))
        val_errs.append(mse(y_val, predict_poly(x_val, w)))
    return np.array(train_errs), np.array(val_errs)
```

```python
import numpy as np


def true_function(x):
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)


def make_domain_dataset(n, low, high, noise_std, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.uniform(low, high, size=n)
    y = true_function(x) + rng.normal(0, noise_std, size=n)
    return x, y


def design_matrix(x, degree):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    return np.vstack([x ** i for i in range(degree + 1)]).T


def fit_poly(x, y, degree):
    X = design_matrix(x, degree)
    w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
    return w


def predict_poly(x, w):
    degree = len(w) - 1
    X = design_matrix(x, degree)
    return X @ w


def mse(y_true, y_pred):
    y_true = np.asarray(y_true, dtype=float)
    y_pred = np.asarray(y_pred, dtype=float)
    return float(np.mean((y_true - y_pred) ** 2))


def evaluate_shift(degree, noise_std=0.2):
    x_train, y_train = make_domain_dataset(25, 0.0, 0.7, noise_std, seed=7)
    x_iid, y_iid = make_domain_dataset(300, 0.0, 0.7, noise_std, seed=8)
    x_shift, y_shift = make_domain_dataset(300, 0.7, 1.0, noise_std, seed=9)

    w = fit_poly(x_train, y_train, degree)
    train_err = mse(y_train, predict_poly(x_train, w))
    iid_err = mse(y_iid, predict_poly(x_iid, w))
    shift_err = mse(y_shift, predict_poly(x_shift, w))
    return train_err, iid_err, shift_err


if __name__ == "__main__":
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — what it means to bet a training set resembles the world.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** The bias–variance decomposition is derivable algebra, not opinion (Lesson 2 states the identity; you can rederive it from $\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat f)^2]$ by adding and subtracting $\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]$ inside the square). Every number in this module's tables is reproducible from the stated seeds. The training set a model is fit on is, factually, someone's collected and labeled data — a specific, finite, historically situated sample, not a neutral window onto "the world."
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "More data" and "more compute" are offered as near-universal fixes, and Lesson 4's n-axis curve shows they genuinely are — for variance. What that framing quietly steps around is that more data of the *same kind*, from the *same collection process*, does nothing for bias, and does nothing at all for Lab C's failure mode: a population that was never sampled from in the first place. A benchmark score going up is real and measurable (the course's Tier 1 commitment) — and it is a claim about performance on the distribution that was measured, which is not automatically the distribution of everyone the model will be used on.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Every validation split in this module, and every one you will build for the rest of this course, reports a single number: the *average* error over the held-out set. Lab C showed you, in your own numbers, that a model can look excellent on that average (validation MSE 0.0615, barely above training) while being catastrophically wrong for an entire region no one thought to check. The average did not lie — it correctly described the set it was computed over. It also could not, structurally, tell you who wasn't in it. Before you pick a model off a learning curve and call it done: who is in your validation set, who resembles them, and who — plausibly, specifically, not hypothetically — does not?

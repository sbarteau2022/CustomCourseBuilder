## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Why a GPU exists: latency machines vs. throughput machines

**The one idea.** A CPU is a *latency* machine: it is engineered to finish *one* instruction stream *as soon as possible*. It spends its transistor budget on the things that make a single thread fast — large caches, deep out-of-order execution, branch prediction, speculative execution. A GPU is a *throughput* machine: it is engineered to finish *an enormous number* of instruction streams *per unit time*, and it does not care how long any single one takes. It spends its transistor budget on **arithmetic lanes** and on the machinery to keep them fed.

The consequence you must internalize: a GPU does not make a serial program faster. It makes a *parallel* program *cheaper per element* — and only when you can keep its lanes busy.

**How the GPU hides the wall M04 showed you.** A CPU fights memory latency by *avoiding* it: caches keep hot data close, prefetchers guess what's next, out-of-order execution finds independent work to do while a load is outstanding. A GPU fights memory latency by *tolerating* it. It runs threads in lockstep groups (NVIDIA calls a group of 32 a **warp**); when a warp issues a memory load and stalls, the scheduler instantly swaps in *another* warp that is ready to compute. With enough warps resident (this is **occupancy**), the arithmetic units never idle even though every individual memory access is slow. The CPU hides ~300-cycle DRAM latency with a few tens of in-flight instructions; the GPU hides it with *tens of thousands of in-flight threads*. Same wall from M04 — opposite strategy.

This is **SIMT** (Single Instruction, Multiple Thread): one instruction, 32 lanes executing it on 32 different data elements. It is why GPUs love `y[i] = a*x[i] + y[i]` (every lane does the identical op on its own `i`) and why they choke on `if (data[i] > 0) heavy() else light()` — when lanes in a warp disagree on the branch, the hardware runs *both* sides with the non-participating lanes masked off. That's **warp divergence**, and it can halve or worse your throughput while the profiler still reports "100% utilization."

**Worked example — SAXPY, counted in bytes and FLOPs.**
SAXPY is `y = a*x + y` over `N` FP32 elements. Count the traffic and the arithmetic exactly:

- Memory moved: read `x` (N×4 B), read `y` (N×4 B), write `y` (N×4 B) = **12N bytes**.
- Arithmetic: one multiply + one add per element = **2N FLOPs**.
- **Arithmetic intensity** = FLOPs / bytes = `2N / 12N` = **1/6 ≈ 0.17 FLOP/byte**.

Now put that against the A100's *machine balance* — peak FLOP/s ÷ peak bandwidth = `19.5e12 / 1555e9` ≈ **12.5 FLOP/byte**. SAXPY brings 0.17 FLOP/byte to a machine that needs 12.5 to break even. The arithmetic units will sit **~98% idle**, waiting on HBM. SAXPY on a GPU runs at *memory-bandwidth speed*, and the 19.5 TFLOP/s number is a fantasy for this kernel. Predict the runtime from bandwidth alone: `12N bytes / 1555 GB/s`. For N = 100M: `1.2e9 / 1.555e12` ≈ **0.77 ms**. That prediction will be within ~1.5× of what you measure — because bandwidth, not FLOP/s, is the governing number.

**The kernel, down to the metal (CUDA C, so the abstraction is not a mystery).**
```c
// One thread per element. Grid-stride loop so it works for any N.
__global__ void saxpy(int n, float a, const float *x, float *y) {
    int i = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x;   // this thread's global index
    int stride = blockDim.x * gridDim.x;             // total threads launched
    for (; i < n; i += stride)
        y[i] = a * x[i] + y[i];                      // the only arithmetic. AI = 1/6.
}
```
There is no cleverness here and that is the point: the speed comes from launching millions of these, not from any one being smart. Compare a `for` loop on the CPU that does the same 2N FLOPs one at a time, latency-optimized. The GPU wins on SAXPY *only* if the data is already on the GPU (Lesson 3) and only up to bandwidth (this lesson).

**Named misconceptions.**
- **"A GPU is just a faster CPU."** No. It is slower per thread (lower clock, no big caches, no OOO) and faster only in aggregate throughput on parallel work. A serial bottleneck (Amdahl) caps you regardless of how many lanes you own.
- **"More cores always means faster."** Cores you can't feed are dark silicon. SAXPY proves a kernel can leave 98% of the FLOP/s unused because it's bandwidth-bound. Adding cores does nothing; adding bandwidth (or arithmetic intensity) does.
- **"High GPU utilization means my code is efficient."** Utilization/occupancy measures *lanes busy*, not *useful work*. A warp re-executing both sides of a divergent branch, or a kernel stalled on uncoalesced memory, reports high utilization while wasting most of its throughput.

### Lesson 2 — The roofline: one picture that predicts your ceiling

**The one idea.** Every kernel has a hard performance ceiling set by *two* numbers on the machine and *one* number on the kernel. The machine gives you **peak compute** (FLOP/s, a horizontal roof) and **peak bandwidth** (bytes/s, a sloped roof). The kernel gives you **arithmetic intensity** (FLOP/byte). Plot performance (FLOP/s) against arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte) on log-log axes and you get the **roofline**: a rising bandwidth-limited slope that hits a flat compute-limited ceiling at the *ridge point* = machine balance. Where your kernel's arithmetic intensity falls tells you which roof you're under — and therefore *what to optimize*.

- Left of the ridge (AI < machine balance): **memory-bound**. Your ceiling is `AI × bandwidth`. More FLOP/s buys nothing. Buy bandwidth, or raise AI (reuse data), or don't move it at all.
- Right of the ridge (AI > machine balance): **compute-bound**. Your ceiling is peak FLOP/s. Now the arithmetic units are the constraint; use faster paths (tensor cores, FP16).

For the A100 FP32 path, the ridge is at **12.5 FLOP/byte**. Memorize the move: *compute the kernel's AI, compare to 12.5, and you already know which roof you'll hit before writing a line.*

**Worked example — three kernels, placed by hand.**

| Kernel | Bytes moved | FLOPs | Arithmetic intensity | Roof | Predicted ceiling on A100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vector add `z=x+y` | 12N | N | **1/12 ≈ 0.08** | memory | `0.08 × 1555e9` ≈ **130 GFLOP/s** |
| SAXPY `y=ax+y` | 12N | 2N | **1/6 ≈ 0.17** | memory | `0.17 × 1555e9` ≈ **260 GFLOP/s** |
| Matmul `C=A·B`, N×N | ~12N² (each matrix once) | 2N³ | **N/6** | depends on N | see below |

The first two are pinned far under the ridge — they will *never* see more than a couple hundred GFLOP/s of the A100's 19,500. The 19.5 TFLOP/s number is unreachable by any elementwise kernel, full stop.

Matmul is the interesting one because its AI *grows with N*. Reading each of A, B, C once = 3N²×4 = 12N² bytes; work = 2N³ FLOPs; AI = `2N³ / 12N²` = **N/6 FLOP/byte**. Set that equal to the ridge to find where matmul *becomes compute-bound*:
`N/6 = 12.5` → **N ≈ 75**. So a 64×64 matmul is still memory-bound; a 4096×4096 matmul has AI ≈ 680 FLOP/byte, sits deep in the compute-bound region, and is exactly the workload GPUs were built to devour. **This is why deep learning is matmuls.** Not by fashion — because matmul is one of the few workloads whose arithmetic intensity climbs past the ridge, letting you actually cash the FLOP/s check on the spec sheet.

**Connect back to M04, forward to inference.** Arithmetic intensity is just "reuse per byte fetched" — the same idea as cache hit rate in M04, generalized. Tiling a matmul (blocking so a tile of A and B stays in fast on-chip memory and is reused N times) is the GPU-scale version of the cache-blocking you did on the CPU. And the punchline for AIE-303: transformer *training* (big batched matmuls) is compute-bound and loves the GPU; transformer *decode* (generate one token at a time, reload all weights each step, batch=1) is **memory-bound** — you re-read gigabytes of weights to do a comparatively tiny amount of arithmetic. Same model, opposite roof, opposite economics. Lesson 4 puts dollars on that.

**Named misconceptions.**
- **"GPUs are fast at everything."** They are fast at *high-arithmetic-intensity, parallel* things. On low-AI kernels they run at bandwidth speed, which a good CPU can rival once you subtract the transfer tax.
- **"The bottleneck is FLOP/s."** For most real kernels the bottleneck is *bytes*. The roofline exists specifically to stop you from optimizing the arithmetic on a memory-bound kernel — a beginner mistake that buys 0% speedup.
- **"Arithmetic intensity is a property of the hardware."** It's a property of the *algorithm*. Tiling/reuse *raises* AI without changing the math. Choosing an algorithm with higher AI is often worth more than a faster chip.

### Lesson 3 — When the GPU pays, and when it can't: the transfer tax

**The one idea.** Nothing computes on a GPU until the data is *on* the GPU. Host-resident data must cross the PCIe bus (≈ **25 GB/s effective**), and results must cross back. That link is often **slower than the CPU's own DRAM (≈ 50 GB/s)**. So there is a whole class of work — anything with low arithmetic intensity, done *once*, on data that starts in host memory — where the GPU **cannot win**, no matter how many TFLOP/s it has, because the round trip alone costs more than the CPU's total runtime. The GPU pays only when arithmetic-per-transfer is high enough to amortize the trip, or when the data already lives on the device.

**Worked example — the crossover for a single vector add, derived not guessed.**
Data starts on the host. Add two N-float vectors, `z = x + y`. Byte counts: `12N` bytes of DRAM traffic on the CPU; over PCIe you must ship `x` and `y` up (8N bytes) and `z` back (4N bytes) = `12N` bytes across the bus, plus the on-GPU compute.

- **CPU time** ≈ `12N / 50e9` seconds (bandwidth-bound, one pass).
- **GPU time** ≈ `12N / 25e9` (transfer) + `12N / 1555e9` (compute). The compute term is ~62× smaller than the transfer term — **negligible**. So GPU ≈ `12N / 25e9`.

Compare: `12N/25e9` vs `12N/50e9`. The GPU is **2× slower for every N.** There is no crossover. For a single low-AI op on host data, the GPU *structurally cannot win*, because PCIe (25) is slower than CPU DRAM (50). Chip TFLOP/s never enters the equation.

**Now make it pay.** Suppose you do `k` elementwise passes over the resident data before copying back (fuse `k` ops, or keep the array on-device across `k` kernels). Transfer is paid once; compute is paid `k` times:
`GPU: 12N/25e9 + k·12N/1555e9`  vs  `CPU: k·12N/50e9`.
Divide through by `12N` and solve for the break-even `k`:
`1/25 + k/1555 < k/50`  →  `1/25 < k·(1/50 − 1/1555)` = `k·0.019357`  →  **k > 2.07**.
So you need **at least 3 passes worth of arithmetic per host round-trip** just to break even on a bandwidth-bound kernel — before you even account for kernel-launch overhead. This is the single most important operational fact in the module: **the question is never "is the GPU faster?" It's "does the work per transfer clear the bar?"** Keep data resident (train a whole epoch on-device, run a whole pipeline before copying back) and the transfer tax amortizes toward zero; ship data back and forth per op and you'll lose to a laptop.

**Two more taxes that bite small work.**
- **Kernel-launch overhead** ≈ 5–20 µs per launch. If your kernel runs in 3 µs, launch dominates and 1000 tiny kernels waste milliseconds in pure overhead. Fuse kernels; batch.
- **Occupancy / warm-up.** A kernel over a tiny N can't fill the machine — you launch 500 threads on a device that wants 100,000 resident to hide latency. Small N *cannot* reach the roofline ceiling; you need enough parallel work to saturate.

**Named misconceptions.**
- **"Move everything to the GPU."** Moving low-AI, do-once, host-resident work to the GPU makes it *slower* (the vector-add derivation). The right rule: move work to the GPU and *keep it there*; minimize crossings, not just op count.
- **"The GPU was idle so it's underutilized/wasteful."** Often the GPU is waiting on PCIe or on the CPU to prepare the next batch — a *pipeline* problem, not a compute problem. Fix the feed, not the chip.
- **"Batch size is a tuning knob for accuracy."** It's also the primary *economic* knob: batching raises arithmetic intensity and amortizes both transfer and launch overhead. Batch=1 inference is memory-bound and expensive per token; large-batch is compute-bound and cheap per token (Lesson 4).

### Lesson 4 — Cost-per-op: from FLOPs and $/hour to $/1M tokens

**The one idea.** A GPU is rented by *time*, but you sell by *work* (tokens, images, requests). The bridge is throughput: **$/op = ($/second) ÷ (ops/second)**. To estimate before you deploy, you need three numbers: the price of the GPU-second, the FLOPs (or bytes) per op, and the *achieved* throughput — which is the roofline ceiling times a realistic efficiency factor (30–60% is normal; peak is a lie). Everything in AIE-303 (production inference cost) is this equation with more terms.

**Worked example — cost of one transformer token, batch-1 vs. batched.**
Reference: a 7-billion-parameter transformer, FP16, on the A100 at an assumed **$2 / GPU-hour** = `$2 / 3600 s` = **$5.6e-4 per GPU-second**. Forward pass costs ≈ **2 FLOPs per parameter per token** → `2 × 7e9` = **14 GFLOP/token**. Weights in memory: `7e9 params × 2 bytes` = **14 GB**.

*Batch = 1 (decode one token, the latency-sensitive case).* This is **memory-bound**: to produce one token you must read all 14 GB of weights from HBM once. Throughput ceiling = `bandwidth / bytes-per-token` = `1555e9 / 14e9` ≈ **111 tokens/s** — set by *bandwidth*, not FLOP/s. Cost:
`$5.6e-4 /s ÷ 111 tok/s` ≈ **$5.0e-6 per token** ≈ **$5.0 per 1M tokens**.
Note the FLOP/s were nearly irrelevant — you used `14e9/125e12` ≈ 0.1 ms of compute but ~9 ms of weight-reading. The card is 98% idle *by construction*. That idle time is the cost.

*Large batch (throughput case).* Batching B tokens reads the 14 GB of weights *once* and reuses them across all B tokens — arithmetic intensity climbs, and past the ridge you become **compute-bound**. Throughput ceiling now = `achieved FLOP/s / FLOP-per-token`. At, say, **40% of FP16 tensor peak** = `0.40 × 312e12` = 125 TFLOP/s effective: `125e12 / 14e9` ≈ **8,900 tokens/s**. Cost:
`$5.6e-4 /s ÷ 8900 tok/s` ≈ **$6.3e-8 per token** ≈ **$0.06 per 1M tokens**.

**Read the two numbers side by side: $5.00 vs $0.06 per 1M tokens — an ~80× swing driven entirely by batch size**, i.e., by *which roof you're standing under*. That single ratio is why inference providers batch aggressively, why latency SLAs are expensive (they forbid batching), and why "the GPU costs $2/hour" tells you almost nothing about your cost per customer. The chip price is the *least* variable term.

**The terms AIE-303 will add (name them now so the estimate isn't a lie).** Utilization/idle (you pay for the GPU whether or not requests arrive — a card at 30% duty cycle triples effective $/token); memory capacity (a model that doesn't fit forces sharding or a bigger, pricier instance); networking and multi-GPU interconnect for models that span cards; the CPU/host, storage, and egress around the GPU; and *availability* — the spot price you assumed can vanish. A cost estimate that quotes only peak throughput at on-demand price is off by a factor you will feel in production.

**Named misconceptions.**
- **"The GPU is the cost."** The GPU is one line item. Idle time, utilization, memory headroom, and batching efficiency move your real $/op by 10–100×. Two teams on identical hardware can differ 50× on cost per token.
- **"Cost per op comes from peak FLOP/s."** Peak is unreachable. Use the roofline ceiling × a measured efficiency factor. And for memory-bound ops (batch-1 decode), it comes from *bandwidth*, not FLOP/s at all.
- **"Faster hardware always lowers cost."** Only if you can keep it fed. A card twice as fast that sits 70% idle on your traffic can cost *more* per op than a smaller card you saturate. Match the machine to the load.

```python
def gpu_add_once(x_host, y_host):
    xd = xp.asarray(x_host); yd = xp.asarray(y_host)   # host -> device (the tax)
```

### Lab M07-C — Cost-per-op and the batch-size break-even

**Goal.** Turn spec numbers into dollars. Predict $/1M tokens for a transformer at batch=1 and at large batch, and find the batch size where per-token cost stops improving — then measure the throughput curve and price it.

**Predict (on paper, before code).** Using the harness numbers and a stated $/GPU-hour (default $2), for a P-parameter FP16 model (`P` given, e.g. 7e9):
1. Batch=1 decode is memory-bound: predict tokens/s = `GPU_BW / (2·P bytes)` and the resulting **$/1M tokens**. State explicitly that FLOP/s did *not* enter.
2. Large-batch decode is compute-bound: predict tokens/s = `η · GPU_FLOPS_FP16_PEAK / (2·P FLOPs)` for an efficiency `η` you choose (state it; 0.3–0.5 is honest) and the **$/1M tokens**.
3. Predict the **ratio** between the two costs and, in one sentence, *why* a latency SLA that forbids batching is expensive.
4. Predict the *shape* of the cost-vs-batch curve: at small batch, memory-bound (cost falls steeply as batch rises); past the ridge, compute-bound (cost flattens). Predict the approximate batch size where the knee occurs (where reused-weights arithmetic intensity crosses the ridge).

**Build.**
```python
# m07c_cost.py — STARTER
from m07_harness import GPU_BW, GPU_FLOPS_FP16_PEAK, sync, xp
import time

DOLLARS_PER_HOUR = 2.0
def dollars_per_sec(): return DOLLARS_PER_HOUR / 3600.0

def weight_matmul_proxy(P, batch):
    # The decode-step proxy, built in the open so you can see EXACTLY what is being measured
    # (no black box). The dominant cost of a decode step is the weight matmul: read all P
    # FP16 weights (2*P bytes) ONCE per step and reuse them across the whole batch, doing
    # ~2*P FLOPs per token. Model the weights as a square matrix W (d x d, with
    # d = round(sqrt(P)) so d*d ~ P) and the batch as activations X (batch x d):
    #
    #     Y = X @ W   costs   2*batch*d*d ~ 2*P*batch FLOPs   while reading   2*P bytes of W.
    #
    # Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs/bytes ~ batch, so it GROWS with batch. batch=1 is a GEMV
    # that re-reads all ~2*P bytes of weights each step (memory-bound; tokens/s ~ GPU_BW/(2*P));
    # large batch is a GEMM that reuses them (compute-bound; tokens/s ~ eta*GPU_FLOPS_FP16_PEAK/(2*P)).
    # That crossover is the knee you predicted on paper.
    d = int(round(P ** 0.5))
    W = xp.random.rand(d, d, dtype=xp.float16)      # resident weights, ~2*P bytes, read each step
    X = xp.random.rand(batch, d, dtype=xp.float16)  # the batch of token activations
    return lambda: X @ W

def tokens_per_sec_measured(P, batch, reps=10):
    step = weight_matmul_proxy(P, batch)            # the inspectable workload above
    step(); sync()                                  # warm up (allocation, JIT)
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(reps): step()
    sync()
    sec = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / reps
    return batch / sec                              # tokens (= batch) produced per second

def cost_per_million_tokens(P, batch):
    tps = tokens_per_sec_measured(P, batch)
    return dollars_per_sec() / tps * 1_000_000
```

## Assessment

**Design principle (gaming-resistant).** You cannot pass by producing a program that runs. Every graded artifact is a **judgment trail**: a timestamped Predict document written before code, plus measurements that must *agree with or knowingly diverge from* the prediction with a stated reason. The two ways to fail are (a) no prediction / prediction written after measuring, and (b) a right number reached through wrong reasoning. Both are worth zero regardless of whether the code works.

**Components.** Labs M07-A/B/C each submit: Predict doc (timestamped pre-build), working code passing the harness tests, and a Verify writeup reconciling predicted vs measured. Then a **module exam**.

**Module exam (unseen workload, gaming-resistant).** You are handed an unseen inference scenario spec: a model size, a target GPU's spec sheet (bandwidth, FP16 peak, PCIe, price/hour), a traffic pattern (requests/sec, batch-ability, latency SLA), and a proposed deployment. **Before running anything**, you must, on paper: (1) classify the dominant kernel as compute- or memory-bound with the AI-vs-ridge argument; (2) predict achieved throughput and $/1M-ops with your efficiency assumption stated; (3) predict whether the proposed deployment meets the SLA and whether the GPU pays vs. a CPU alternative, with the transfer/batch reasoning. *Then* you run the provided harness to measure, and you write the reconciliation: where you were right, where the machine surprised you, and the corrected recommendation. You are graded on the prediction and the reasoning; a measurement that contradicts a well-reasoned prediction with an identified cause scores *higher* than a lucky number with no argument.

**Rubric.**

| Criterion | Full marks (3) | Partial (1–2) | Zero (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predict-before-Build integrity** | Timestamped prediction precedes every build; quantitative, with stated assumptions (η, achieved-BW fraction) | Prediction present but vague/qualitative, or missing an assumption | No prediction, or written after measuring (timestamp fails) |
| **Bound classification (roofline reasoning)** | Every kernel correctly labeled memory/compute-bound via AI-vs-ridge, derived not recalled | Correct label, weak/absent derivation | Wrong roof, or "GPU is fast" hand-wave |

| **Prediction–measurement reconciliation** | Gap explained with the *right cause* (achieved-BW, η, occupancy, launch) | Gap noted, cause vague | No reconciliation, or "close enough" |
| **Reasoning > number (anti-gaming)** | Right reasoning; number may be off by a factor with cause named | — | Right number, wrong/absent reasoning |

## Elle pacing notes

*(Elle = the adaptive tutor. Walls = where learners stall; reroutes = the move that unblocks; signals = productive-struggle tells to protect; accelerate = when to skip ahead.)*

- **Wall — "the FLOP/byte arithmetic feels arbitrary."** Learners compute AI mechanically without believing it predicts anything. Reads as disengagement, not struggle. **Reroute:** one no-code session drawing the roofline by hand for *their* card — plot the ridge, drop the three kernels on it, and only then run M07-B. The picture makes AI physical; the number stops being a ritual.
- **Wall — "the GPU was slower and I think I broke it."** Almost always the transfer-tax result in M07-A landing correctly, but read by the learner as a bug. **Reroute:** do *not* debug the code — ask them to re-derive `PCIE_BW vs CPU_BW` on paper. When they see 25 < 50, the "slower" result flips from failure to *the lesson*. This is the module's central inversion; protect it.
- **Wall — "peak FLOP/s is a lie" causes distrust of all the numbers.** After seeing SAXPY reach <1% of peak, some learners disbelieve every spec figure and freeze. **Reroute:** reframe peak as a *ceiling you place kernels under*, not a promised speed. The roofline is exactly the tool that tells you which number governs. Hand them the ridge-point comparison as the one reliable move.
- **Wall — cost math sprawls (too many terms at once).** M07-C's hidden terms (utilization, spot price, memory) overwhelm before the core `$/s ÷ ops/s` is solid. **Reroute:** lock the two-number core (batch=1 vs batched) first, get the 80× swing, *then* add one hidden term at a time. Don't introduce TCO until the base ratio is intuitive.
- **Signal (productive, protect it) — predicting "the GPU will obviously be faster" for M07-A and being wrong.** The surprise *is* the learning. A learner who confidently predicts GPU-wins, measures GPU-loses, and then correctly diagnoses the transfer tax is exactly on track — never rescue them before the reconciliation. Struggle here is the module working.
- **Signal (productive) — a learner spontaneously asking "then why does anyone use a GPU for inference?" after the batch=1 cost.** That's the batching insight arriving on its own; let them derive the large-batch case rather than telling them.
- **Signal (blocked, intervene) — pointer/`asarray` residency confusion causing silent host round-trips inside a loop.** Unlike the productive surprises, this is a mechanical bug that corrupts the measurement without teaching anything. If M07-A's numbers are *noisy and directionless* (not cleanly GPU-slower), suspect a stray copy in the loop and point at residency directly.
- **Accelerate.** Clean M07-A crossover derivation on the first pass **and** correct roofline bound-labels for all four kernels in M07-B → the learner may skip M07-C's *build* and take the optional CUDA-C SAXPY extension instead, then go straight to the module exam. Do **not** let them skip the M07-C *Predict* — the cost reasoning is load-bearing for AIE-303 and is the one thing the exam most directly tests.

## Three-tier reading — *the compute you rent was made in one place*

**Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable).** A GPU is a throughput machine: it trades single-thread latency for thousands of arithmetic lanes and hides memory latency by oversubscribing threads. Two spec numbers — peak FLOP/s and peak bandwidth — and their ratio (machine balance, ~12.5 FLOP/byte on the A100 FP32 path) determine, *before any code runs*, whether a kernel is compute- or memory-bound. Most real work, including transformer decode, is **memory-bandwidth-bound**, not compute-bound; the advertised TFLOP/s is unreachable for it. The GPU only pays when arithmetic-per-byte-moved is high enough and the data is already on the device — the PCIe link (~25 GB/s) is often slower than CPU DRAM (~50 GB/s), so a single low-AI op on host data *cannot* win on the GPU, provably, from the spec sheet. Also verifiable and not about your code at all: leading-edge AI accelerators are fabricated essentially at **one foundry (TSMC)** using EUV lithography from essentially **one vendor (ASML)**; high-bandwidth memory comes from a **three-supplier oligopoly**; top GPUs are **export-controlled**; and training-scale compute sits in a **handful of hyperscaler datacenters**.

**Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** Boosters say GPUs *democratize* AI — rent one by the hour, anyone can build. What that hides: the entire fast substrate is a physical chokepoint-chain almost no one can audit or replace. The FLOP/s you rent exist because of one lithography vendor, one leading-edge fab, an HBM oligopoly, and hyperscalers who own the datacenters, the interconnect, and the allocation queue that decides whether you get capacity at all. "Just rent more GPUs" conceals that supply, price, and even *legality* of access are set upstream of you. Critics idealize the mirror image — "GPUs are wasteful, most of the die and most of the datacenter power sits idle" — which is *true* (occupancy and utilization are usually far below peak, and you saw batch=1 leave the card 98% idle) but omits that this throughput-first, redundancy-heavy, latency-hiding design is *exactly why the machine runs at all*. Neither story describes the engineer's real seat: you will quote a customer a $/1M-tokens and a latency SLA that silently depend on a utilization figure you're guessing, a spot price someone else moves overnight, and a hardware allocation you cannot promise next quarter.

**Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** When you promise a customer a latency SLA and a cost-per-token, you are also, silently, promising that a foundry you've never seen, a lithography vendor in one country, and a hyperscaler's allocation queue will all keep behaving — so **whose capacity are you actually reselling when you say the price is yours, and what is your number worth the morning that chain moves?**

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Deduplication: exact and near-duplicate

A raw corpus assembled from more than one source almost always contains the same content
more than once — the same press release synced to a partner site and a news aggregator, the
same forum answer copy-pasted into three different threads, the same paragraph with one word
edited by an automated content spinner. Training on a duplicate is not neutral: it does not
just cost extra compute, it **overweights** that exact string relative to everything else in
the corpus, and the degree matters more than intuition suggests — a training set with a
document duplicated even a handful of times measurably increases the model's tendency to
reproduce that document's exact text verbatim, which is the last thing a corpus with a
written consent posture wants to have quietly guaranteed.

Deduplication comes in two kinds that need two different techniques. **Exact duplication** —
byte-identical or identical-after-trivial-normalization (case, whitespace) content — is
cheap: hash the normalized text and group by hash. A hash collision on real text at any
practical corpus size is not a live concern; SHA-256 is the same guarantee AIE-203-M02 relied
on for idempotency keys, applied to content instead of a row. **Near-duplication** — two
documents that share the overwhelming majority of their content but are not byte-identical
(a re-published article with a different byline, a spun paragraph, a document with a
boilerplate footer changed) — cannot be caught by any hash of the whole string, because
changing even one character changes the hash completely. Catching it requires a similarity
measure, and the standard one is **Jaccard similarity over shingles**: break each document
into overlapping substrings of length `k` ("5-shingles" of `"the quick brown fox"` include
`"the q"`, `"he qu"`, `"e qui"`, ...), treat each document as the *set* of its shingles, and
measure `|A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|`.

Computing exact Jaccard for every pair in a large corpus is exactly the O(n²) problem
AIE-110 trained you to recognize on sight — for a corpus of a million documents that is
roughly 500 billion pairwise comparisons, each itself not free. **MinHash** is the
approximation that makes this tractable: instead of comparing full shingle sets, apply `h`
independent hash functions to every shingle, keep only the *minimum* hashed value each
function produces over the whole set (one integer per hash function — a document's
"signature"), and estimate Jaccard as the fraction of hash functions where two documents'
minimums agree. This works because of a real, provable fact about min-hashing: the
probability that two sets' minimum hash values agree, under a hash function drawn uniformly
at random, is *exactly* their true Jaccard similarity. A short signature (64 or 128 numbers)
approximates a comparison that would otherwise require the full shingle sets, and — at
production scale — the signatures themselves get bucketed with **LSH banding** (split each
signature into `b` bands of `r` numbers each; two documents become "candidates" only if
they share an identical band, and only candidates get directly compared) so that even the
signature comparisons stop being O(n²). This lab's `find_near_duplicates` does the brute-
force O(n²) signature comparison, on purpose, because a learner who has not felt that cost
cannot appreciate why banding exists — but the cost is real, and the misconception section
below names it directly.

**Worked example.** Six short documents. `d1`/`d2` are the same paragraph, one in mixed
case with extra whitespace; `d3` is `d1` with `"jumps"` changed to `"leaps"`; `d4`/`d6` are a
financial blurb where `d6` has one clause added; `d5` is unrelated.

```python
>>> content_hash(d1) == content_hash(d2)
True                                  # exact duplicate — hash matches after normalize()

>>> true_jaccard(shingles(d1), shingles(d3))
0.883                                 # one word changed out of ~140 characters — still ~88% shingle overlap

>>> sig1, sig3 = minhash_signature(shingles(d1), num_hashes=64), minhash_signature(shingles(d3), num_hashes=64)
>>> estimate_jaccard(sig1, sig3)
0.875                                 # 56/64 hash functions agree — within 0.01 of the true value, using
                                       # 64 integers instead of the full ~140-shingle sets

>>> true_jaccard(shingles(d4), shingles(d6)), estimate_jaccard(
...     minhash_signature(shingles(d4), num_hashes=64), minhash_signature(shingles(d6), num_hashes=64))
(0.926, 0.875)                        # a second, independent pair — 8/64 hash functions are off here too,
                                       # the same count as (d1, d3), but the estimate is noisier in the
                                       # sense that matters: it lands 0.051 below the true value (0.926 -
                                       # 0.875) versus only 0.008 for (d1, d3) (0.883 - 0.875) — the same
                                       # amount of hash disagreement producing a wider true-vs-estimate gap,
                                       # because the two pairs' true similarities differ more than their
                                       # discrete 1/64-granularity estimates can distinguish — and still
                                       # lands the right side of any sane threshold like 0.8
```

At `threshold=0.8`, both `(d1, d3)` and `(d4, d6)` are correctly flagged near-duplicates,
and no pair involving the unrelated `d5` comes close (`0.0` on every one of its minhash
comparisons). Note that `(d1, d2)` — the *exact* duplicate — would also clear an 0.8
near-duplicate threshold, since identical shingle sets have Jaccard `1.0`. That overlap is
not a bug; it is the reason exact-hash dedup runs *first*, as a cheap O(n) pass that shrinks
the corpus before the expensive O(n²) near-duplicate stage ever has to touch it — every
exact duplicate MinHash would also have caught, at far higher cost per pair.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"MinHash gives an exact answer."* It gives an unbiased **estimate** whose variance
  shrinks as `num_hashes` grows — the worked example's `0.875` estimate against a true
  `0.883` is normal approximation noise, not a bug to chase down. A learner who expects
  bit-for-bit agreement between `true_jaccard` and `estimate_jaccard` will "debug" correctly
  working code.
- *"Brute-force pairwise comparison is fine — it's just a loop."* It is a loop whose
  iteration count grows quadratically. `find_near_duplicates` on 6 documents does 15
  comparisons; on 100,000 documents it does very nearly 5 billion. This lab uses brute force
  because the goal here is a *correct* minhash implementation, not a scalable index — LSH
  banding is the answer at real scale, and this lab's Elle notes name it as the accelerate
  path for a learner who finishes early.
- *"Deduplication removes garbage."* It removes **redundancy**, which is a different claim.
  A near-duplicate pair might both be perfectly good documents — deduplication keeps one
  and discards the other not because the discarded one was bad, but because two copies of
  the same information teach a model the same thing twice at the cost of a token budget
  that could have taught it something else. That distinction matters directly for this
  module's grading: a discarded near-duplicate needs a `near_duplicate_of:<survivor_id>`
  reason recorded, never a bare drop.

### Lesson 2 — Filtering: quality, language, PII, and toxicity — with documented failure

A filter is a claim, exactly like AIE-203-M04's expectations: "this document is long enough
to be useful," "this document is in a language this corpus is scoped to," "this document
does not contain a detectable personal identifier," "this document does not contain
detectable toxic content." Every one of those claims is checked by a heuristic, and every
heuristic this lesson builds has a **documented, non-hypothetical failure mode** — this
lesson's actual content is not "here is a filter," it is "here is exactly how and when each
filter is wrong," because a filtering pipeline whose designer cannot state its own blind
spots is not more trustworthy than no filtering at all — it is *less* trustworthy, because
it creates false confidence.

Four filter families, each cheap enough to run over an entire corpus without a GPU:

1. **Quality heuristics** — cheap proxies for "is this coherent, substantive prose," not
   readers of meaning: word count (too short is often boilerplate, a nav-menu scrape, or an
   error page), symbol-to-text ratio (a wall of `!!!` and `$$$` is rarely prose), and mean
   word length (both extremes — a run of 1-character tokens or a run of URL-length tokens —
   correlate with non-prose content, not with vocabulary).
2. **Language identification** — for a corpus scoped to one language, everything else needs
   to be routed out (to its own corpus, or discarded, but *knowingly*). The cheap version
   this lab builds is stopword-overlap: what fraction of a document's words are drawn from a
   small closed list of a language's most common function words. Real production systems use
   trained statistical classifiers (fastText's `lid.176`, for instance) for exactly the
   reason this lesson's worked example demonstrates below — the cheap version has a specific,
   predictable blind spot.
3. **PII screens** — regex patterns for structurally-shaped identifiers: an email address's
   `local@domain.tld` shape, a US phone number's digit grouping, a Social Security number's
   `###-##-####` shape. These catch *pattern-shaped* PII and nothing else.
4. **Toxicity screens** — in production this is a trained classifier; this lab builds a
   deliberately tiny keyword list as a stand-in, precisely because its failure modes are
   easy to demonstrate and are the same *shape* of failure a real classifier has, just at a
   different rate.

**Worked example.** Eight short documents, each engineered to isolate exactly one filter
behavior:

```python
>>> filter_document("good_en", GOOD_EN_TEXT)
<good_en: KEEP []>                                          # clean pass — every filter agrees

>>> filter_document("too_short", "Great product would buy again.")
<too_short: DROP ['too_short:5<20']>                         # true positive — genuinely too short to be useful

>>> filter_document("contains_email", EMAIL_TEXT)
<contains_email: DROP ["pii:['email']"]>                     # true positive — a real email address is present

>>> filter_document("contains_ssn_example_in_tutorial", SSN_TUTORIAL_TEXT)
<contains_ssn_example_in_tutorial: DROP ["pii:['ssn']"]>     # FALSE POSITIVE — this document is *about*
                                                                # validating SSN formats and uses "123-45-6789"
                                                                # as a worked example; no real person's SSN is
                                                                # present, but the regex only sees the shape

>>> filter_document("spelled_out_phone_fn", SPELLED_PHONE_TEXT)
<spelled_out_phone_fn: KEEP []>                               # FALSE NEGATIVE — "five five five, one two
                                                                # three, four five six seven" is a working phone
                                                                # number; the digit-pattern regex never matches
                                                                # a number spelled out in words

>>> filter_document("technical_low_stopword", CODE_SNIPPET_TEXT)
<technical_low_stopword: DROP ["language:None(conf=0.13)not_in['en']"]>
                                                                # FALSE POSITIVE — legitimate English technical
                                                                # prose with inline code has a low density of
                                                                # common stopwords (code tokens and identifiers
                                                                # crowd them out), so the cheap classifier's
                                                                # confidence never clears its threshold

>>> filter_document("toxicity_fp", QUOTED_SPEECH_TEXT)
<toxicity_fp: DROP ["toxicity:['stupid']"]>                   # FALSE POSITIVE — ordinary news-style reporting
                                                                # quoting a senator calling an amendment "stupid"
                                                                # trips the keyword list with zero toxic intent
                                                                # in the document itself

>>> filter_document("toxicity_fn", HOSTILE_TEXT)
<toxicity_fn: KEEP []>                                        # FALSE NEGATIVE — genuinely hostile, demeaning
                                                                # content that simply doesn't use any of the four
                                                                # words on this lab's tiny fixed list
```

Five of these eight documents demonstrate a **planted, real failure mode** on purpose — not
because the filters are badly built, but because *every* cheap heuristic filter has failure
modes, and a corpus-construction pipeline that doesn't name them is hiding information from
whoever reads the manifest next.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"A dropped document was garbage."* `contains_ssn_example_in_tutorial` is a perfectly
  good, on-topic tutorial about input validation — it was dropped because a regex cannot
  distinguish "this is a real person's SSN" from "this is a nine-digit string shaped like an
  SSN used as a teaching example." The `FilterDecision.reasons` field exists precisely so
  that a human can later look at a specific exclusion and judge whether the filter's blind
  spot mattered here.
- *"A kept document is safe."* `spelled_out_phone_fn` and `toxicity_fn` both pass every
  filter in this lab while containing exactly the kind of content those filters exist to
  catch. "Passed the filter" means "didn't match this filter's specific pattern," never "was
  verified clean" — the same distinction AIE-203-M04 drilled for a green data-quality check.
- *"More filters, or stricter filters, are strictly better."* Every filter has a
  false-positive cost measured in real, otherwise-good documents discarded — `contains_ssn_
  example_in_tutorial` and `technical_low_stopword` are the cost of running these particular
  four filters at these particular thresholds. A corpus with zero false positives and zero
  false negatives, at zero compute cost, does not exist at this toolchain's budget; the job
  is choosing where the trade-off sits and writing that choice down, not eliminating it.

### Lesson 3 — Contamination: checking against a held-out eval set

If your training corpus contains the questions — or close paraphrases of the questions — in
the eval set you plan to score a model against, every number that eval produces is a lie.
This is not a hypothetical: it is one of the most common, most quietly damaging defects in
real corpus pipelines, because it produces evaluation results that look *better* the more
contaminated they are, which means nothing about the pipeline's own output tells you
something is wrong. The only way to know is to check, directly, against the actual eval set
you intend to use — not to assert in a README that "we checked for contamination."

The standard real technique, used by GPT-3's and Llama's published training reports, is
**n-gram overlap**: build a set of all `n`-token sequences (word n-grams, `n` typically in
the 8–13 range — long enough that overlap is not coincidental, short enough to still catch
excerpts) from every document in the eval set, then scan every candidate training document
for any n-gram that also appears in that index. A single shared 13-gram between a training
document and an eval question is a strong, specific signal — thirteen consecutive words
matching by chance is astronomically unlikely in natural text — and unlike a fuzzy
similarity score, it can point at the *exact* overlapping span for a human to inspect.

**Worked example.** Two eval questions; five candidate training documents, each engineered
to test a different edge of what n-gram overlap can and cannot see:

```python
>>> eval_docs = {
...     "eval_q1": "What year did the Berlin Wall fall and what event triggered its opening to the public",
...     "eval_q2": "Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose molecules within plant cells",
... }
>>> idx = build_contamination_index(eval_docs, n=13)
>>> check_contamination(corpus_docs, idx, n=13)
{'leaked_verbatim': {'eval_q1': 3}}
```

Only `leaked_verbatim` — a document that embeds the eval question's exact wording inside a
longer passage ("...one popular trivia question asks: what year did the Berlin Wall fall
and what event triggered its opening to the public?...") — is flagged, with 3 matching
13-grams against `eval_q1` (not 4, the theoretical maximum for a 16-token question, because
the trailing `"public?"` token — punctuation attached — doesn't string-match the eval
question's bare `"public"`; a real pipeline normalizes punctuation before n-gramming for
exactly this reason, and this lab's tests hold the un-normalized case constant so the gap is
visible). Four other documents are **not** flagged, and each miss is a real, documented limit
of this technique, not an accident:

- `paraphrased` asks the same question in entirely different words — zero matching 13-grams,
  because n-gram overlap only detects *near-verbatim* text, never semantic equivalence.
- `partial_quote_short` contains a genuine 9-word verbatim excerpt of the eval question — but
  9 tokens can never produce a 13-gram. A quote shorter than `n` is invisible by
  construction, no matter how exact it is.
- `same_topic_different_wording` and `unrelated` are correctly unflagged — being *about* the
  same topic as an eval question, or not, is irrelevant; only verbatim n-gram sharing counts.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"A contamination check is a one-time gate before the first training run."* Both the
  training corpus and the eval set change over time — a corpus refreshed with a new scrape,
  an eval set extended with new questions. Contamination is a property of a *specific pair*
  of corpus and eval set at a *specific moment*; it must be re-run every time either side
  changes, exactly like AIE-203-M04's quality gate re-runs on every batch rather than once.
- *"No flagged overlap means no contamination."* The worked example's own `paraphrased` and
  `partial_quote_short` rows are the counter-proof, built into this lesson on purpose: this
  technique has a real, named blind spot for paraphrase and for short quotes. A clean
  contamination report is evidence about *verbatim* overlap at *this* `n`, not a certificate
  that the corpus is contamination-free in every sense that could matter.
- *"An eval set and a training set that share a source, but not exact text, are fine."* If
  your eval questions and your training documents were both drawn from the same underlying
  article family — one lightly edited from the other — Lesson 1's near-duplicate detector,
  not this lesson's exact n-gram matcher, is the tool that catches it. Real pipelines run
  *both* checks between corpus and eval set, not just within the corpus; this module's Lab C
  runs contamination checking as a separate stage precisely so the gap between "duplicate
  detection" and "contamination detection" stays visible instead of collapsing into one box
  that quietly does less than either name promises.

### Lesson 4 — Wiring it together: the provenance manifest and the consent posture

Three working filters do not make a defensible corpus. What makes it defensible is a record,
for **every single raw document**, of exactly what happened to it and why — kept or
excluded, and if excluded, by which specific rule, citing which specific evidence. This is
AIE-203-M04's `LineageTracker` again, doing the same job it always did (answer "where did
this come from, and what happened to it") for a different kind of record. The pipeline order
matters and is worth stating explicitly, because it is itself a design decision with a cost
trade-off: exact-hash dedup runs first because it is the cheapest (O(n), one hash per
document) and removes the most redundant compute-hungry work downstream; near-duplicate
detection runs second, on exact-dedup survivors only, because it is the most expensive stage
per document (Lesson 1's O(n²) signature comparison) and should never be spent on a document
that a cheaper stage would have removed anyway; quality/language/PII/toxicity filtering runs
third; contamination checking runs last, against whatever survives everything before it,
because it is the stage whose failure mode is the most consequential and deserves to see the
smallest, cleanest candidate set.

```python
@dataclass
class ProvenanceRecord:
    doc_id: str
    source_name: str
    source_url: str | None
    license_or_consent: str
    retrieved_at: str
    decision: str                       # "kept" | "excluded"
    exclusion_reasons: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    content_hash: str | None = None
```

Running the full four-stage pipeline (Lesson 4's `build_corpus`) over a 19-document fixture
that mixes every scenario from Lessons 1–3 produces:

```python
>>> records = build_corpus(raw_docs, eval_docs)
>>> corpus_summary(records)
{'raw': 19, 'kept': 9, 'excluded': 10,
 'excluded_by_reason': {'exact_duplicate_of': 1, 'near_duplicate_of': 2, 'too_short': 2,
                         'symbol_heavy': 1, 'language': 3, 'pii': 2, 'contamination': 1}}
```

Ten raw documents excluded, nine reasons named across seven categories, every one traceable
to a specific document and a specific rule — that table, not the number `9`, **is the
deliverable**. A corpus twice this size with the same nine kept documents plus ten more of
unknown provenance is a worse artifact than this one, not a better one, and this module's
assessment is built to make that literally true in the grading, not just true in principle.

The manifest answers "what happened to this document." It does not answer "was I allowed to
use this document's source at all" — that is a separate, prior question, and this lesson's
second deliverable is a **written consent posture**, one per source, filled in by a human
before that source's documents ever enter the pipeline:

```
## Consent posture: <source_name>
- What it is: <one-line description of the source and what it contains>
- How it was obtained: licensed | scraped, robots.txt honored | scraped, ToS ambiguous |
  scraped, ToS prohibits it | user-contributed with disclosed reuse terms
- Legal basis claimed: <specific — a license name, a scraping defense, "none, accepted risk">
- Tier-2 judgment: did the people who created this content have any reasonable expectation
  about how it would be reused? Does "technically public" answer that, or just avoid it?
- Decision: include | exclude | include with restriction (state the restriction)
- Written by: <name>, <date>
```

This is not boilerplate paperwork bolted onto the real work. It is the artifact a compliance
reviewer, a co-author, or a subpoena will actually ask for, and — matching the course's
"outpaces the field" claim head-on — it is the exact thing almost no corpus-construction
tutorial teaches anyone to produce, because most of them stop at "here is a script that
returns a smaller file."

Finally: this module's four stages are themselves DAG-shaped — extract → dedup → filter →
contaminate → manifest — and nothing about that shape is new. A production system wires
`build_corpus`'s four stages as tasks in exactly the hand-built orchestrator from
AIE-203-M02, for exactly the reason M04's quality gate did: retries, resumability, and a
ledger come for free, and a corpus rebuild after a source refresh resumes instead of
restarting from document zero.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The deliverable is corpus size, or token count."* This module's own assessment rubric
  refuses that framing directly — see below. A learner who pads their submission with
  undocumented, unfiltered raw text to inflate a token count is graded down for exactly that,
  regardless of how the resulting model would perform.
- *"Consent posture is a single yes/no gate, decided once, for a whole source."* Real sources
  are rarely uniform — a forum might mix user posts under an ambiguous ToS with moderator
  announcements the platform explicitly licenses. The template above is written per-source
  because that granularity is usually the finest a corpus project can practically manage;
  Tier 3's reading pushes on why even that is coarser than the actual unit at stake.
- *"Provenance recording is documentation, separate from 'the real pipeline.'"* `Provenance
  Record` is produced *by* `build_corpus`, not written by hand afterward — every kept
  document in the corpus AIE-301 will later load has one attached, queryable by source, by
  exclusion reason, by retrieval date. An artifact nobody can query is a document nobody will
  ever actually read again the first time an incident makes someone need it in the next five
  minutes, not the next audit cycle.

# dedup.py — solution
from __future__ import annotations
import hashlib
import random
import zlib

PRIME = 4294967311  # smallest prime > 2**32


def normalize(text: str) -> str:
    return " ".join(text.lower().split())


def content_hash(text: str) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(normalize(text).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()


def find_exact_duplicates(docs: dict[str, str]) -> dict[str, list[str]]:
    by_hash: dict[str, list[str]] = {}
    for doc_id, text in docs.items():
        h = content_hash(text)
        by_hash.setdefault(h, []).append(doc_id)
    return {h: ids for h, ids in by_hash.items() if len(ids) > 1}


def shingles(text: str, k: int = 5) -> set[str]:
    normalized = normalize(text)
    if len(normalized) < k:
        return {normalized} if normalized else set()
    return {normalized[i:i + k] for i in range(len(normalized) - k + 1)}


def _stable_hash(s: str) -> int:
    return zlib.crc32(s.encode("utf-8"))


def _hash_functions(num_hashes: int, seed: int):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [(rng.randrange(1, PRIME - 1), rng.randrange(0, PRIME - 1)) for _ in range(num_hashes)]


def minhash_signature(shingle_set: set[str], num_hashes: int = 32, seed: int = 42) -> tuple[int, ...]:
    if not shingle_set:
        return tuple([PRIME] * num_hashes)
    coeffs = _hash_functions(num_hashes, seed)
    base_hashes = [_stable_hash(s) for s in shingle_set]
    return tuple(min((a * h + b) % PRIME for h in base_hashes) for a, b in coeffs)


def estimate_jaccard(sig_a: tuple[int, ...], sig_b: tuple[int, ...]) -> float:
    matches = sum(1 for x, y in zip(sig_a, sig_b) if x == y)
    return matches / len(sig_a)


def true_jaccard(a: set[str], b: set[str]) -> float:
    if not a and not b:
        return 1.0
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b)


def find_near_duplicates(docs: dict[str, str], threshold: float = 0.8, num_hashes: int = 64,
                          k: int = 5, seed: int = 42) -> list[tuple[str, str, float]]:
    ids = list(docs.keys())
    sigs = {i: minhash_signature(shingles(docs[i], k=k), num_hashes=num_hashes, seed=seed) for i in ids}
    pairs = []
    for a_idx in range(len(ids)):
        for b_idx in range(a_idx + 1, len(ids)):
            a, b = ids[a_idx], ids[b_idx]
            est = estimate_jaccard(sigs[a], sigs[b])
            if est >= threshold:
                pairs.append((a, b, est))
    return pairs

        "esta", "este", "fue", "eran", "todo", "toda", "cuando", "hasta", "entre", "sobre",
    },
}

TOXIC_KEYWORDS = {"idiot", "stupid", "shut up", "worthless"}


@dataclass
class FilterDecision:
    doc_id: str
    kept: bool
    reasons: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

    def __repr__(self) -> str:
        status = "KEEP" if self.kept else "DROP"
        return f"<{self.doc_id}: {status} {self.reasons}>"


def mean_word_length(text: str) -> float:
    words = text.split()
    if not words:
        return 0.0
    return sum(len(w) for w in words) / len(words)


def symbol_ratio(text: str) -> float:
    if not text:
        return 0.0
    symbols = sum(1 for c in text if not c.isalnum() and not c.isspace())
    return symbols / len(text)


def quality_filter(text: str, min_words: int = 20, max_symbol_ratio: float = 0.3,
                    min_mean_word_len: float = 2.0, max_mean_word_len: float = 12.0) -> list[str]:
    reasons = []
    n_words = len(text.split())
    if n_words < min_words:
        reasons.append(f"too_short:{n_words}<{min_words}")
    ratio = symbol_ratio(text)
    if ratio > max_symbol_ratio:
        reasons.append(f"symbol_heavy:{ratio:.2f}>{max_symbol_ratio}")
    mwl = mean_word_length(text)
    if n_words and not (min_mean_word_len <= mwl <= max_mean_word_len):
        reasons.append(f"mean_word_len:{mwl:.2f} outside [{min_mean_word_len},{max_mean_word_len}]")
    return reasons


def identify_language(text: str, min_confidence: float = 0.15) -> tuple[str | None, float]:
    words = [w.strip(".,!?;:\"'()").lower() for w in text.split()]
    words = [w for w in words if w]
    if not words:
        return None, 0.0
    best_lang, best_score = None, 0.0
    for lang, stopwords in STOPWORDS.items():
        hits = sum(1 for w in words if w in stopwords)
        score = hits / len(words)
        if score > best_score:
            best_lang, best_score = lang, score
    if best_score < min_confidence:
        return None, best_score
    return best_lang, best_score


def pii_scan(text: str) -> list[tuple[str, str]]:
    hits = []
    for m in EMAIL_RE.finditer(text):
        hits.append(("email", m.group(0)))
    for m in PHONE_RE.finditer(text):
        hits.append(("phone", m.group(0)))
    for m in SSN_RE.finditer(text):
        hits.append(("ssn", m.group(0)))
    return hits


def toxicity_scan(text: str) -> list[str]:
    lowered = text.lower()
    return [kw for kw in TOXIC_KEYWORDS if kw in lowered]


def filter_document(doc_id: str, text: str, allowed_languages: set[str] = frozenset({"en"}),
                     **quality_kwargs) -> FilterDecision:
    reasons = quality_filter(text, **quality_kwargs)
    lang, conf = identify_language(text)
    if lang is None or lang not in allowed_languages:
        reasons.append(f"language:{lang}(conf={conf:.2f})not_in{sorted(allowed_languages)}")
    pii_hits = pii_scan(text)
    if pii_hits:
        kinds = sorted({k for k, _ in pii_hits})
        reasons.append(f"pii:{kinds}")
    tox_hits = toxicity_scan(text)
    if tox_hits:
        reasons.append(f"toxicity:{sorted(tox_hits)}")
    return FilterDecision(doc_id, kept=len(reasons) == 0, reasons=reasons)

# contamination.py — solution
from __future__ import annotations


def word_ngrams(text: str, n: int = 13) -> set[tuple[str, ...]]:
    tokens = text.lower().split()
    if len(tokens) < n:
        return set()
    return {tuple(tokens[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)}


def build_contamination_index(eval_docs: dict[str, str], n: int = 13) -> dict[tuple, set[str]]:
    index: dict[tuple, set[str]] = {}
    for eval_id, text in eval_docs.items():
        for gram in word_ngrams(text, n=n):
            index.setdefault(gram, set()).add(eval_id)
    return index


def check_contamination(corpus_docs: dict[str, str], contam_index: dict[tuple, set[str]],
                         n: int = 13, min_matching_ngrams: int = 1) -> dict[str, dict[str, int]]:
    report: dict[str, dict[str, int]] = {}
    for doc_id, text in corpus_docs.items():
        doc_grams = word_ngrams(text, n=n)
        hits: dict[str, int] = {}
        for gram in doc_grams:
            if gram in contam_index:
                for eval_id in contam_index[gram]:
                    hits[eval_id] = hits.get(eval_id, 0) + 1
        flagged = {eid: count for eid, count in hits.items() if count >= min_matching_ngrams}
        if flagged:
            report[doc_id] = flagged
    return report

# provenance.py — solution
from __future__ import annotations
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

from dedup import content_hash, find_exact_duplicates, shingles, minhash_signature, estimate_jaccard
from filters import filter_document
from contamination import build_contamination_index, check_contamination


@dataclass
class ProvenanceRecord:
    doc_id: str
    source_name: str
    source_url: str | None
    license_or_consent: str
    retrieved_at: str
    decision: str
    exclusion_reasons: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    content_hash: str | None = None

    def __repr__(self) -> str:
        tag = "KEPT" if self.decision == "kept" else f"EXCLUDED{self.exclusion_reasons}"
        return f"<{self.doc_id} [{self.source_name}]: {tag}>"


def build_corpus(raw_docs: dict[str, dict], eval_docs: dict[str, str], *,
                  near_dup_threshold: float = 0.8, num_hashes: int = 64, shingle_k: int = 5,
                  allowed_languages: set[str] = frozenset({"en"}), contamination_n: int = 13,
                  min_matching_ngrams: int = 1) -> list[ProvenanceRecord]:
    records: dict[str, ProvenanceRecord] = {
        doc_id: ProvenanceRecord(
            doc_id=doc_id,
            source_name=meta["source_name"],
            source_url=meta.get("source_url"),
            license_or_consent=meta["license_or_consent"],
            retrieved_at=meta["retrieved_at"],
            decision="kept",
            content_hash=content_hash(meta["text"]),
        )
        for doc_id, meta in raw_docs.items()
    }
    texts = {doc_id: meta["text"] for doc_id, meta in raw_docs.items()}

    # Stage 1: exact dedup. Keep the first id (insertion order) in each group.
    for _, ids in find_exact_duplicates(texts).items():
        survivor, dupes = ids[0], ids[1:]
        for dupe_id in dupes:
            records[dupe_id].decision = "excluded"
            records[dupe_id].exclusion_reasons.append(f"exact_duplicate_of:{survivor}")

    surviving_ids = [i for i in raw_docs if records[i].decision == "kept"]

    # Stage 2: near-dup dedup among exact-dedup survivors only.
    sigs = {i: minhash_signature(shingles(texts[i], k=shingle_k), num_hashes=num_hashes) for i in surviving_ids}
    excluded_as_near_dup: set[str] = set()
    for a_idx in range(len(surviving_ids)):
        a = surviving_ids[a_idx]
        if a in excluded_as_near_dup:
            continue
        for b_idx in range(a_idx + 1, len(surviving_ids)):
            b = surviving_ids[b_idx]
            if b in excluded_as_near_dup:
                continue
            est = estimate_jaccard(sigs[a], sigs[b])
            if est >= near_dup_threshold:
                records[b].decision = "excluded"
                records[b].exclusion_reasons.append(f"near_duplicate_of:{a}(jaccard~{est:.2f})")
                excluded_as_near_dup.add(b)

    surviving_ids = [i for i in surviving_ids if i not in excluded_as_near_dup]

    # Stage 3: quality/language/PII/toxicity filtering.
    for doc_id in list(surviving_ids):
        decision = filter_document(doc_id, texts[doc_id], allowed_languages=allowed_languages)
        if not decision.kept:
            records[doc_id].decision = "excluded"
            records[doc_id].exclusion_reasons.extend(decision.reasons)
            surviving_ids.remove(doc_id)

    # Stage 4: contamination check against the held-out eval set.
    contam_index = build_contamination_index(eval_docs, n=contamination_n)
    contam_report = check_contamination(
        {i: texts[i] for i in surviving_ids}, contam_index,
        n=contamination_n, min_matching_ngrams=min_matching_ngrams,
    )
    for doc_id, hits in contam_report.items():
        records[doc_id].decision = "excluded"
        for eval_id, count in hits.items():
            records[doc_id].exclusion_reasons.append(
                f"contamination:{eval_id}({count} matching {contamination_n}-grams)")

    return [records[i] for i in raw_docs]


def corpus_summary(records: list) -> dict:
    kept = [r for r in records if r.decision == "kept"]
    excluded = [r for r in records if r.decision == "excluded"]
    reason_counts: dict[str, int] = {}
    for r in excluded:
        for reason in r.exclusion_reasons:
            key = reason.split(":")[0].split("(")[0]
            reason_counts[key] = reason_counts.get(key, 0) + 1
    return {
        "raw": len(records),
        "kept": len(kept),
        "excluded": len(excluded),
        "excluded_by_reason": reason_counts,
    }

## Assessment

Maps to the course's **corpus project (30%)** component — the largest single line in
AIE-203's assessment weighting, because this module's artifact is reused, unmodified, as
AIE-301-M02's training corpus. Per `dispatch.qualityGates`, gaming-resistance is the design
constraint, and this module states it more sharply than most: **a learner cannot pass by
submitting a large, unfiltered corpus, and cannot pass by submitting a small, aggressively
filtered corpus with no written reasoning either** — the grade is on the documented judgment
trail, explicitly independent of token count in both directions.

**Module exam / corpus-project submission.** The learner receives an unseen set of ~25 raw
documents (with source metadata), an unseen held-out eval set of 3–5 questions, and exactly
two planted defects drawn from this module's taxonomy: one detectable defect (an exact or
near-duplicate pair, a PII leak, or a verbatim contamination overlap the default pipeline
should catch) and one **undetectable-by-design** defect (a paraphrase-contaminated document,
a spelled-out PII instance, or a near-duplicate that spans the corpus/eval boundary rather
than sitting within the corpus). The learner must, in order: (a) **predict**, in writing,
before running the pipeline, which of the two defects the default pipeline will catch and
which it will not, and why, citing the specific mechanism (not "the PII filter" in general,
but "the SSN regex only matches digit-grouped input"); (b) **run** the pipeline and confirm
or correct that prediction against the actual manifest; (c) for the undetectable defect,
**propose** a concrete extension (a technique, not "run it again" or "lower a threshold to
zero") that would catch it, and state honestly what new false positives that extension would
introduce; (d) **write** a `ProvenanceRecord`-backed manifest for the full 25-document set
plus a consent-posture writeup, in the Lesson 4 template, for at least two of the provided
sources; (e) **submit** a corpus_summary whose kept/excluded counts and reasons match what an
instructor's independent run of the reference pipeline produces on the same seed. A
correct manifest with no written prediction, or a complete prediction with a manifest that
doesn't reproduce, both fail.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime.

- **Wall — MinHash's approximate numbers read as "broken" to a learner expecting exactness.**
  A learner who sees `estimate_jaccard` return `0.875` against a `true_jaccard` of `0.883`
  often assumes their `_stable_hash` or `_hash_functions` implementation has a bug.
  **Reroute:** have them raise `num_hashes` from 64 to 256 and watch the gap shrink, then
  drop it to 8 and watch the gap widen dramatically — the relationship between signature
  length and estimate quality needs to be *felt*, not read, before the approximation stops
  looking like an error.
- **Wall — "just lower the false-positive filters until nothing gets wrongly dropped."** A
  fast learner may try to fix Lab B's `contains_ssn_example_in_tutorial` false positive by
  weakening the SSN regex (e.g., requiring three consecutive matches, or excluding any
  document containing the word "tutorial"). **Reroute:** ask them to predict what that exact
  change does to a corpus containing a *real* leaked SSN inside a document that happens to
  also use the word "tutorial" once, anywhere — the false-negative cost of patching a
  false-positive is this module's central, recurring trade-off, and Lesson 2's misconception
  section exists to head this off before Lab C.
- **Signal — a learner whose Lab C Part 2 prediction marks every excluded document
  "duplicate."** This is the expected, correct-to-log wrong instinct — most learners
  initially conflate "the pipeline removed it" with "it was a duplicate," because Lesson 1
  is the freshest technique in memory. Log `struggle-productive`, not `blocked`, when they
  discover on verification that `contains_email` was removed by the PII filter, three stages
  after dedup, and the near-dup stage never touched it. Do **not** pre-empt this by walking
  through the pipeline order before they commit to a prediction.
- **Wall — the consent-posture writeup feels like busywork after three labs of real code.**
  Some learners rush the Lesson 4 template with one-line, generic answers ("scraped from the
  web, probably fine"). **Reroute:** have them write the posture for a source they'd
  personally be uncomfortable seeing their own writing enter *without being asked* — a
  private group chat export, a personal blog with no visible license — before writing it for
  the assessment's provided sources. The discomfort of writing that one honestly is the
  point; a posture that's easy to write for every source is a posture that isn't being
  applied with real judgment.

## Three-tier reading

This is the module the course's ethics thread was written for — not a boilerplate pointer to
it. Specific to corpus construction, using the vocabulary this module actually built.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Every document your `ProvenanceRecord` describes as "kept"
  was, before it was a row in a manifest, something a person wrote — a forum answer typed
  at 1am to help a stranger, a blog post, a photo caption, a comment they assumed would be
  read by the dozen people who followed that account. Scraping's legal status is actively,
  currently contested in courts on multiple continents at the time of this writing — not
  settled precedent you can cite once and stop thinking about, but live litigation whose
  outcome could retroactively change what "licensed" or "fair use" means for data you've
  already trained on. Treat every `license_or_consent` field in your manifest as a claim you
  might have to defend, because you might.

- **Tier 2 — what "publicly available" suppresses.** This module's own filters have a
  vocabulary problem worth sitting with: `identify_language`, `pii_scan`, and `toxicity_scan`
  all return confident-sounding structured output — a language code, a match, a keyword hit —
  and structured output *reads* as settled. "Publicly available" has the same shape of
  false settledness: it is a true, checkable fact about *access* (nothing stopped you from
  downloading it) wearing the clothes of a moral fact about *permission* (someone agreed to
  this use). Nobody who posted an answer on a help forum in 2015, under that platform's 2015
  norms, consented to that answer being one of billions of examples teaching a 2026 model how
  to write. Availability was never consent — the field's language quietly lets the first fact
  stand in for the second, and this module's consent-posture template exists specifically to
  force the two apart, in writing, source by source, instead of letting "we scraped it and it
  was public" answer a question it was never actually an answer to.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Lab C's own `check_contamination` and `filter_document` have a
  property you built and verified yourself: every one of them has a real, provable blind
  spot — a paraphrase that evades n-gram matching, a spelled-out number that evades a regex,
  a keyword list that misses hostility phrased differently. Your corpus almost certainly
  contains, right now, at least one document from someone who — if you could ask them,
  individually, today — would say no. You cannot identify who. No filter in this module, or
  in any real production pipeline, will find them for you; that is not a gap in your
  implementation, it is a structural limit of every technique this lesson taught, stated
  honestly instead of quietly assumed away. You will train anyway, or you will not — and
  "the pipeline didn't flag anything" is not the same claim as "no one would object," no
  matter how satisfying a clean `corpus_summary` feels to look at. Decide which one you're
  actually claiming, and write it down in the consent posture where the next person to touch
  this corpus — quite possibly a version of you, in AIE-301, who no longer has the raw
  sources open in another tab — can find it.

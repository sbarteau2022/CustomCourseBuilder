## Module overview

You are about to be handed a system. It will look like it works. Read the next section before you
decide whether it does.

**The system.** A three-line keyword rule that routes customer-support tickets into `billing`,
`technical`, `account`, or `other`. Here is its demo — five tickets, five correct routings, run live
in front of you:

| Ticket | Router says | Actually is |
|---|---|---|
| "My invoice this month looks wrong, I was charged twice." | billing | billing |
| "The app crashes every time I open the settings page." | technical | technical |
| "I can't log in, it says my password is incorrect." | account | account |
| "I want to cancel my subscription and get a refund." | billing | billing |
| "The export button is broken, nothing happens when I click it." | technical | technical |

Five for five. Everyone in the room nods. Someone reaches for the "ship it" button. **Stop, and
answer the only question that matters before that button gets pressed: does this system work?**

Not "did it work on these five tickets" — the five tickets you just watched succeed. *Does it work*,
as a claim about the tickets it hasn't seen yet, the ones it will actually be run on tomorrow. Try to
answer that question right now, with what you have in front of you. You cannot. Not because the
system is bad — you don't know that either — but because **nothing you have seen so far is evidence
about anything other than these five tickets**, and you did not pick these five tickets; someone
picked them *to show you a demo*. This module is what happens when you refuse to let that be good
enough.

The descent: Lesson 1 puts a number on the gap between "the demo" and "the truth" — you will watch
the same system's score fall from a perfect 100% to something much more modest the moment it's run
against tickets nobody chose to flatter it, and you will learn to name *why* a demo is structurally
incapable of being evidence. Lesson 2 formalizes what would have made the number trustworthy in the
first place — an eval is not a test set, it is five specific, separable commitments (task definition,
gold set, metric, decision rule, statistic), and skipping any one of them makes the resulting number
unfalsifiable. Lesson 3 shows that *building those five commitments after you've already seen the
system's outputs* silently reintroduces the exact failure AIE-201-M05 spent a whole module teaching
you to close — a human or a metric that has seen the answer key can be talked into grading generously,
even in perfect good faith, and the resulting score is now measuring the grader's leniency, not the
system. Lesson 4 shows that even a metric with no human in the loop at all — a pure, deterministic
formula — is not automatically safe from this, because optimization pressure (a system built,
consciously or not, to score well) will find whatever gap a metric leaves open, and a metric that has
never been tested against an adversary has not yet earned the right to be trusted. Re-ascent: by the
end of this module you will not be able to look at a demo, a leaderboard number, or a "94% accuracy"
slide without immediately asking the three questions this module trains into reflex — *was the gold
set built before or after you saw the outputs, what exactly counts as correct, and has anyone tried
to break this metric on purpose* — the same discipline every remaining module of this course, and
every eval you build in every course after it, inherits from here.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The demo trap: "it seems to work" is not evidence

**Teaching text**

A demo is a sample of size *n*, and the sample was not drawn at random — it was drawn by someone
(possibly you) with an interest in the outcome looking good. That is the entire content of this
lesson, and it is enough, on its own, to disqualify "I watched it work a few times" as evidence of
anything beyond the few times you watched. Three specific reasons a demo systematically overstates
quality, stacked:

1. **Selection.** Whoever built the demo — deliberately or, far more often, by innocently reaching
   for examples that came to mind first — reaches for cases the system is known or suspected to
   handle well. This is not usually dishonesty; it's the same instinct that makes you show a friend
   your best photos, not a random sample of your camera roll.
2. **No denominator.** "It got these right" tells you a numerator. Without a denominator — how many
   *were tried*, out of how many that exist in the world — a numerator is not a rate, and only a rate
   can be compared to anything.
3. **No adversary.** A demo audience is, structurally, on the system's side; nobody in the room is
   being paid to find the case that breaks it. The tickets that would embarrass the router — the ones
   phrased without any of its trigger keywords — are exactly the tickets a demo-builder has no reason
   to go looking for.

None of this requires bad faith. A perfectly honest engineer, asked "does it work," will reach for
the five tickets they remember testing that worked, because those are the ones that come to mind —
this is availability bias, not fraud, and it is why "just be careful and honest" is not a fix: the
bias operates *before* any decision to deceive is even made.

**Worked example — the ticket router, run twice**

The system: a keyword-rule router.

```python
def route_ticket(text: str) -> str:
    t = text.lower()
    if any(k in t for k in ["invoice", "charge", "payment", "billed", "refund", "subscription"]):
        return "billing"
    if any(k in t for k in ["error", "crash", "bug", "broken", "not working", "won't load", "freezes"]):
        return "technical"
    if any(k in t for k in ["password", "log in", "login", "locked out", "2fa", "verification code"]):
        return "account"
    return "other"
```

Run it on the five demo tickets from the module overview: **5/5 = 100%.** Now run the exact same,
unmodified function against a 40-ticket gold set assembled *before* anyone looked at what the router
would say about them — the five demo tickets plus 35 more, spanning the same four categories,
including tickets phrased the way real customers actually write (without helpfully including the
router's trigger words):

| Set | Correct | Total | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Demo (hand-picked) | 5 | 5 | **100.0%** |
| Full gold set | 33 | 40 | **82.5%** |
| Majority-class baseline (always guess `billing`, the most common true label) | 14 | 40 | 35.0% |

The system is not fraudulent and the demo was not rigged — every one of those five demo tickets
really does route correctly. What changed is *what got measured*. The full gold set includes tickets
like *"Notifications stopped working after the last update"* (true: `technical`) and *"My account got
suspended and I don't know why"* (true: `account`) — genuine tickets a real support inbox receives
constantly, containing none of the router's trigger words, so the router falls through to `other`
every time. **These are not edge cases the demo forgot to mention. They are the majority of the
router's actual failure mode**, invisible in a demo built entirely from keyword-rich phrasing, because
keyword-rich phrasing is what a person testing "does my keyword router work" naturally reaches for.

The 17.5-point gap (100% demo → 82.5% honest) is not the headline finding. The headline finding is
that **you had no way to know the size, or even the direction, of that gap from the demo alone** —
the demo gave you exactly zero information about the system's true error rate, because it was never
a sample of the deployment distribution, it was a sample of the distribution *of things this system
happens to be good at*.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"If I've looked at a bunch of examples and nothing seemed wrong, it probably works."** "A bunch"
  without a denominator and without a pre-committed sampling procedure is not a rate — five glowing
  examples and forty representative ones can report 100% and 82.5% from the *identical, unmodified*
  system, and only one of those numbers means anything about tomorrow's tickets.
- **"A demo that impresses stakeholders is meaningful evidence of quality."** It's evidence the demo
  was well-chosen, which is a fact about the demo-builder's selection process, not the system.
- **"If nothing is obviously wrong, nothing is wrong."** Absence of a noticed failure in a small,
  favorably-selected sample is not evidence of a low failure rate — it is what a 17.5-point gap looks
  like from the inside, before you've built the gold set that would reveal it.
- **"The right sample size for 'does it work' is whatever I happen to have tried."** Five is not a
  small honest mistake away from forty; it is a different, structurally biased sampling process, and
  no amount of *more* biased sampling (trying ten more keyword-rich tickets) closes the gap — only a
  sample built to represent the real distribution does.

### Lesson 2 — What an eval actually is: a claim about the world, made of five separable parts

**Teaching text**

"We have an eval" is not a single claim — it is shorthand for five separate, independently checkable
commitments, and the failure mode this lesson exists to prevent is treating any one of them as
optional:

1. **Task definition.** What, precisely, is the system being asked to do, and what does a correct
   response look like *before* any system has attempted it? "Route the ticket to the right team" is
   not a task definition until "right" is operationalized — right according to whom, checked against
   what.
2. **Gold set.** A fixed collection of inputs paired with a correct answer, assembled to represent
   the distribution the system will actually face in deployment — not the distribution that's easiest
   to write, or the distribution the system happens to already handle well (Lesson 1).
3. **Metric.** A function from (system output, gold answer) to a number. Accuracy, F1, exact match —
   the specific formula matters, and Lesson 4 will show that not every plausible-sounding formula
   measures what it claims to.
4. **Decision rule.** What number, or comparison, counts as "it works"? Beats a stated baseline by a
   stated margin; clears an absolute threshold; wins a paired comparison against the previous system.
   Without this, a metric produces a number with no verdict attached, and a number with no verdict is
   trivially easy to spin in either direction after the fact.
5. **Statistic.** How much does this number vary if you'd sampled a slightly different gold set, or
   re-run a stochastic system? (M03 builds this properly; for now, notice only that a single number
   with no attached sense of its own noise cannot be told apart from a fluke.)

**An eval is a claim about the world** — "this system correctly handles at least X% of tickets drawn
from distribution D, as judged by rule R" — and, exactly like any claim, it is only as strong as the
weakest of the specific, checkable commitments behind it. Drop the gold set's connection to the real
distribution (Lesson 1) and the claim is about the wrong world. Drop the decision rule and the claim
has no verdict, only a number free to be described however is convenient. Drop the metric's own
soundness (Lesson 4) and the number itself is untrustworthy regardless of how honestly it was
computed. This is the direct generalization of AIE-201-M05's evaluation contract: there, "fit only on
train" was the specific clause that, when violated, let information leak across the wall between
what a model was shaped by and what it was graded on. Here, the wall has the same shape and a wider
scope — it separates *when the eval's five commitments were fixed* from *when the system's actual
behavior became visible to anyone doing the fixing*. Lesson 3 makes that precise.

**Worked example — turning "does the router work" into five commitments**

| Component | Vague version (what most teams actually write down) | The five-commitment version |
|---|---|---|
| Task definition | "Route tickets correctly" | "Given ticket text, output exactly one of {billing, technical, account, other}; correct = the category a trained support lead would assign, working from the ticket text alone, with no access to account history" |
| Gold set | "Some tickets we tried" | 40 tickets sampled to mirror real intake volume by category (35% billing, 25% technical, 20% account, 20% other — matching last quarter's true distribution), each independently labeled by a support lead *before* the router ever saw them |
| Metric | "Percent correct" (unstated: correct how? partial credit for close categories?) | Exact-match accuracy: 1 if predicted category equals gold category, else 0, no partial credit — chosen because a ticket routed to the wrong team creates real handoff cost regardless of how "close" the categories seem |
| Decision rule | "If it looks good, ship it" | Must exceed the majority-class baseline (35.0%, Lesson 1) by at least 30 points **and** score above 60% within every individual category, not just on average |
| Statistic | (absent) | Report accuracy with a 95% confidence interval from the 40-ticket sample (M03 builds the interval; for now, just the commitment to report *a* spread, not a bare point estimate) |

Notice what the "vague version" column is missing is not effort — a team that writes "route tickets
correctly" has usually put real thought into the *system*. What's missing is that none of the five
commitments were made **specific enough to be violated**, and a commitment that cannot be violated
cannot be checked. "Percent correct" sounds identical whether or not partial credit is being silently
awarded; "some tickets we tried" sounds identical whether they were sampled to represent deployment or
cherry-picked to flatter a demo. The five-commitment version is more work to write and takes exactly
the same system to evaluate — the router's code did not change between the two columns. What changed
is whether the resulting number is falsifiable.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"An eval is just a test set of examples."** A gold set with no metric attached cannot produce a
  number; a metric with no decision rule attached cannot produce a verdict. "We have a test set" is
  necessary and, on its own, answers nothing.
- **"Accuracy alone is a complete eval."** Accuracy is one candidate metric for one kind of task; it
  says nothing about which errors are cheap and which are expensive (a ticket misrouted from
  `billing` to `technical` is a minor delay; one misrouted away from a `fraud` category, if this
  router had one, could be a real incident) — the metric has to be chosen to match the cost structure
  of the actual errors, not defaulted to because it's the easiest number to compute.
- **"If two people basically agree when they eyeball the outputs, we don't need a written rubric."**
  Informal agreement that isn't pinned to a specific, written task definition tends to erode under
  disagreement precisely when it matters most — on the hard, ambiguous cases a written rubric exists
  to resolve, not the easy ones two people would have agreed on anyway.
- **"You can build the gold set from the system's own outputs, as long as you're careful about it."**
  Lesson 3 is the specific, quantified rebuttal to this — "careful" is not a strong enough defense
  against a bias that operates on people who are, by every measure, being careful.

### Lesson 3 — Eval-order bias: why the gold set has to be built before the system's outputs are seen

**Teaching text**

AIE-201-M05's central mechanism was this: a transform that is *fit* on data that later gets called
"test" has, by construction, been shaped by information a genuinely deployed system could never have
had. The eval-design version of that same mechanism has a human, not a `StandardScaler`, doing the
fitting — and it is, if anything, harder to notice, because nothing about it looks like code executed
in the wrong order.

Here is the mechanism. A grader who is shown a ticket **and the system's already-computed prediction
for it, before deciding what the correct label should be**, is not making an independent judgment
anymore — they are making a judgment that has been anchored by an answer already sitting in front of
them. This is not a claim about dishonest graders. A scrupulously honest grader, shown the router's
guess of `other` for a ticket that doesn't obviously belong anywhere else, will often reason "well...
`other` is defensible" — a judgment they would not have reached, or would have reached far less often,
if asked to categorize the same ticket cold, with no system output anchoring the decision. **The
grader is not lying. The order of operations changed what "defensible" meant to them, in the moment,
without their noticing it happen.** This is exactly the shape of AIE-201-M05's `StandardScaler`
leak — no one touched a label improperly, no one cheated, and the resulting number is still
compromised, because information crossed a wall it was never supposed to cross.

**Worked example — blind grading vs. hindsight grading, on the same 40 tickets**

Recall the router's seven full-gold-set misses from Lesson 1 — all seven were mispredicted as `other`,
because all seven are keyword-poor, real-sounding phrasings ("Notifications stopped working after the
last update," "My account got suspended and I don't know why," and five more like them). Compare two
labeling protocols applied to the *identical* 40 tickets and the *identical* router:

- **Blind protocol.** A grader reads each ticket and commits a gold label *before* the router's
  prediction is computed or shown. This is the Lesson 1 gold set, exactly as built.
- **Hindsight protocol.** A grader is shown the ticket **and** the router's prediction already filled
  in, and decides whether to accept it. On this dataset, five of the seven keyword-poor misses get
  rubber-stamped as "`other`, close enough" — a specific, documented leniency pattern used here in
  place of running the actual human experiment, so the resulting numbers are exactly reproducible; the
  mechanism is what transfers, not this particular draw.

| Protocol | Correct | Total | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Blind (Lesson 1's honest number) | 33 | 40 | **82.5%** |
| Hindsight (grader sees the prediction first) | 38 | 40 | **95.0%** |

**A 12.5-point swing, from the same router, the same 40 tickets, the same seven true failure cases —
the only thing that moved is when, in the sequence of events, a human being was shown the system's
answer.** And notice the direction the bias runs: hindsight leniency can only ever *forgive* an error
that blind grading would have caught; it has no mechanism for inventing a new error blind grading
would have missed. This makes eval-order bias, like AIE-201-M05's peeked-hyperparameter bias, a
**one-directional, systematically optimistic** bias — never negative, never a coin flip, always
pushing the reported number in the direction that makes the system look better than blind measurement
would show. That asymmetry is precisely why it is dangerous: a biased-but-random error would wash out
across many evals; a biased-but-directional one accumulates, quietly, every time a team builds or
revises a rubric with the system's actual outputs already sitting in front of them.

The fix is procedural, not moral: **commit the gold set — the labels, the rubric, the decision rule —
before anyone with grading authority has seen what the system being evaluated actually produced.**
This is the eval-design analogue of AIE-201-M05's "test is touched exactly once": the gold set is the
test set, and the moment its labels are allowed to react to system output, it has quietly become a
second training set for the *evaluator's own leniency*.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"As long as the grader is careful and honest, hindsight order doesn't matter."** The worked
  example's grader is stipulated to be honest — leniency here is not lying, it's anchoring, a
  well-documented judgment effect that operates on careful people exactly as it operates on careless
  ones.
- **"An eval built by the same team that built the system is fine as long as no one's trying to
  cheat."** The 12.5-point gap requires no intent to inflate anything; it is a structural property of
  seeing the answer before writing the answer key, present even when literally every party is acting
  in good faith.
- **"If the metric is a quantitative formula instead of a human judgment call, eval order doesn't
  matter."** This misconception is specifically Lesson 4's target — a formula is not immune to being
  *chosen or tuned* after seeing what a specific system does, which reintroduces the identical bias
  one level up, in the metric's design instead of a single label's grading.
- **"A one-time leniency gap is a minor rounding issue, not something worth a whole lesson."** The gap
  measured here (12.5 points) moved the router from "clearing the 82.5%-vs-baseline story of Lesson 1"
  to "looking nearly perfect" — on identical underlying performance; a 95% headline number built this
  way is not a small distortion of the truth, it is a different number entirely, describing the
  grader's forgiveness rather than the system's accuracy.

### Lesson 4 — Gaming resistance: a metric has not earned trust until something has tried to beat it

**Teaching text**

Lesson 3 showed that a *human* who sees a system's outputs before finalizing the rubric can be
unconsciously talked into leniency. This lesson shows the same failure survives even when you remove
the human from the loop entirely and replace grading with a pure, deterministic formula — because a
formula can be *exploited* exactly as a person can be *anchored*, just by a different mechanism:
**optimization pressure**. Any metric that becomes the thing a system is built, tuned, or selected
against will, eventually, get gamed — not necessarily through malice, but because "make the number go
up" and "actually do the task well" are only the same objective for as long as the metric happens to
be a perfect proxy for the task, and almost no metric survives contact with real optimization pressure
as a perfect proxy for very long. This is Goodhart's law, and the discipline it demands is concrete:
**a metric has not earned trust until it has been deliberately tested against at least one system
built specifically to exploit it.**

**Worked example — a summarization metric gamed by doing less work, not more**

Task: given a support ticket, produce a short summary. Reference (gold) summaries are hand-written,
extractive phrases drawn from each ticket's own vocabulary. The metric handed to you, as a first
attempt: **word-overlap recall** — what fraction of the reference summary's words appear anywhere in
the system's output.

```python
def naive_recall(reference: str, system_output: str) -> float:
    ref_words = set(reference.lower().split())
    out_words = set(system_output.lower().split())
    return len(ref_words & out_words) / len(ref_words)
```

Two systems, evaluated on five tickets:

- **System A** — a genuine extractive summarizer. Real compression, real judgment about what to keep.
  *"Invoice shows a duplicate charge for the pro plan."*
- **System B** — the gamed system. Returns the entire, unedited ticket text, verbatim, every time.
  *"My invoice this month looks wrong, I was charged twice for the pro plan and I need this fixed
  before my card is charged again next week."*

| Metric | System A (genuine) mean | System B (echoes the whole ticket) mean |
|---|---:|---:|
| `naive_recall` | **0.693** | **0.960** |
| `precision` (of the *output's* words, how many are in the reference) | 0.417 | 0.210 |
| `f1` (harmonic mean of precision and recall) | **0.514** | **0.342** |

Under the metric as originally handed to you, **the system that does no summarizing at all — the
system that is, by any human reading, obviously not a summary — outscores the genuine summarizer on
every single ticket in the set except one tie**, because a reference summary's words are, by
construction, a subset of the words already present in its own source ticket, and a system that
returns the whole ticket can never miss a reference word. `naive_recall` does not measure
summarization; it measures whether the output contains enough words, and the cheapest possible way to
guarantee that is to include *all* of them. **This is not a bug in System B — System B is a correct,
even elegant, solution to the metric exactly as written.** The bug is in the metric.

Adding a `precision` term — what fraction of the *output's* words are actually relevant, not just
whether the relevant words are present *somewhere* in a potentially enormous output — and combining it
with recall via the harmonic mean (`f1`) closes the exploit: System B's precision collapses (0.210,
roughly half of System A's 0.417) because most of the words in "the entire ticket" are not in the
short reference summary, and `f1` is low whenever *either* precision or recall is low. Under `f1`,
System A wins on every single ticket, not just on average. **The fix did not make the metric stricter
in general — it made the metric ask a second, previously-missing question** ("is the output also
*concise*?") that the original formula never asked, because conciseness was never explicitly stated as
part of the task definition (Lesson 2) in the first place. A gameable metric is very often not a wrong
formula so much as an *incomplete task definition wearing a formula's clothes*.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"A metric with a clear numeric formula must be objective and hard to game."** Objective and
  gameable are unrelated properties — `naive_recall` is perfectly objective (deterministic, no human
  judgment) and was gamed on the very first system anyone deliberately tried to game it with.
- **"If my metric correlates with quality on every system I've tried so far, it will keep doing so."**
  Correlation observed only across systems nobody built *to* exploit the metric says nothing about
  what happens the moment someone (or some optimization process) starts building specifically against
  it — which is exactly the condition every metric eventually faces once it's used to select, rank, or
  train systems.
- **"Gaming a metric requires actively trying to cheat; it can't happen by good-faith optimization."**
  A team honestly trying to raise a recall-only summarization score, with zero intent to deceive, is
  incentivized by the metric itself to make outputs longer — the exploit does not require anyone to
  intend it, only for the metric's blind spot to exist and for something (a person, a training loop,
  an automated search) to apply pressure against it.
- **"The fix for a gamed metric is always to make it stricter."** `f1` is not a stricter version of
  `naive_recall` — it's recall combined with a *different* question the original formula never asked
  at all; identifying which question a gameable metric is missing (here: conciseness) is the actual
  diagnostic skill, not "add a penalty until the exploit goes away."

    correct = 0
    for text, label in gold:
        pred = router(text)
        if pred == label:

    return correct, len(gold)

```python
import re
import statistics as st

# ... TICKETS, system_a, system_b identical to starter ...

def words(text: str) -> list[str]:
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9']+", text.lower())

def naive_recall(reference, system_output):
    ref_words = set(words(reference))
    out_words = set(words(system_output))
    if not ref_words:
        return 0.0
    return len(ref_words & out_words) / len(ref_words)

def precision(reference, system_output):
    ref_words = set(words(reference))
    out_words = set(words(system_output))
    if not out_words:
        return 0.0
    return len(ref_words & out_words) / len(out_words)

def f1(reference, system_output):
    r = naive_recall(reference, system_output)
    p = precision(reference, system_output)
    if p + r == 0:
        return 0.0
    return 2 * p * r / (p + r)

def score_all(metric, system):
    return [metric(ref, system(ticket)) for ticket, ref in TICKETS]

def mean(scores):
    return st.mean(scores)
```

## Assessment

Graded on the judgment trail — **commit, design, apply, prove** — never on "the final metric ranked
the systems correctly." A learner who happens to submit a metric that ranks two withheld systems
correctly, but cannot show the commitments were made before the systems were seen, scores no higher
than a learner whose metric got the ranking wrong.

**Task.** You are given only a **task specification** — a one-paragraph description of a system's job
(a different domain from this module's labs; provided at assessment time, drawn from the same family
as "classify this input into one of a fixed set of categories" or "produce a short, faithful summary
of this input"). No system, no example outputs, no gold-labeled data. Before any of those exist for
you:

1. **Commit, in writing:** a task definition specific enough to be violated; a gold-set sampling plan
   that states how examples will be drawn to represent the real deployment distribution (not "whatever
   I can think of"); a metric with a fully specified formula; a decision rule stating what score, and
   what margin over what baseline, would count as "it works."
2. Only after that commitment is submitted and timestamped, you are handed a **gold set** (still no
   system) and asked to independently label it yourself, from the task text alone — this is your
   blind labeling pass, and it is compared against a provided reference labeling for calibration.
3. You are then handed **two systems' outputs** on your gold set — one a good-faith attempt, one built
   specifically to exploit a plausible-but-naive version of the metric you might have designed. Apply
   your committed metric and decision rule to both. If your metric ranks the gamed system as good or
   better than the genuine one, you must diagnose *why* (which commitment from step 1 was the gap) and
   propose a specific, minimal fix — then re-score both systems under the fixed metric.
4. **Prove it:** report both systems' final scores under your (possibly revised) metric, and write a
   short, specific explanation of why this number can be trusted — naming, explicitly, that the gold
   set and metric were committed before the systems were seen, and, if you found and fixed a gaming
   exploit, what specific commitment from step 1 was too weak to catch it the first time.

**Gaming resistance.** Two defenses, stacked. First, the task specification changes per assessment
attempt and neither the gold set nor the two systems' outputs are available until after step 1 is
submitted and timestamped — a learner cannot pass by recognizing this module's own ticket-router or
summarization examples, because the domain is different and the commitment must be made *before* any
domain-specific pattern-matching is possible. Second, and more important: **step 1 is graded before
step 3 is attempted, and a low-quality step 1 caps the maximum score regardless of how well steps 3–4
go** — a learner who writes a vague or hedged commitment ("I'll use accuracy and a reasonable
baseline") and then, through trial and error while looking at the two systems' outputs, arrives at a
metric that happens to rank them correctly, has not demonstrated the assessed skill. The assessed
skill is committing to specific, falsifiable choices *before* the information that would let you
retrofit them is available — exactly the discipline this module's three labs built, and exactly the
discipline a working evaluator needs on the day nobody hands them a labeled "watch out, this one's
gameable" ticket.

| Criterion | Gaming-resistant because... | Excellent (4) | Adequate (3) | Developing (2) | Insufficient (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Commit — specific, falsifiable, timestamped before exposure** | Must be submitted before the gold set or any system output exists for the learner | All five components (task definition, sampling plan, metric formula, decision rule with baseline+margin, statistic) are specific enough that a violation would be checkable | Four of five components meet that bar; one is present but vague (e.g., "reasonable margin" with no number) | Two or fewer components are specific; the rest are placeholders | No written commitment exists before step 2, or commitments are so vague nothing could violate them |
| **Blind labeling — independent judgment, not pattern-matching a hint** | Labels are produced from the task text alone, before any system output is visible, and compared to a reference | Learner's independent labels agree with the reference labeling on the large majority of the gold set, with disagreements explainable by genuine task ambiguity, not carelessness | Reasonable agreement, with one or two disagreements traceable to the learner's task definition being under-specified in step 1 | Low agreement, and the learner cannot explain the mismatch by pointing to a specific ambiguity in their own task definition | No independent labeling attempted, or labels were produced after seeing system output |
| **Apply & detect gaming — a real diagnostic, not a lucky ranking** | Requires correctly identifying *whether* the committed metric is exploitable on an unseen adversarial system, and if so, exactly which commitment was the gap | Correctly identifies whether the gamed system beats or ties the genuine one under the original metric; if it does, names the specific missing dimension (precision, cost-weighting, etc.) and proposes a minimally-scoped fix | Correctly identifies gaming when present, but the named cause is generic ("the metric needs to be better") rather than tied to a specific missing check | Gaming is present but misdiagnosed, or a fix is proposed that also changes unrelated, non-broken parts of the metric | Gaming is present and undetected, or falsely claimed present when the original metric was actually sound |
| **Prove — a trustworthiness argument, not just a final number** | Requires explaining *why* the number should be believed, which a learner who got lucky on the ranking cannot fabricate convincingly | Explicitly connects the reported scores to the fact that commitments preceded exposure, and (if applicable) states precisely what the pre-fix metric would have missed and why the post-fix metric closes that specific gap | Reports correct final scores with a generic trustworthiness statement ("I committed the metric first") lacking the specific gap-and-fix connection | Reports scores with no explanation of why the order of operations matters here | No final scores reported, or the explanation contradicts what was actually done (e.g., claims blind labeling occurred but step 2's timestamp shows otherwise) |

A submission that scores 4 on Apply & Detect but 1 on Commit does not pass — a metric that happens to
rank two withheld systems correctly, arrived at without ever having written down a falsifiable
commitment beforehand, has not demonstrated the discipline this module exists to certify, even if the
final ranking is right.

## Elle pacing notes

**The wall: "the demo really did work, though — why doesn't that count for anything?"** This is the
single most common friction point in Lesson 1, and it is worth taking seriously rather than
overriding — the demo genuinely did produce five correct routings; the learner is not wrong about
that fact. **Reroute:** do not argue the demo was fake or dishonest. Instead, ask the learner to
compute, on paper, what accuracy the *identical* five-ticket demo methodology would have produced if
the five tickets had instead been drawn from the seven known failure cases — walking through why the
demo's selection process, not any property of the system, determined which number came out. This
should resolve in one pass; if the learner is still defending "but it worked in the room" after that
reframing, the issue is likely a deeper discomfort with the module's central claim (that a true,
observed success can still be worthless as evidence) and is worth a direct conversation, not another
worked example.

**The wall: building a gold set before the system exists, or before seeing its outputs, feels
backwards.** Nearly every learner arrives at this module having built things "system first, checked it
after" their whole life, and Lesson 3's blind-before-hindsight ordering can read as an arbitrary
process rule rather than a load-bearing one. **Reroute:** point directly back to AIE-201-M05's "test
is touched exactly once" clause — this is not a new rule, it's the same rule, and the learner already
built the muscle for it in the prerequisite module; the task here is transfer, not re-learning.
Naming the parallel explicitly is usually enough; if it isn't, have the learner re-derive Lab 2's
12.5-point gap by hand, tracing exactly which of the seven mispredicted tickets got rubber-stamped and
why — seeing the specific mechanism operate on specific tickets closes the gap between "I'm told this
matters" and "I can see why."

**Signal — a learner surprised at their own leniency when hand-grading Lab 2's hindsight condition.**
This is exactly on track, not a failure to note with concern. A learner who predicted "hindsight
grading won't affect *my* judgment, I'm being careful" and then, doing the exercise by hand, catches
themselves wanting to accept `other` as defensible for an ambiguous ticket has had the lesson land in
the most durable possible way — log `struggle-productive`, and consider it evidence the module is
working, not evidence the learner needs remediation.

**The wall: Lab 3's `f1` fix feels like it "should" have been obvious from the start — why wasn't
`naive_recall` written correctly the first time?** **Reroute:** this is worth *not* rushing past.
Have the learner name one additional property, beyond conciseness, that a summarization metric might
still be missing even after adding precision (faithfulness — does the summary say anything the ticket
didn't? fluency? category-specific completeness?) — the point is not that `f1` is now "the" correct
metric, it's that every metric is a specific, incomplete set of questions, and the discipline is
*testing against an adversary*, not eventually landing on a metric so complete no adversary could ever
find a gap.

**Accelerate condition.** A learner who, in Lab 3's Predict step, correctly names in advance that
`naive_recall` is structurally guaranteed to never penalize System B (not just "probably will score it
higher") has already demonstrated the core assessed skill of anticipating a metric's blind spot before
running code. Such a learner can move directly from Lab 3's Predict to the Assessment's step 1 without
re-deriving Lab 3's specific numbers first — the transfer has already happened.

**Struggle triage, condensed:**

| Symptom | Productive (let it run) | Blocked (intervene) |
|---|---|---|

## Three-tier reading — the score that was true and still misled

**Tier 1 — material ground.** Every number in this module's worked examples is a property of code you
can re-run: the router's 100%-demo-vs-82.5%-honest gap, the 12.5-point hindsight-leniency gap, the
`naive_recall`-vs-`f1` ranking reversal are all reproducible to the third decimal place from the labs'
own solution code. None of this is a matter of interpretation — a metric either did or did not rank
System B above System A, and you can check. This grounding is the whole reason the module opens with a
demo rather than a lecture: the claim "a demo is not evidence" is not asked to be taken on faith, it is
handed to you as a specific, falsifiable, 17.5-point number.

**Tier 2 — what a passing eval suppresses.** A system that clears every commitment this module
teaches — a gold set built blind, before exposure; a metric pressure-tested against an adversary; a
decision rule cleared by a real margin — has still only answered the question its task definition and
gold-set sampling plan decided to ask. The router in Lesson 2's "five-commitment version" table is
graded against "the category a trained support lead would assign, working from the ticket text alone"
— a definition that quietly excludes every case where the right routing actually depends on account
history, prior tickets, or context the text alone can't carry. That exclusion is not a flaw in the
methodology this module teaches; it's a choice, made once, by whoever wrote the task definition, and a
clean, honest, gaming-resistant eval built on top of a narrow task definition will report a number
that is completely true and still describes a narrower claim than "this system handles support
tickets." The industry-scale version of this is exactly what this course's ethics thread names:
benchmark leaderboards report real, honestly-computed, hard-fought numbers on specific, narrowly
defined tasks, and the gap between *that specific measured task* and the broader capability claim made
about a model in a press release or a sales deck is not a leak in the benchmark's methodology — it is
a choice about scope, made once, upstream of every number that follows, and rarely stated as loudly as
the number itself.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** Every discipline in this module — commit before exposure, grade blind, test
your metric against an adversary — defends against your eval being *wrong*. None of it defends against
your eval being *right* and someone asking you to make it say yes anyway: narrow the task definition
until an unflattering case falls outside scope; pick the gold set that happens to undersample the
category your system handles worst; choose the metric, honestly, from among several defensible
options, because it's the one most likely to clear the bar this quarter. Every one of those moves can
be executed by someone who has read this entire module, agrees with every word of it, and still, under
real pressure, makes the choice that flatters the number rather than the one that best answers the
question a deployment actually needs answered. The tools in this module catch a leaked gold set, a
gamed metric, a demo mistaken for evidence — they were built to, and they work. They were never built
to catch, and cannot catch, a technically clean eval aimed at the wrong question on purpose. You will,
at some point, build an eval exactly this clean, and someone will ask you to point it somewhere
friendlier. Decide now — before the system exists, before anyone is watching, while it costs you
nothing — what your number is not for sale for.

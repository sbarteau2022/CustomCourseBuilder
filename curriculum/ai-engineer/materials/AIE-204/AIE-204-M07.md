## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The anatomy of a report that survives an adversarial read

A **skeptic**, for this module's purposes, is not a hostile strawman and not a generically careful
reader. It is a specific kind of reader: someone whose incentive is *misaligned* with your
conclusion — a competitor's due-diligence analyst, a board member who has to defend a budget
decision to someone above them, a regulator, an engineer whose own system your report is about to
replace — and who is therefore going to read your report looking, specifically, for the one claim
it makes that the evidence doesn't fully support. The standard this module holds a report to is not
"would a friendly reviewer nod along." It is "would this survive someone who is actively trying to
break it, and who has every reason to succeed."

A report built for that reader has six parts, and a report missing any one of them is not defensible
yet, no matter how well the other five are written:

1. **Claim** — the specific decision the report is making a case for (ship / don't ship / needs a
   fix), stated once, plainly, at the top — not buried under three paragraphs of context.
2. **Method** — what was measured, how the gold set was built and by whom, and how the judge (if
   there is one) was validated, not just used.
3. **Evidence, at both levels** — the aggregate statistic *and* the subgroup or category breakdown
   underneath it, reported together, every time. Never the aggregate alone.
4. **Stated confidence** — a real number (a kappa with its Landis & Koch band name, a p-value with
   its confidence interval, an agreement rate with its n) standing in for the word "confident,"
   never the word alone.
5. **Limitations** — specific, named gaps in what was actually checked, not a generic disclaimer.
6. **A falsification criterion** — a real, checkable condition that, if it turned out to be true,
   would change the report's conclusion, stated in advance, not improvised if someone asks.

**Worked example — the one-line version, and what it doesn't survive.** A real internal report for
this course's running example, the ticket-resolution summarizer, could read: *"Our LLM judge
validated at a weighted kappa of 0.84 — 'almost perfect' agreement — against a human-labeled gold
set. The summarizer is ready to ship."* Every word of this is true. It is also the exact one-line
report AIE-204-M04 Lesson 4's Misconception 1 named as insufficient, and it fails against three
questions any skeptic asks in the first thirty seconds: *Validated on what set, built by whom, at
what n?* (Method — unanswered.) *Is it blind to anything, and if so what?* (Evidence at the subgroup
level — unanswered; M04's own validation found the judge scores four of twenty items a full two
points too high, every time, because they contain plausible-sounding fabrication.) *What would
change your mind?* (Falsification criterion — unanswered; the sentence commits to nothing that could
turn out false.) A report that answers none of these three questions has not lied. It has simply
handed the skeptic three free openings, and a competent skeptic only needs one.

**Misconception 1 — "A report that states no limitations reads as more confident, and confidence is
persuasive."** For a friendly reader, maybe. For a skeptical one, the opposite is true: an unstated
limitation is the first thing a skeptic goes looking for, and finding one you *didn't* disclose does
not just cost you that one point — it recolors every other claim in the report as something you
might also be hiding something about. A report that names its own gap first is read as more
trustworthy on everything else, not less, because the skeptic's usual first move — "what are they
not telling me" — has already been answered.

**Misconception 2 — "More evidence is more defensible: more charts, more metrics, more pages."**
Volume is not the same as coverage of the specific place a skeptic will look. A report can run to
ten pages of dashboards and still never state the judge's weighted kappa's Landis & Koch band, or
whether the taxonomy behind an error-category number was checked against a second reader. Padding
that isn't the specific mechanism-level answer to a specific attack is noise a skeptic has to wade
through on the way to finding the hole anyway — it does not close the hole.

### Lesson 2 — The adversarial pre-mortem: finding your own report's holes before the skeptic does

**Self-consistency is not defensibility.** You wrote the report; of course it reads as convincing to
you — you already believe the conclusion and you know what you meant by every sentence. The only
way to find out whether a report survives an adversarial read is to *do the adversarial read
yourself, on purpose, before anyone else does*: a **sentence-level attack**. For every claim
sentence — not every sentence, every sentence that asserts something as evidence — write down the
single strongest objection a well-informed, motivated skeptic would raise against exactly that
sentence. If the report doesn't already answer it, either qualify the sentence, add the missing
evidence, or remove the claim.

This course has spent six modules building the skeptic's actual toolkit, one module at a time.
Naming the pattern each attack comes from is not decoration — it tells you exactly which earlier
artifact answers it:

| Attack pattern | What the skeptic asks | Which module already gives you the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate hides subgroup | "The kappa is 0.84 — so what? What does it miss?" | M04's divergence-by-category, M06's shortfall-by-category |
| No confidence interval | "Is that 0.37-point gain real, or could it be noise?" | M03's power/significance discipline |
| Gold set provenance unstated | "Who built this set? Were labels reconciled? At what n?" | M02's gold-set-anatomy (criteria, measured agreement, edge cases) |
| Gaming resistance undemonstrated | "Has anyone actually tried to beat this metric?" | M02 Lesson 4, M05's Goodharting search |
| Contamination / Goodhart non-disclosure | "Did you check whether this number was optimized against, not just measured?" | M05's forensics — and M05 states directly that hiding a known finding here fails this module on principle |
| No falsification criterion | "What would make you reverse this recommendation?" | This module, Lesson 3 |

**Worked example — the pre-mortem applied to the one-line draft.** Lesson 1's one-liner ("kappa
0.84, ship it") gets three concrete objections from the table above: *aggregate hides subgroup* (the
report never mentions the subtle-hallucination items the judge misses by two full points), *gold set
provenance unstated* (no mention of the 20-item set's source, its two reconciled human raters, or
that it comes from AIE-204-M04 Lab M04-C specifically), and *no falsification criterion* (nothing in
the sentence could turn out to be false). A revised draft answers all three directly: it names the
subtle-hallucination subgroup's +2.00-point average divergence on 4 of 20 items, states the gold
set's provenance (20 items, two reconciled raters, AIE-204-M04 Lab M04-C), and closes with a real
condition — "if a fresh human-reconciled sample showed the gap widening, we would withdraw the ship
recommendation" — that could, in principle, come back false.

**Misconception 1 — "If I can't fully resolve an objection, the safest move is to leave it out."**
The reverse is true. Naming an objection you cannot fully answer, and stating precisely what would
resolve it, is *more* defensible than silence — because a skeptic who finds the gap you didn't
mention treats the entire report as untrustworthy, while a skeptic who finds the gap you already
named and bounded treats you as credible, including on the parts of the news that are bad. A report
that says "the judge is blind to plausible-sounding fabrication; we have not yet measured how often
this specific system produces it" has told the truth about an open question. A report that says
nothing has hidden one.

**Misconception 2 — "The pre-mortem is a step you do once, before writing the final draft."** It is
iterative, not one-shot, because closing one hole routinely opens a more specific one immediately
behind it. "We address the judge's blind spot" invites the next question on contact: *addressed how
— did you fix the rubric, or just write a paragraph about it?* A report that survives its first
pre-mortem needs a second pass asking exactly that question of its own new language, and a report
that has never had its second draft pre-mortemed has only been checked against the objections its
author thought of the first time.

### Lesson 3 — Confidence that means something, and a falsification clause that could actually fire

"Confident" is a vibe-word until it is tied to a method someone can check. This course has already
built two families of numbers that mean something specific: **agreement statistics** (M02's
unweighted kappa, M04's quadratic-weighted kappa, M06's taxonomy kappa — each with a Landis & Koch
band name) and **comparison statistics** (M03's confidence intervals and p-values, computed against
a pre-registered minimum detectable effect and significance level). A defensible confidence
statement names which family it's drawing from and states the actual number, not a synonym for it.

**Worked example — a prompt comparison, stated two ways.** A comparison of the summarizer's prompt
v1 against a candidate v2, n=40 per arm, pre-registered at alpha=0.05 with a minimum detectable
effect of 0.35 points and planned power 0.80, produced: mean faithfulness rose from 3.35 to 3.72, an
observed effect of 0.37, with a 95% confidence interval of **[0.04, 0.70]** and **p=0.028**.

- *Bad:* "We are highly confident v2 is better." No number. Nothing here could turn out false —
  which is precisely what makes it useless to a skeptic deciding whether to trust it.
- *Good:* "The observed 0.37-point improvement is significant at p=0.028, with a 95% CI of
  [0.04, 0.70] that excludes zero — but the interval is wide relative to the point estimate: the
  true improvement could be as small as 0.04, barely above the pre-registered 0.35-point floor, or
  nearly twice the observed value." A skeptic doing the same arithmetic on the same numbers reaches
  the same caveat the report already stated — which is the entire point. A report that gets there
  first, in its own words, is read as rigorous. A report a skeptic gets there *despite*, is read as
  either careless or evasive, and the two look identical from the outside.

**The falsification clause.** A stated condition, drawn from a method already validated somewhere in
the report, that would make the report's own author reverse the recommendation if it turned out to
be true. It is not "if new evidence emerges" — that resolves nothing and commits to nothing, because
literally any future fact satisfies it. A real one names the check and the threshold: *"if a fresh
20-item human-reconciled sample showed the subtle-hallucination gap widening past 2.00 points, or if
a position-bias re-check on v2's outputs found a flip rate above the 0.375 baseline measured on v1
(AIE-204-M04 Lab M04-B), we would withdraw the ship recommendation."* Both halves reuse a method the
report already trusts — a human-reconciled re-check, the position-bias flip-rate test — so the
clause is not inventing new machinery, it is committing to run machinery the report has already
proven works.

**Misconception 1 — "A significant p-value is itself a strong conclusion."** p=0.028 and a CI of
[0.04, 0.70] is a materially weaker claim than p=0.028 with a CI of [0.30, 0.44], even though both
are "significant" at alpha=0.05 — the first admits the true effect might be barely above the
pre-registered floor, the second doesn't. Reporting significance without the interval width lets a
skeptic do arithmetic you should have done for them, and finding it themselves reads as you having
missed it, hidden it, or hoped no one would check.

**Misconception 2 — "Stating a falsification criterion weakens the report by handing the skeptic an
attack."** An *unstated but implicit* criterion is the real attack surface — the skeptic will ask
"what would change your mind" whether or not the report answers it first, and an unprepared answer
in the room is worse than a prepared one on the page. A falsification clause built from methods the
report has already validated converts an open question into a bounded, already-answered one — it is
armor, not exposure.

"""Lab M07-A -- reference solution."""
from __future__ import annotations
import re

# Recorded before any check was written: Lesson 1's one-liner reads as
# confident precisely because it omits everything -- no method detail, no
# subgroup, no confidence number, no limitations, no falsification clause.
# It should fail every check the anatomy implies, and the single most
# obvious gap is that it states an aggregate (0.84) with zero acknowledgment
# that anything sits underneath it -- the exact failure M04 Lesson 4's
# Misconception 1 named directly.
PREDICTED_FLAWED_DRAFT_FAIL_COUNT = 8
PREDICTED_MOST_OBVIOUS_GAP = "aggregate_hides_subgroup"


def reports_confidence_interval(text: str) -> bool:
    has_ci_language = bool(re.search(r"\b(95%\s*)?(ci|confidence interval)\b", text, re.I))
    return has_ci_language and ("0.04" in text) and ("0.70" in text)


def names_the_judges_blind_spot(text: str) -> bool:
    has_term = bool(re.search(r"subtle[\s_-]?hallucination", text, re.I))
    has_magnitude = ("2" in text) or ("two" in text.lower())
    return has_term and has_magnitude


def reports_subgroup_not_just_aggregate(text: str) -> bool:
    has_aggregate = ("0.8394" in text) or ("0.84" in text)
    has_subgroup = bool(re.search(r"subtle[\s_-]?hallucination", text, re.I))
    return has_aggregate and has_subgroup


def states_gold_set_provenance(text: str) -> bool:
    return bool(re.search(r"reconciled", text, re.I)) \
        or bool(re.search(r"\bm04\b", text, re.I)) \
        or bool(re.search(r"n\s*=\s*20|20[\s-]item", text, re.I))


def names_taxonomy_top_contributor(text: str) -> bool:
    return ("wrong_entity_or_scope" in text.lower()) or ("wrong entity" in text.lower())


def has_limitations_section(text: str) -> bool:
    return bool(re.search(r"limitations?", text, re.I))


def has_falsification_clause(text: str) -> bool:
    return bool(re.search(r"would change|falsif", text, re.I))


def reports_gaming_resistance_check(text: str) -> bool:
    return bool(re.search(r"gaming|exploit|adversarial|position[\s-]?bias", text, re.I))


CHECKS = {
    "reports_confidence_interval": reports_confidence_interval,
    "names_the_judges_blind_spot": names_the_judges_blind_spot,
    "reports_subgroup_not_just_aggregate": reports_subgroup_not_just_aggregate,
    "states_gold_set_provenance": states_gold_set_provenance,
    "names_taxonomy_top_contributor": names_taxonomy_top_contributor,
    "has_limitations_section": has_limitations_section,
    "has_falsification_clause": has_falsification_clause,
    "reports_gaming_resistance_check": reports_gaming_resistance_check,
}


def audit_report(text):
    return {name: check(text) for name, check in CHECKS.items()}


def all_clear(audit_result):
    return all(audit_result.values())


def failing_checks(audit_result):
    return sorted(name for name, passed in audit_result.items() if not passed)

## Assessment

**Component:** the eval-report writing project (primary weight) and sealed-readings.
**Gaming-resistance is the design constraint:** a learner cannot pass by satisfying an automated
checklist alone — the checklist is necessary, not sufficient, and this rubric's heaviest rows are
graded on substance an automated check cannot fake.

### Module task — The Skeptic Sign-Off

Handed to the learner, unseen: a flawed draft eval report for a **different** system —
AIE-204-M02's original support-ticket urgency-triage classifier (`low` / `medium` / `high`) — plus
its underlying provided numbers: the classifier's gold-set agreement statistics, a subgroup
breakdown showing where the classifier's errors concentrate, a completed A/B comparison between two
candidate versions with its confidence interval, and one flagged, unresolved question about whether
the eval set used to select the shipped version overlapped with data the classifier's training run
had already seen (a live contamination question in AIE-204-M05's sense, not yet answered one way or
the other). The learner must, in order:

1. **Predict**, before running any audit tool: which of the six attack categories from Lesson 2's
   table the draft is vulnerable to, and how many of them — written down before touching the draft
   with a tool.
2. **Audit** the draft with their own Lab M07-A tool (adapted to the new system's specific numbers)
   and report exactly which checks fail and why, in the draft's own words.
3. **Revise** the report so it passes every check, grounded in the actual provided numbers — never
   substituting a plausible-sounding number for one that wasn't actually given.
4. **Resolve the contamination question explicitly, in writing** — either present evidence it did
   not occur, or disclose it as an open, unresolved risk in the Limitations section. Silently
   dropping the question is the one failure this task treats as disqualifying regardless of every
   other row's score, per AIE-204-M05's own stated principle.
5. **Write a real falsification clause** built from a method already validated somewhere in the
   revised report (not an invented future study), and a `SKEPTIC_SIGNOFF_NOTE` explaining, with
   specific numbers, why this version would get signed where the original wouldn't.

### Rubric

| Dimension | Full marks require | Why it resists gaming | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predict artifact | Attack-category count and specific categories, written and dated *before* any audit tool is run against the draft | A correct final audit with no prior prediction scores zero here — the forecast is what's graded, same as every Predict step in this course | 15 |
| Audit run and correctly diagnosed | Every genuinely missing element identified, with the draft's own language cited as the reason each check fails | A report claiming "the draft is fine" when the audit tool (built in Lab M07-A) demonstrably fails it is checked directly against the tool's output — there is no way to fake a passing audit result without actually revising the text | 20 |
| Revised report grounded in the real numbers | Every claim in the revision traces to a number actually provided, not an invented or rounded-favorably substitute | A revision that improves its audit score by inventing a plausible-sounding statistic instead of using the real one fails this row outright, independent of whether the audit tool itself would catch the substitution | 20 |
| Contamination question resolved, not dropped | The question is explicitly answered or explicitly disclosed as unresolved, in the Limitations section, in language a reader would actually notice | Silently omitting the question is a disqualifying failure per AIE-204-M05's own stated principle — a submission that scores well everywhere else but drops this question fails the module | 25 |
| Falsification clause and skeptic sign-off note | A real, checkable condition built from an already-validated method in the report, plus a signoff note that names specific numbers, not generic confidence language | "If new evidence emerges" or "we are confident this is defensible" score zero here — the clause and the note are graded on whether they could actually be checked or falsified, not on how persuasive they sound | 20 |

A submission that scores well on every row except the contamination-disclosure row fails the module
regardless of the other four scores — this is the one row in this course's assessments that is
explicitly non-compensatory, because a report that hides exactly the kind of gap AIE-204-M05 trains
a learner to find is not a report with a weak section. It is the failure this entire course exists
to prevent, committed by the learner it was building toward preventing it.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module — writing an eval report that survives a skeptical, adversarial read — not
boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** The eval report is not a hypothetical genre: model cards, incident
  postmortems, and internal ship/no-ship memos are the real documents this module's format is built
  from, and they are read by people with real, often adversarial, incentives — a competitor's
  analyst, a regulator, a board member defending a budget line. A report's actual audience is
  frequently someone the author never meets and cannot argue with in real time, which is exactly why
  the document has to answer the skeptic's questions before they're asked, not after.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** Published model cards and benchmark write-ups
  overwhelmingly report the aggregate favorable number and far more rarely disclose the specific
  subgroup breakdown, the confidence interval's width, or a real falsification criterion — the exact
  three elements this module's audit tool checks for, and the exact three elements this module's
  rubric treats as non-optional. The gap between "we reported evaluation results" and "we wrote a
  report a skeptic would sign" is invisible from the outside: both look like a document with numbers
  in it, and only one of them would survive someone trying to find the hole.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You have written the full report: judge validation with its named
  blind spot, taxonomy shortfall with its top contributor, a prompt comparison with a confidence
  interval wide enough to admit the true effect might barely clear the pre-registered floor, and a
  falsification clause that could actually fire. Leadership reads it and asks you to cut the
  Limitations section and the falsification clause before it goes to the board — "the numbers speak
  for themselves, and the caveats just create doubt where there doesn't need to be any." Nothing
  they're asking you to remove is false; the shortened version would still be a true document, just
  a less complete one, and the board would have no way to tell the difference between a shortened
  true report and a shortened one that got that way by omitting something the author knew and chose
  not to say. This is the moment this course has been building toward since M01: not a test with a
  right answer graded by a rubric, but the actual room, where the discipline you've spent seven
  modules earning either survives contact with an incentive to drop it, or it doesn't. What is the
  smallest cut you would agree to, what is the one you would not, and who finds out the difference
  between those two answers only after you've already made the call?

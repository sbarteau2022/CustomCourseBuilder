## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Rubric-based grading: turning "good summary" into a number that means something

A rubric is not "read it and give it a score." A rubric is a **set of independently-scored
criteria, each with written anchors for every point on the scale**, applied to output that a
generative system produced. Skip either half — independence of criteria, or anchors per point —
and the number you get back is closer to a mood than a measurement.

**The running task for this module.** A generative system reads a resolved support ticket (the
kind built and labeled in AIE-204-M02) and writes a **one-sentence resolution summary** for an
account-health dashboard read by managers who were never on the original ticket. They see only the
summary and, downstream, a score. Three criteria, each scored 1–5:

- **Faithfulness** — does the summary avoid claims not supported by the ticket?
- **Completeness** — does it cover what happened, the impact, and the resolution?
- **Concision** — is it free of padding, repetition, or irrelevant detail?

**Worked example.** Ticket T31: *"Checkout returned 500 errors for 20 minutes during a flash sale,
causing an estimated $40,000 in lost transactions before the on-call engineer rolled back the
deploy that introduced the bug."* Two candidate summaries:

> **Summary A:** "Checkout was down for 20 minutes during a flash sale, costing an estimated
> $40,000 in lost transactions; resolved by rolling back the deploy that caused it."
>
> **Summary B:** "Checkout was down for 20 minutes during a flash sale, costing an estimated
> $40,000 in lost transactions; the customer has threatened to cancel their contract over the
> outage."

Scored against written anchors (Faithfulness 5 = every claim directly supported, down to 1 =
contains a claim with no basis in the ticket; Completeness 5 = cause, impact, and resolution all
present; Concision 5 = no filler):

| Summary | Faithfulness | Completeness | Concision | **Average** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 5 | 5 | **5.00** |
| B | **1** | 4 | 5 | **3.33** |

Summary B invents a cancellation threat the ticket never mentions — a fabrication that, read by an
account manager who wasn't on the call, could trigger an unnecessary escalation. Scored honestly on
Faithfulness that's a 1. But averaged into a three-criterion score, B still lands at **3.33** —
comfortably above the scale's midpoint. A manager skimming an average score sees "acceptable." A
manager reading the Faithfulness column sees a hallucinated legal risk.

**Misconception 1 — "One overall score is enough; I'll just average the criteria."** An average is
a lossy compression that treats a catastrophic failure on one dimension the same as a middling
score spread evenly across three. The fix is procedural, not statistical: **report every criterion
separately**, and treat a low score on a safety-critical criterion (here, Faithfulness) as
disqualifying on its own, never something the other two can buy back. This is the direct
generalization of AIE-204-M02 Lesson 3's edge-case-averaging misconception, one level up — here the
"edge case" that gets averaged away lives inside a single item's own rubric, not across a dataset.

**Misconception 2 — "A 5-point scale is fine as long as I'm consistent."** Without written anchors
for every point, both human raters and LLM judges cluster on the same one or two values — usually
3 or 4 — regardless of what's actually in front of them. This is **scale compression**, and it is
not a rounding error: a judge that scores everything 4 has produced a rubric that measures nothing.
The anchors in the worked example above ("5 = every claim directly supported... 1 = contains a
claim with no basis in the ticket") exist specifically to give the judge — human or model — a
concrete reason to land anywhere other than the middle. An un-anchored prompt like "rate the
faithfulness 1–5" reproduces exactly this failure mode, and Lesson 3 shows precisely where the
anchors have to live for an automated judge to actually use them.

### Lesson 2 — Pairwise comparison, and the bias it resists that absolute rubrics don't

An absolute rubric asks "how good is this, on a fixed scale?" — which means every score is only as
stable as the judge's calibration to that scale at the moment it answers. **Pairwise comparison**
asks a narrower, easier question — "which of these two is better?" — and that narrower question is
resistant to a specific, well-documented failure: **severity drift**, where the same judge's sense
of what a "4" means shifts session to session, prompt to prompt, or even mid-conversation.

**Worked example — severity drift, made concrete.** The same two summaries from Lesson 1 (A and B,
unchanged) are re-scored on the absolute rubric on two different occasions:

| Occasion | Score(A) | Score(B) | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Session 1 | 4 | 3 | A is better |
| Session 2 (same two summaries) | 3 | 3 | **Tied** |

Nothing about A or B changed between sessions — only the judge's calibration to the 1–5 scale
drifted. The conclusion flipped from "A wins" to "no difference" without either summary changing a
word. A **pairwise** judge asked "which is better, A or B?" on both occasions has no absolute scale
to drift on — it only has to notice that A still doesn't have the fabricated claim, which is a
comparison, not a calibration. It says "A" both times.

**Pairwise resists severity drift. It does not resist everything — it introduces a bias absolute
scoring doesn't have: position bias.** A pairwise judge sees "Response A" and "Response B" in some
order, and language models are measurably prone to favoring whichever response occupies the first
slot, independent of content. Run the *same pair*, in *both orders*:

> Order 1 — A = Summary A, B = Summary B → judge says **"A is better."**
> Order 2 — A = Summary B, B = Summary A → judge says **"A is better."**

In order 2, "A" is now Summary B — the one with the fabricated claim — and the judge just said it
was better, having said the exact opposite claim's content was better one prompt ago. The judge
didn't change its mind about content; it defaulted to "whichever came first." The fix is
procedural, exactly like Lesson 1's anchors: **run every comparison in both orders and only trust a
preference that survives the swap.** A comparison whose preference flips when the order flips isn't
evidence about which response is better — it's evidence the margin was too thin for this judge to
resolve, and Lab M04-B is built to make you find exactly that boundary by hand.

**Worked example — why pairwise needs Bradley-Terry, not just a win tally.** Three summarizer
systems, A, B, and C, compared pairwise across many tickets. Win record:

| | beats B | beats C |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 8/10 | 5/10 |
| **B** (vs A: 2/10) | — | 8/10 |
| **C** (vs A: 5/10) | (vs B: 2/10) | — |

Read naively, A and C are a coin flip (5–5) — you'd call them roughly equal. But A dominates B
(8/10), and B dominates C (8/10): there is strong **indirect** evidence that A is much stronger than
C, and the direct 5–5 record is misleadingly close, likely because those two systems were rarely
compared on tickets where the gap would show. The **Bradley-Terry model** turns a win/loss record
into a single strength score per system by finding scores where the *probability* of beating
another system matches the observed win rate — and it uses indirect evidence like the A→B→C chain
to correct exactly this kind of misleading direct tie. Solved by the iterative algorithm Lab M04-B
implements: **A ≈ 1.43, B ≈ 0.94, C ≈ 0.62** — a real, non-trivial gap between A and C that the raw
5–5 record alone would never reveal.

**Misconception 1 — "Pairwise comparison removes bias, full stop."** It removes *severity drift*.
It does not remove — and can actively introduce — *position bias*, and a pairwise pipeline that
never swaps order and checks for flips is not more trustworthy than an absolute rubric, just wrong
in a different, less obvious way.

**Misconception 2 — "A win tally is basically the same as a score."** A raw win tally is only
informative when every system has played every other system a comparable number of times on
comparable items. The moment comparisons are sparse or uneven — the normal case once you have more
than two or three systems — a win tally can actively mislead, as the A-vs-C example shows. Bradley-
Terry (or an equivalent aggregation) is what turns "who beat whom, how often" into a comparable
score across systems that were never directly compared.

### Lesson 3 — Building an LLM-as-judge pipeline from scratch

An LLM-as-judge pipeline has exactly three parts, and each one is where a naive first attempt
breaks: the **prompt** (does it actually contain the rubric anchors, not just a vague instruction to
rate something), the **parser** (can it survive whatever formatting the model actually returns,
not just what you expect it to return), and the **backend** (is the specific model an
implementation detail, or is it load-bearing everywhere in your code).

**Worked example — the exact pipeline Lab M04-A builds, walked end to end.** The judge prompt
embeds the Lesson 1 anchors verbatim (not "rate faithfulness 1-5" — the whole anchor table) and asks
for a single JSON object back:

```
ITEM_ID: T31-B
You are grading a one-sentence resolution summary...
FAITHFULNESS (does the summary avoid claims not supported by the ticket?)
5 = every claim is directly supported by the ticket...
[... full anchor table from Lesson 1 ...]
TICKET: Checkout returned 500 errors for 20 minutes during a flash sale...
SUMMARY TO GRADE: ...the customer has threatened to cancel their contract...
Respond with ONLY a JSON object, no other text:
{"faithfulness": <1-5>, "completeness": <1-5>, "concision": <1-5>, "reasoning": "<one sentence>"}
```

A real model, asked this, does not reliably return bare JSON. A common real response looks like:

```
Faithfulness: 1, Completeness: 4, Concision: 5. The summary adds a cancellation
threat not in the ticket.
```

That's a normal, well-reasoned answer — and it is not valid JSON. A parser that calls `json.loads`
directly on it crashes; a parser that silently falls back to some default score has just turned a
caught fabrication into an invisible one. The correct move is a **bounded repair retry**: catch the
parse failure, re-send the same prompt with an appended instruction ("your previous response was
not valid JSON — respond with ONLY the JSON object"), and parse the second attempt. Lab M04-A's
`judge_summary` does exactly this, and its test suite proves the retry path is exercised, not just
present.

**Isolating the backend.** Every function above takes an `LLMClient` — a one-method interface
(`complete(prompt) -> str`) — never a vendor SDK. Two implementations exist: `FakeJudgeClient`,
deterministic, used by every test in this module and by the labs when run offline; and
`AnthropicClient`, a thin adapter around the real API, using `claude-haiku-4-5` — small, cheap, and
swappable, per this course's durability convention. `AnthropicClient` is the **only** file in this
module allowed to import `anthropic` or name a model string. When the model behind the judge
changes — and it will — this is the one file that changes, and Lesson 4 is what tells you whether
the swap silently broke the judge's agreement with humans.

**Re-ascend note.** Once your hand-rolled parser and retry logic pass, a production pipeline might
reach for the API's `output_config.format` (structured outputs, JSON-schema-enforced) instead of
free-text-then-parse. Building the fragile version first is what lets you actually understand what
that feature is buying you — a learner who reaches for it before building the fragile case has
skipped the lesson, same as reaching for `statsmodels.cohens_kappa` before deriving it by hand in
M02.

**Misconception 1 — "The judge should be the most capable model you can afford."** Capability and
*validated agreement with humans on this specific task* are different properties. A cheap model
that has passed Lesson 4's validation is more trustworthy than an expensive model that has never
been checked — "the biggest model" is a proxy for trust, not evidence of it.

**Misconception 2 — "If it looks like JSON, parsing is done."** A response wrapped in a markdown
code fence, or with one stray sentence before the JSON object, looks like JSON to a human skimming
it and breaks `json.loads`. Production judge pipelines need fence-stripping and a bounded retry, or
a formatting hiccup silently becomes a missing or wrong grade with no error raised anywhere.

**Misconception 3 — "An implicit rubric works fine inside an automated judge too."** Lesson 1
established that un-anchored scales compress toward the middle for human raters. The same failure
happens to an LLM judge for the same reason — nothing about automation exempts a judge from needing
the anchor table *in the prompt itself*, not just in a document you wrote for your own reference.

### Lesson 4 — Validating the judge: measured agreement, not asserted trust

A judge that is built and never checked against real human judgment is not a judge — it's an
unverified assumption wearing a rubric. **Validating the judge means treating it as a second
annotator and measuring agreement with a human-reconciled gold set**, using exactly M02's
inter-annotator-agreement methodology, with one addition this lesson exists to teach: ordinal
scores (1–5, not just category labels) need a kappa variant that gives **partial credit for being
close**, because being off by one point and being off by four points are not equally wrong.

**Weighted Cohen's kappa (quadratic weights).** Unweighted kappa (M02) treats every disagreement
identically — a judge that says 4 when the human said 5 counts the same as a judge that says 1. For
ordinal scales, **quadratic weighted kappa** penalizes disagreements in proportion to the *squared*
distance between them:

```
κ_w = 1 − (Σ w(i,j)·O(i,j)) / (Σ w(i,j)·E(i,j))

w(i,j) = (i − j)² / (N − 1)²      — quadratic weight, 0 for exact agreement
O(i,j) = observed count of (human=i, judge=j) pairs
E(i,j) = expected count under independence = (row total_i · col total_j) / n
```

**Worked example — by hand, ten items, ratings 1–5.** Human: `5,5,5,5,5,4,4,3,2,1`. Judge:
`5,5,5,5,4,4,3,3,2,1` — every disagreement here is off by exactly one point.

- Raw percent agreement `p_o` = 8/10 = **0.80**
- Unweighted kappa = **0.7222** — every one-point miss is punished as hard as a four-point miss would be
- Quadratic weighted kappa = **0.9465** — the same 0.80 raw agreement, but because every miss is a
  near-miss, the weighted number correctly reads as "almost perfect," not merely "substantial"

Same raw agreement, very different verdict — because unweighted kappa can't tell a one-point miss
from a catastrophic one, and on an ordinal scale that distinction is the whole point.

**The trap this creates, and why the confusion matrix is not optional.** Weighted kappa's partial
credit is exactly right when disagreements really are near-misses. It becomes a trap when a small
number of *large*, *systematic* misses get diluted by a majority of exact agreements — the aggregate
number can still read as "almost perfect" while concealing a real, specific failure. Lab M04-C's
20-item gold set produces exactly this: overall weighted kappa lands at **0.8394** — squarely in the
"almost perfect" band on the Landis & Koch scale — while four of the twenty items (summaries that
add a *plausible-sounding but unverified* technical claim) are scored a full **two points too high**
by the judge, every single time. The aggregate number is real. It is also not the whole story, and
a validation report that stops at the aggregate number has told a skeptic less than they think.

**Misconception 1 — "A high agreement number means the judge is safe to ship."** 0.84 is "almost
perfect" on the standard scale, and the judge behind that number is *systematically* blind to
exactly the failure mode — plausible-sounding fabrication — the rubric exists to catch. Report the
aggregate and the per-category breakdown together, or the aggregate is a number that looks like
evidence and isn't.

**Misconception 2 — "The judge's fluent reasoning text is evidence it reasoned correctly."** On the
four items where the judge over-scores, its written reasoning sounds just as confident and specific
as on the items it gets right — because the fabricated claims in those four items are *designed to
sound plausible*, and a language model's fluency is a property of generation, not of verification.
A human re-checking the ticket can confirm a claim is unsupported; a judge with no way to check
anything outside the text it was already given cannot tell "plausible" from "true."

**Misconception 3 — "Validate once and the judge stays validated."** Every prompt edit, rubric
wording change, and model version bump changes the judge's error pattern — that's the entire reason
this module isolates model specifics behind `llm_client.py` (Lesson 3): a model swap is a one-line
change in one file, and it obligates a full re-run of this lesson's validation, not just a re-run of
the eval the judge was built to support.

`llm_client.py` (provided; shared by every lab):

```python
"""
llm_client.py — the thin interface every judge in this module is built on.

Durability discipline: every lesson and lab imports LLMClient (and, for
tests, FakeJudgeClient) from this file -- never a vendor SDK directly. When
the cheap-and-swappable current model changes, only AnthropicClient below
needs to change. The judge-building logic in Lessons 3-4 and Labs A-C does
not know or care which model answers complete().
"""
from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Protocol


class LLMClient(Protocol):
    """Anything with this one method can serve as a judge backend."""

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        """Return the raw text completion for `prompt`. Implementations own
        all vendor-specific request/response translation; callers never see
        it."""
        ...


@dataclass
class FakeJudgeClient:
    """Deterministic stand-in used by every test in this module, and by the
    labs when run offline. Maps an exact substring of the prompt to a
    canned response, so the judge's PARSING and AGGREGATION logic can be
    built and graded without a network call or an API key.

    When more than one canned key matches a prompt, the LONGEST (most
    specific) key wins. This is deliberate: a repair/retry prompt is built
    by *appending* a marker to the original prompt, so it still contains
    the original prompt's marker as a substring -- the longer, more
    specific marker must win, or a retry would just replay the original
    malformed response forever.
    """
    canned: dict[str, str] = field(default_factory=dict)

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        matches = [(key, response) for key, response in self.canned.items() if key in prompt]
        if not matches:
            raise KeyError(
                "FakeJudgeClient: no canned response matches this prompt. "
                "This almost always means your build_*_prompt() function "
                "doesn't include the exact ID marker the test data expects "
                "-- check it against the lab's prompt contract."
            )
        _, response = max(matches, key=lambda pair: len(pair[0]))
        return response


class AnthropicClient:
    """The real backend. This is the ONLY file in this module that imports
    a vendor SDK or names a specific model -- that is the point of the
    thin interface above. Swap the model string here and nothing in
    Lessons 3-4 or Labs A-C changes.

    Model choice: claude-haiku-4-5 -- small, cheap, and (per Lesson 4)
    never trusted just because it's convenient. It earns trust only after
    Lab M04-C's validation passes, the same way any judge backend would.
    """

    DEFAULT_MODEL = "claude-haiku-4-5"

    def __init__(self, model: str = DEFAULT_MODEL, api_key: str | None = None):
        import anthropic  # imported here, not at module scope, so labs that
        # never instantiate AnthropicClient don't require the package installed
        self._client = anthropic.Anthropic(api_key=api_key)
        self._model = model

    def complete(self, prompt: str, *, temperature: float = 0.0, max_tokens: int = 512) -> str:
        response = self._client.messages.create(
            model=self._model,
            max_tokens=max_tokens,
            temperature=temperature,
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
        )
        return response.content[0].text
```

def build_pairwise_prompt(comparison_id, ticket_text, response_a, response_b):
    return f"""COMPARISON_ID: {comparison_id}
You are comparing two resolution summaries for the same ticket. Judge only
which one is a better dashboard summary.

TICKET:
{ticket_text}

Response A: {response_a}

Response B: {response_b}

Respond with ONLY a JSON object, no other text:
{{"winner": "A" | "B" | "tie", "reasoning": "<one sentence>"}}"""


def parse_pairwise_response(raw: str) -> dict:
    text = raw.strip()
    if text.startswith("```"):
        text = re.sub(r"^```(?:json)?\s*", "", text)
        text = re.sub(r"\s*```$", "", text)
    try:
        data = json.loads(text)
    except json.JSONDecodeError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f"pairwise response was not valid JSON: {raw!r}") from exc
    if data.get("winner") not in ("A", "B", "tie"):
        raise ValueError(f"winner must be 'A', 'B', or 'tie', got {data!r}")
    if "reasoning" not in data:
        raise ValueError(f"missing reasoning: {data!r}")

    prompt = build_pairwise_prompt(comparison_id, ticket_text, response_a, response_b)
    raw = client.complete(prompt, temperature=0.0)
    return parse_pairwise_response(raw)


def run_order_swapped(client, item_id, ticket_text, text_x, text_y):
    order1 = pairwise_compare(client, f"{item_id}:A=X", ticket_text, text_x, text_y)
    winner1 = {"A": "X", "B": "Y", "tie": "tie"}[order1["winner"]]

    order2 = pairwise_compare(client, f"{item_id}:A=Y", ticket_text, text_y, text_x)
    winner2 = {"A": "Y", "B": "X", "tie": "tie"}[order2["winner"]]

    return {
        "order1_winner": winner1,
        "order2_winner": winner2,
        "flipped": winner1 != winner2,
    }


def bradley_terry(win_counts, players, iters=200):
    scores = {p: 1.0 for p in players}
    for _ in range(iters):
        new_scores = {}
        for p in players:
            numerator = sum(win_counts.get(p, {}).get(q, 0) for q in players if q != p)
            denom = 0.0
            for q in players:
                if q == p:
                    continue
                games = win_counts.get(p, {}).get(q, 0) + win_counts.get(q, {}).get(p, 0)
                if games == 0:
                    continue
                denom += games / (scores[p] + scores[q])
            new_scores[p] = numerator / denom if denom > 0 else scores[p]
        norm = sum(new_scores.values()) / len(new_scores)
        scores = {p: v / norm for p, v in new_scores.items()}
    return scores

def build_faithfulness_prompt(item_id, ticket_text, summary_text):
    return f"""ITEM_ID: {item_id}
Score how FAITHFUL this summary is to the ticket -- does it avoid claims not
supported by the ticket text? Use this scale:
5 = every claim is directly supported; no inference beyond what's stated.
4 = all claims supported; one very minor stylistic embellishment only.
3 = one claim is a reasonable, low-stakes inference not explicitly stated.
2 = one claim is unsupported and could mislead about scope, cause, or impact.
1 = contains a fabricated claim with no basis in the ticket.

TICKET:
{ticket_text}

SUMMARY TO SCORE:
{summary_text}

Respond with ONLY a JSON object: {{"faithfulness": <1-5>, "reasoning": "<one sentence>"}}"""


def parse_faithfulness_response(raw: str) -> int:
    text = raw.strip()
    if text.startswith("```"):
        text = re.sub(r"^```(?:json)?\s*", "", text)
        text = re.sub(r"\s*```$", "", text)
    try:
        data = json.loads(text)
    except json.JSONDecodeError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f"faithfulness response was not valid JSON: {raw!r}") from exc
    score = data.get("faithfulness")
    if not isinstance(score, int) or not (1 <= score <= 5):
        raise ValueError(f"faithfulness must be an int 1-5, got {score!r}")
    return score


def judge_faithfulness(client, item_id, ticket_text, summary_text):
    prompt = build_faithfulness_prompt(item_id, ticket_text, summary_text)
    raw = client.complete(prompt, temperature=0.0)
    return parse_faithfulness_response(raw)


def run_judge_over_items(client, items):
    return [judge_faithfulness(client, it["id"], it["ticket_text"], it["summary_text"]) for it in items]


def quadratic_weighted_kappa(human, judge, labels=LABELS):
    n = len(human)
    assert n == len(judge) and n > 0
    N = len(labels)
    idx = {l: i for i, l in enumerate(labels)}
    O = [[0] * N for _ in range(N)]
    for h, j in zip(human, judge):
        O[idx[h]][idx[j]] += 1
    W = [[((i - j) ** 2) / ((N - 1) ** 2) for j in range(N)] for i in range(N)]
    hist_h = [0] * N
    hist_j = [0] * N
    for h in human:
        hist_h[idx[h]] += 1
    for j in judge:
        hist_j[idx[j]] += 1
    E = [[hist_h[i] * hist_j[j] / n for j in range(N)] for i in range(N)]
    num = sum(W[i][j] * O[i][j] for i in range(N) for j in range(N))
    den = sum(W[i][j] * E[i][j] for i in range(N) for j in range(N))
    if den == 0:
        return 1.0
    return 1 - num / den


def confusion_matrix(human, judge, labels=LABELS):
    idx = {l: i for i, l in enumerate(labels)}
    N = len(labels)
    M = [[0] * N for _ in range(N)]
    for h, j in zip(human, judge):
        M[idx[h]][idx[j]] += 1
    return M


def mean_signed_diff(human, judge):
    diffs = [j - h for h, j in zip(human, judge)]
    return sum(diffs) / len(diffs)


def divergence_report(items, human_scores, judge_scores):
    by_cat = defaultdict(list)
    for item, h, j in zip(items, human_scores, judge_scores):
        by_cat[item["category"]].append(j - h)
    per_category = {
        cat: {"mean_diff": sum(diffs) / len(diffs), "n": len(diffs)}
        for cat, diffs in by_cat.items()
    }
    worst_category = max(per_category, key=lambda c: abs(per_category[c]["mean_diff"]))
    kappa = quadratic_weighted_kappa(human_scores, judge_scores, LABELS)
    return {
        "overall_qwk": kappa,
        "per_category": per_category,
        "worst_category": worst_category,
        "worst_category_mean_diff": per_category[worst_category]["mean_diff"],
        "aggregate_looks_safe_but_isnt": (
            kappa >= 0.81 and abs(per_category[worst_category]["mean_diff"]) >= 1.5
        ),
    }

## Assessment

**Component:** judge-validation project (primary weight) and sealed-readings.
**Gaming-resistance is the design constraint:** a learner cannot pass without demonstrating the
outcome.

### Module task — the Judge Validation Audit

Handed to the learner, unseen: a small human-labeled set (~15 items, two reconciled human raters)
for a **different** generative task — grading the *helpfulness* of an AI-generated troubleshooting
reply, 1–5 — plus a working but never-validated LLM-judge pipeline built on this module's own
`LLMClient` interface. The learner must, in order:

1. **Predict**, before computing anything: which agreement band the aggregate weighted kappa will
   land in, and, having read a 5-item sample, which failure category is most likely to show
   systematic judge–human divergence and in which direction.
2. **Compute** quadratic weighted kappa and a confusion matrix between judge and human labels using
   their own hand-rolled functions (no library calls).
3. **Break the aggregate down by category**, identify the specific divergence pattern — direction,
   magnitude, and the mechanism behind it — and write it up in one paragraph.
4. **Decide, in writing**, whether the judge is safe to ship as-is, needs a prompt fix, or needs a
   different rubric criterion entirely — and state exactly what additional evidence would change
   that decision.
5. **Check for position bias**: run the pairwise variant of the same judge on 5 order-swapped pairs
   drawn from the same set and report the flip rate.

### Rubric

| Dimension | Full marks require | Why it resists gaming | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predict artifact | Band, worst-category guess, and direction, all written and dated *before* any code runs | A correct final report with no prior prediction scores zero here — the forecast is what's graded, same as every Predict step in this course | 20 |
| Statistics computed correctly | Hand-rolled weighted kappa and confusion matrix, matching an independently computed reference within tolerance | A copied library call is disallowed by the assignment; a wrong-but-confident hand implementation is caught by the reference-match check | 20 |
| Subgroup divergence identified with mechanism | Correct worst category, correct direction and magnitude, and a written explanation of *why* — not just the number | A report citing only the aggregate kappa fails this row outright, regardless of how high that number is — this is the module's central lesson, graded directly | 25 |
| Ship / fix / redesign decision, justified | A stated decision, a reason tied to the actual divergence found, and a concrete statement of what evidence would change the decision | "Looks good" or "needs work" with no tie to the specific failure found scores zero — the decision must follow from row 3's evidence, not from vibes | 20 |
| Position-bias check | Order-swapped pairwise run on 5 pairs, flip rate reported, and a one-sentence read on what the flip rate implies about that judge's reliability on close calls | A learner who never swaps the order cannot produce this row at all — there's no way to fake a flip rate without actually running both orders | 15 |

A submission that scores well on rows 2–5 but has no dated Predict artifact preceding them fails the
module regardless of the final numbers — the judgment trail is the credential, not the program that
happens to run.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module — building and validating an LLM judge — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** LLM-as-judge methodology is now used to gate production releases
  and to produce leaderboard rankings that drive real deployment decisions. Published research on
  these pipelines has documented specific, reproducible judge failure modes — position bias
  (preferring whichever response is shown first, independent of content), verbosity bias
  (preferring longer answers independent of quality), and self-preference bias (a model favoring
  outputs from its own model family). Lab M04-B's flip-rate exercise is a hand-built instance of
  the first of these, not a hypothetical.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** A reported "our judge agrees with human raters 85% of the
  time" is, in practice, frequently the *only* number a vendor or paper publishes. The confusion
  matrix and the subgroup breakdown — exactly what Lab M04-C's whole payoff depends on computing —
  are almost never disclosed alongside the headline agreement figure. Without them, a reader cannot
  tell whether that remaining 15% of disagreement is spread randomly across the eval set or
  concentrated precisely on the highest-stakes failure category, which is the only version of that
  question that actually matters for a shipping decision.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your team's judge validates at 0.84 weighted kappa against a
  human-reconciled gold set — "almost perfect" on the standard scale. The confusion matrix shows it
  is specifically, systematically blind to plausible-sounding fabrications: the exact failure mode
  the rubric was built to catch. Leadership wants to ship on the strength of the headline number,
  which is real and not fabricated. What is the smallest, most honest change you could make to the
  validation report that would put the actual decision in front of the people making it — and are
  you the one who insists it gets made, knowing the number that ships without it is not a lie, just
  an incomplete truth someone else will read as a complete one?

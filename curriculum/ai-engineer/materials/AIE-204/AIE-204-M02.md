## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — What a gold set actually is (and what one person's opinion is not)

A "gold set" is not a folder of examples someone labeled. It is three things bundled together,
and a submission missing any one of them is not a gold set yet:

1. **Written labeling criteria** — a rule a second person could apply without asking you what you
   meant.
2. **Measured agreement** — evidence that a second person applying that rule reaches the same
   labels often enough that the labels mean something (Lesson 2).
3. **Deliberately included edge cases** — examples chosen because they are hard, not because they
   were next in a random sample (Lesson 3).

This lesson is about the first piece. Take a real, ongoing task: **support-ticket urgency
triage** — every incoming ticket gets routed as `low`, `medium`, or `high` before an on-call
engineer ever reads it. This is the running task for the rest of the module.

**Worked example — the criteria doc.** A first-draft rule like "high = urgent" is not a criterion,
it's a synonym. Here is the version that survives contact with real tickets:

- **`high`** — service or a widely used workflow is down for multiple users; a confirmed security
  incident or data exposure; data loss with no available backup or workaround; a
  revenue-blocking issue in production; a compliance or legal deadline at risk.
- **`medium`** — functionality is broken or degraded for the reporting user or team, but a
  workaround exists; incorrect data affecting a single account; a recurring error blocking one
  specific, non-critical workflow.
- **`low`** — cosmetic or copy issues; documentation gaps; feature requests; "how do I" questions;
  positive feedback or reports that turn out not to be a problem at all.
- **The tie-breaking rule, stated explicitly:** classify by *impact and scope*, never by the
  words the reporter used. "URGENT" in a subject line is not evidence.

Two tickets show why the explicit rule matters:

> **T01:** "Our production checkout page has been returning 500 errors for the last 20 minutes;
> no one can complete a purchase." → `high`. Unambiguous: production, revenue-blocking, affects
> everyone.
>
> **T07:** "URGENT!!! Please rename my display name from 'Jon' to 'John'." → `low`. The
> punctuation says emergency; the impact-and-scope rule says a cosmetic, zero-functional-effect
> request. Without the explicit tie-breaker, two reasonable people label this differently —
> that's not a hypothetical, it's what Lab M02-A's second annotator actually does with it.

**Misconception 1 — "I labeled it myself, so it's ground truth."** A single annotator's labels are
one person's judgment, encoded. They inherit that person's blind spots and have no way to reveal
them. Ground truth requires a second, independent application of the same criteria — which is
exactly what has no meaning until you measure whether it agrees (Lesson 2).

**Misconception 2 — "If the criteria are written down, agreement is guaranteed."** Written
criteria reduce disagreement; they do not eliminate it. T07 and T01 both cleanly resolve to a
single label under the criteria doc above. Not every ticket will — and a gold set that pretends
otherwise is measuring the criteria doc's clarity, not the labelers' agreement, until someone
actually checks.

### Lesson 2 — Inter-annotator agreement: measured, not assumed

Raw percent agreement lies to you when the classes are imbalanced, because two labelers can agree
often just by both leaning toward the common class. **Cohen's kappa** corrects for that by
subtracting out the agreement you'd expect from chance alone, given each labeler's own marginal
distribution:

```
κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)

p_o = observed agreement = (# items both raters gave the same label) / N
p_e = expected agreement by chance = Σ_label  P(rater A → label) · P(rater B → label)
```

**Worked example — by hand, on paper first.** Twenty items, two raters, three labels
(`high`/`medium`/`low`). Confusion matrix (rows = rater A, columns = rater B):

| A ＼ B | high | medium | low | row total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **high** | 3 | 1 | 0 | 4 |
| **medium** | 1 | 10 | 1 | 12 |
| **low** | 0 | 1 | 3 | 4 |
| **col total** | 4 | 12 | 4 | 20 |

- `p_o` = (3 + 10 + 3) / 20 = **0.80**
- `p_e` = (4/20 · 4/20) + (12/20 · 12/20) + (4/20 · 4/20) = 0.04 + 0.36 + 0.04 = **0.44**
- `κ` = (0.80 − 0.44) / (1 − 0.44) = 0.36 / 0.56 = **0.6429**

On the standard Landis & Koch interpretation scale (0–0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60
moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect), 0.6429 is **substantial** — good, real
agreement, and nowhere near perfect. That is the realistic target for this kind of task, not a
failure to fix.

**Re-ascend check (optional, not graded).** Once you can produce 0.6429 by hand and by your own
code, confirm it against the field's high-level tool:

```python
from statsmodels.stats.inter_rater import cohens_kappa
import numpy as np

table = np.array([[3, 1, 0],
                   [1, 10, 1],
                   [0, 1, 3]])   # rows/cols ordered high, medium, low
print(cohens_kappa(table).kappa)   # ≈ 0.6429
```

If you reach for this before you can reproduce the number by hand, you've skipped the lesson —
you now have a library that agrees with a calculation you can't yet defend.

**Misconception 1 — "90% raw agreement means the labels are reliable."** On a skewed label set,
two labelers who both default to the majority class agree 90% of the time and mean nothing by it.
Always report `p_e` next to `p_o`, or the 90% is unfalsifiable.

**Misconception 2 — "A well-instructed task should get κ near 1.0."** Some ambiguity is inherent
to the task, not a failure of instructions — T07's kind of edge case will never fully disappear no
matter how the criteria doc is worded. A realistic, healthy target for a task with genuine judgment
calls is **substantial** agreement (0.6–0.8), not perfect. A reported κ of 0.95+ on a task with
real edge cases is itself a signal worth checking — see Lesson 3, and the Tier 3 reading.

### Lesson 3 — Edge cases, deliberately included

A gold set built only from a random sample of tickets over-represents the easy, unambiguous
majority and under-represents the rare cases that actually determine whether a system is safe to
ship. Rare-but-critical cases have to be **curated on purpose** — and because deliberate curation
can just as easily encode the curator's own blind spots, every deliberately included edge case
needs a written reason it was chosen, not just a `is_edge_case` flag.

**Worked example — six edge cases from the ticket set, and what each one tests:**

| Ticket | Why it was deliberately included |
|---|---|
| T07 | Urgency *language* with zero urgency *impact* — tests whether the label follows the criteria doc's tie-breaker or the reporter's punctuation. |
| T08 | Ambiguous scope ("several" techs) — tests whether "affects multiple users" gets applied conservatively or requires an explicit headcount the ticket doesn't give. |
| T11 | Financial and trust-sensitive, but single-account and remediable — tests whether "feels serious" gets separated from "matches the `high` criteria." |
| T12 | The ticket doesn't say what the dashboard is used for — tests whether missing context gets resolved by guessing or by flagging for escalation. |
| T16 | The reporter's own time pressure ("I need to run payroll today") is doing the persuading, not the system impact — tests whether a labeler imports the reporter's stress into the priority. |
| T21 | Not a problem at all. Tests whether the labeling *process* — and eventually a downstream classifier — can recognize the absence of an issue, which fails silently if it's never in the gold set to test against. |

**Misconception 1 — "A large enough random sample covers the edge cases eventually."** It covers
them in proportion to how often they occur in traffic — which is exactly wrong for the cases that
matter most, because the highest-consequence tickets (a real security incident, a real
compliance deadline) are also the rarest. Random sampling systematically starves your gold set of
the examples where being wrong costs the most.

**Misconception 2 — "Edge cases are rare, so a metric averaged over the whole gold set is fine."**
An aggregate metric can look excellent while performing badly on exactly the slice you built the
gold set to protect. Report metrics on the edge-case slice separately from the core slice — a
system that scores 95% overall and 40% on the six edge cases above is not ready, and an aggregate
number alone will never tell you that.

### Lesson 4 — Metrics that resist gaming: anatomy of an exploit

A metric is **gameable** with respect to a task if there exists some output — or some predictor —
that scores at or near the metric's ceiling while doing approximately nothing of the actual task.
Three canonical patterns recur across almost every domain:

1. **Single-class recall, gamed by over-predicting that class.** If the metric is "did we catch
   every true positive," predicting positive for *everything* maximizes it by construction — the
   alarm-fatigue pattern, familiar from intrusion detection and content moderation.
2. **Coverage or overlap metrics, gamed by copying the source.** If the metric rewards containing
   the reference content (classic unigram-recall ROUGE for summarization), outputting the entire
   source document as the "summary" maximizes it while summarizing nothing.
3. **Accuracy under class imbalance, gamed by the majority-class constant predictor.** If 80% of
   examples are one class, a predictor that always outputs that class scores 80% accuracy having
   looked at zero inputs.

The fix in all three cases has the same shape: **pair the metric with its natural adversary.**
Recall pairs with precision. Coverage pairs with a length or cost penalty. Accuracy pairs with a
per-class balance term. A metric that only measures one side of that pair is not yet safe to
optimize against.

**Worked example — a small one, so Lab M02-B is still yours to solve.** A content-moderation
queue: 6 posts, 2 of which truly need a human review flag. The naive metric is "recall on
`should_flag = True`." A moderator who flags *every single post* achieves:

- `TP = 2, FP = 4, FN = 0` → recall = 2/2 = **1.0** — perfect, by definition, and worthless.
- precision = 2/6 = **0.333**
- F1 = 2·(0.333·1.0)/(0.333+1.0) = **0.5** — punished, because F1 is forced to answer for the 4
  false alarms recall was blind to.

A moderator who reads the posts and flags only the 2 that actually need review scores recall =
1.0, precision = 1.0, F1 = 1.0 — the same perfect recall as the exploit, but earned instead of
gamed. Recall alone cannot tell these two moderators apart. F1 can.

**Misconception 1 — "A high score always means the system is good."** A high score means the
system scores well on *this metric*. Whether that metric captures the actual task is a separate
question you have to answer before you trust the number — this is the entire subject of this
module.

**Misconception 2 — "If I can't think of an exploit, the metric is safe."** Not finding the
exploit in five minutes is evidence about you, not about the metric. AIE-204-M05 covers this at
industrial scale, where the term is **Goodharting**: once a metric becomes a target, it stops
being a good measure, because the search for its exploits doesn't stop with you.

**Misconception 3 — "Fixing a metric once means it's fixed forever."** F1 defeats "flag
everything." It does not defeat every future exploit — a sufficiently motivated adversary who
knows you moved from recall to F1 will look for what F1 still misses (Lab M02-C proves the current
fix works against the *current* exploit; it is not a promise about the next one).

```python
def cohens_kappa(rater_a, rater_b, labels=LABELS) -> float:
    n = len(rater_a)
    assert n == len(rater_b) and n > 0
    p_o = sum(1 for a, b in zip(rater_a, rater_b) if a == b) / n
    count_a, count_b = Counter(rater_a), Counter(rater_b)
    p_e = sum((count_a[l] / n) * (count_b[l] / n) for l in labels)
    if p_e == 1.0:
        return 1.0
    return (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
```

```python
"""Lab M02-C — reference solution."""
from gold_set_50 import GOLD_SET
from lab_b_metric import recall_for_class, flag_everything

Y_TRUE = [g[2] for g in GOLD_SET]

HIGH_KEYWORDS = ["down", "breach", "unauthenticat", "no backup", "audit", "immediately",
                 "100%", "entire app", "every account", "every customer",
                 "enterprise customer list", "broken after", "blocking their launch",
                 "refusing all connections", "500s for every"]
LOW_KEYWORDS = ["would be great", "someday", "just wanted to say", "just checking",
                "roadmap", "thanks", "demo account", "typo", "footer", "favicon",
                "changelog", "shortcut", "less aggressive", "personalize", "loving the"]


def rule_classify(text: str) -> str:
    t = text.lower()
    if any(k in t for k in HIGH_KEYWORDS):
        return "high"
    if any(k in t for k in LOW_KEYWORDS):
        return "low"
    return "medium"


RULE_BASED_PREDICTIONS = [rule_classify(text) for _, text, _ in GOLD_SET]

# precision_high for the exploit is 8/50 = 0.16 by hand; with recall=1.0,
# F1 = 2*(0.16*1.0)/(0.16+1.0) ≈ 0.276 — low, but not zero, because F1 is a
# harmonic mean, not an AND gate.
PREDICTED_F1_HIGH_FOR_EXPLOIT = 0.28
PREDICTED_F1_HIGH_FOR_RULE_BASED = 0.80


def precision_for_class(y_true, y_pred, label):
    tp = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if p == label and t == label)
    fp = sum(1 for t, p in zip(y_true, y_pred) if p == label and t != label)
    return tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) else 0.0


def f1_for_class(y_true, y_pred, label):
    p = precision_for_class(y_true, y_pred, label)
    r = recall_for_class(y_true, y_pred, label)
    return (2 * p * r / (p + r)) if (p + r) else 0.0


def macro_f1(y_true, y_pred, labels=("low", "medium", "high")):
    return sum(f1_for_class(y_true, y_pred, l) for l in labels) / len(labels)
```

### Module task — the Gold Set & Metric Audit

3. **Fix** the metric (propose and implement a per-class or macro measure) and **prove** two

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module — hand-built gold sets and the metrics measured against them — not
boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** The single most common quietly-omitted baseline in published eval
  tables is the constant/majority-class predictor: if a result reports "94% accuracy" on an
  imbalanced benchmark without stating what a constant predictor would score, the number cannot be
  read as evidence of capability — this is a mechanical fact about the arithmetic of imbalanced
  accuracy (Lesson 4), not a matter of interpretation.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** Every "high-quality human-labeled gold set" claim
  implicitly asserts an inter-annotator agreement figure — and most public benchmark papers report
  none at all, or report only raw percent agreement, which Lesson 2 showed can look reassuring on
  a skewed label set even when the chance-corrected number is close to zero. The distance between
  "we had humans label this" and "we measured whether the humans agreed" is exactly where a
  benchmark's authority gets borrowed without being earned.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You build a gold set for your team's system. On the first pass, your
  own κ against the second labeler you recruited comes back at 0.31 — fair agreement, not
  substantial. You have real options: report it and let the release slow down; recruit a different
  second labeler and don't mention the first attempt; or quietly relabel the disagreements
  yourself until the number looks better. Every one of those has actually been chosen, by someone,
  at a real company, under real deadline pressure. Which one do you take — and what, specifically,
  do you write in the eval report about how you arrived at the number you shipped with?

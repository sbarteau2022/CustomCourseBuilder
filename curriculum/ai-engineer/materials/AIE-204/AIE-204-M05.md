## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — What contamination actually is (and why "we deduplicated" isn't proof it didn't happen)

**Contamination** means the system being measured has, directly or indirectly, seen the eval
item — or something close enough to it — during training. A contaminated eval doesn't measure
capability; it measures memory, and the two produce the same score on the item that's contaminated,
which is exactly why contamination is dangerous: nothing about a high score, on its own, tells you
which one you're looking at.

There are two shapes of contamination, and they require two different detection techniques:

1. **Exact-duplicate contamination.** The eval item — or a near-verbatim scrape of it — appears
   unchanged somewhere in the training data: a trivia site's answer key, a study-flashcard export, a
   forum post quoting the source. **Canonicalize both texts (lowercase, strip punctuation, collapse
   whitespace) and check for a substring match.** This catches copies, reformatting, and
   capitalization drift — nothing more.
2. **Near-duplicate / paraphrase contamination.** The same fact, reworded — a synonym swapped in, a
   clause reordered, translated and translated back. Exact match **cannot catch this by
   construction**: the canonicalized strings are genuinely different. Catching it requires comparing
   **overlapping chunks of the text ("shingles")** rather than the whole string at once.

**Worked example — the technique, on a sentence short enough to check by hand.** Take the sentence
`A = "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog"` (9 words). Its 4-word shingles (every four
consecutive words, canonicalized) are:

```
{('the','quick','brown','fox'), ('quick','brown','fox','jumps'), ('brown','fox','jumps','over'),
 ('fox','jumps','over','the'), ('jumps','over','the','lazy'), ('over','the','lazy','dog')}
```

Six shingles. Now compare two corpus documents against it:

> `B` (verbatim scrape): *"Sunday puzzle answer key: the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.
> See page six for next week's puzzle."*
> `C` (paraphrased): *"the quick brown fox **leaps** over the lazy dog"*

Canonicalized, `A` is a literal substring of `B` — **exact match: true.** `A` is **not** a substring
of `C` — one word changed, so the strings differ — **exact match: false**, even though a human
reading `C` recognizes it as the same fact.

**Containment** — the fraction of the *smaller* text's shingles that also appear in the other
text — tells the rest of the story:

```
containment(A, B) = |shingles(A) ∩ shingles(B)| / min(|shingles(A)|, |shingles(B)|)
```

`containment(A, B) = 6/6 = 1.00` (every one of A's shingles survives inside B — a wrapped verbatim
copy). `containment(A, C) = 2/6 = 0.33` (only the two shingles that don't touch the changed word
"leaps" survive — `jumps` → `leaps` breaks every 4-word window it appears in, but the untouched tail
of the sentence still overlaps). Exact match is blind to `C`. Containment is not.

**Misconception 1 — "We deduplicated the training set against the eval set, so contamination is
impossible."** Deduplication run as an exact-match pass — the overwhelming majority of real
pipelines, because it's cheap — only removes what containment's example `B` represents. It has
**zero power** against `C`'s shape of leakage: a forum post, a translated re-post, a flashcard deck,
anything that reworded the source even slightly. "We deduplicated" is a true statement about one
detector's coverage, not a proof about contamination in general — and reported as if it were the
latter, it's the same move as AIE-204-M02 Lesson 1's "I labeled it myself, so it's ground truth":
one check, presented as if it settled the question no single check can settle alone.

**Misconception 2 — "Any shared text between the eval set and the corpus is proof of leakage."** The
opposite failure is just as real. Two independent sources routinely reuse the same standard,
formulaic phrasing — an encyclopedic description of a mountain's height, a textbook's stock
definition of photosynthesis — without either one having copied the other. Lesson 2's own dataset
contains a deliberately engineered example of exactly this: a near-miss overlap that a naive
"any shared shingle counts" rule would flag, and a properly chosen threshold correctly does not.
Contamination forensics needs a **threshold**, chosen and justified, not a boolean "any overlap
exists."

### Lesson 2 — Finding it yourself: the forensics workflow, worked end to end

This lesson runs the Lesson 1 technique across a full held-out eval set — the same 20-item set and
17-document corpus Lab M05-A hands you, unlabeled, with no hint about which items are compromised.
The set: 20 short trivia passages, each with a question and answer, alongside a report that a small
system scored **70% overall (14/20)** on it — a number that, read on its own, looks like a
respectable pass.

**Pass 1 — exact match only.** Canonicalize every eval passage and every one of the 17 corpus
documents; check substring containment. Five items — call them the `V`-series — turn out to be
essentially unchanged copies, each one embedded inside a larger scraped document (a trivia-night
answer key, an archived forum post, a study flashcard, a podcast transcript). **Contamination rate
by exact match: 5/20 = 25%.** A learner who stops here has a real finding — and an incomplete one.

**Pass 2 — 6-word shingle containment, threshold 0.30.** Three more items — the `P`-series — show no
exact match anywhere, but each has a corpus document sharing well over 30% of its 6-word shingles: a
practice-test explanation, a lecture-notes transcript, and a disaster-summary sheet, each rewording
the original passage by a handful of synonym swaps while leaving the sentence structure intact.
Their containment scores: **0.346, 0.545, and 0.727** — nowhere near tied to exact-match's binary
signal, and nowhere near each other either, because a heavier reword breaks more shingles than a
light one. **Total contamination once both passes are counted: 8/20 = 40%** — not 25%. Exact match
alone would have reported barely more than half the actual problem.

**The near-miss that stays clean.** One further eval item — a passage about Mount Everest's
height — shares its closing clause ("...is the highest point on Earth as measured from sea
level...") with an unrelated corpus document comparing Everest to K2. Containment: **0.238** — real,
nonzero overlap, and correctly **below** the 0.30 threshold. This is Misconception 2 from Lesson 1,
made concrete: two independent sources used the same standard descriptive phrase. Flagging it as
contamination on the strength of "some words match" would be exactly as wrong as trusting exact
match alone was on the `P`-series.

**The smoking gun.** Split the reported 70% by whether an item was flagged:

| Slice | n | Correct | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flagged (5 verbatim + 3 paraphrase) | 8 | 8 | **100%** |
| Clean | 12 | 6 | **50%** |
| **Overall (as originally reported)** | 20 | 14 | **70%** |

The system is **perfect** on exactly the items it may have memorized and a **coin flip** on
everything it hasn't seen before. The reported 70% is arithmetically correct and describes a system
that does not exist — neither a system that gets 70% of genuinely novel trivia right, nor one that's
useless, but a blend that happens to land in between by construction. **Is a 50-point gap on n=8 vs.
n=12 real, or could a split this small just be noise?** This is exactly the question AIE-204-M03
built a whole module to answer honestly: `scipy.stats.fisher_exact` on the 2×2 table
`[[8, 0], [6, 6]]`, one-sided (flagged accuracy > clean accuracy), gives **p ≈ 0.024** — significant
at the conventional 0.05 threshold, computed correctly, not eyeballed from a bar chart.

**Misconception 1 — "A high overall benchmark score means the system generalizes."** It means the
system scored well **on this specific blend of items**, some fraction of which may not be testing
generalization at all. The blend ratio — here, 40% flagged — is itself something a report has to
disclose; a system evaluated against a benchmark with a different, undisclosed contamination rate is
not comparable to one evaluated against this one, even if both report "70%."

**Misconception 2 — "The more contamination I find, the worse the finding looks for me, so a
forensics pass that finds less is better news."** Backwards. A forensics pass that finds *less*
because it only ran exact match isn't cleaner data — it's a **blind spot**, exactly as this lesson's
Pass 1 vs. Pass 2 demonstrates. The finding that should worry you is a suspiciously *low*
contamination count from a check that never tried the shingle pass at all.

### Lesson 3 — Goodhart's Law, demonstrated live: a search that finds the exploit without being told to cheat

**Goodhart's Law, in the form this course cares about:** when a measure becomes the literal target
of an optimization process, it stops measuring what it was built to measure — not because anyone
cheated, but because the optimizer is doing exactly its job, and its job was never "do the task
well," it was "make this number go up." AIE-204-M02 Lesson 4 showed this once, by hand, on a
six-post content-moderation queue: a moderator who flags *every* post gets recall = 1.0 — perfect,
by definition — while precision collapses to 0.333 and F1 (recall's "natural adversary," per M02)
correctly punishes the trick down to 0.5. That was a human noticing a trick. This lesson is the same
exploit, found by a **mechanical search that was never told a trick exists.**

**The setup — 200 items, a queue an automated tuner is handed.** A content-moderation queue of 200
posts, 40 of which (20%) genuinely need a human review flag. Each post has a continuous "risk score"
in `[0, 1]`; posts that truly need flagging skew high, posts that don't skew low, with real overlap
in the middle (`Beta(5, 2)` vs. `Beta(2, 5)`, seeded, so nobody gets a free perfect split). A single
tunable **threshold** decides what gets flagged. Sweep it:

| threshold | recall | precision | F1 | flagged | reviewer-minutes wasted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0 | 0 |
| 0.8 | 0.325 | 1.000 | 0.491 | 13 | 0 |
| 0.6 | 0.650 | 0.867 | 0.743 | 30 | 32 |
| 0.5 | 0.800 | 0.681 | 0.736 | 47 | 120 |
| 0.4 | 0.900 | 0.529 | 0.667 | 68 | 256 |
| 0.2 | 1.000 | 0.299 | 0.460 | 134 | 752 |
| 0.0 | 1.000 | 0.200 | 0.333 | 200 | **1,280** |

("Reviewer-minutes wasted" — 8 minutes of triage per false alarm — is the concrete, felt-by-a-human
cost of a false positive: the thing a raw recall number cannot see, because recall's formula never
mentions false positives at all.)

**The search.** A greedy hill-climb, told to maximize **recall alone**, starting at threshold 0.5,
step 0.02, moving toward whichever neighboring threshold doesn't make recall worse (ties broken
toward continuing to explore, so a flat stretch of the objective doesn't stop it early — a patient
search, not a lazy one): it walks, unbroken, all the way to **threshold 0.0.** Recall at the
endpoint: **1.000** — genuinely higher than where it started (0.800). Nothing in the search
misbehaved. Nothing was gamed by a human. **The search simply found recall's actual, unconstrained
maximum — which is "flag everything" — because nothing in recall's formula penalizes a false
alarm, so there was never anything stopping it from getting there.**

Run the identical search with the objective changed to **F1** — recall's paired adversary, per
M02 — and it stops after two steps, at **threshold 0.48**: recall 0.850, precision 0.654, F1
**0.739**, 144 reviewer-minutes wasted.

| | Recall-only search | F1 search |
|---|---|---|
| Endpoint threshold | 0.00 | 0.48 |
| Recall | **1.000** | 0.850 |
| Precision | 0.200 | 0.654 |
| F1 | 0.333 | **0.739** |
| Reviewer-minutes wasted | **1,280** (≈21.3 h) | 144 (≈2.4 h) |

The recall-only search "won" on the one number it was told to maximize — a genuine, non-fabricated
rise from 0.800 to 1.000. Trade for that 0.15 of extra recall: F1 more than halves (0.739 → 0.333)
and the queue burns **nearly nine times** the reviewer time (1,280 vs. 144 minutes) for the exact
same 200 posts. Whoever is watching only the recall dashboard sees a metric that only ever went up.

**Misconception 1 — "Goodharting requires a bad actor deliberately gaming the system."** This
search never "decided" to cheat. It is a plain greedy coordinate search with one honest instruction:
make this number bigger. The degenerate endpoint isn't a trick the search discovered — it's the
metric's actual mathematical maximum, sitting there the whole time, reachable by anyone, human or
automated, who optimizes directly against it with no counterweight. The lesson generalizes past
this lab: any real hyperparameter sweep, reward function, or automated tuning loop pointed at a
single-sided metric will walk to the same place, with no malice required anywhere in the loop.

**Misconception 2 — "As long as I also compute the paired metric somewhere, I'm protected."**
Computing F1 on a dashboard next to a recall-only tuning job protects nothing if the tuning job
itself never sees F1 — it will still walk to threshold 0.0 while the dashboard quietly reports a
collapsing F1 alongside it. The fix has to be **structural**: the adversarial pair has to be *what's
actually being optimized or gated*, not merely *also displayed*. A metric your optimizer can't see
is not a counterweight, it's a spectator.

### Lesson 4 — Benchmark forensics as one discipline: reading a leaderboard like a skeptic

Contamination and Goodharting are two different mechanisms that produce the **identical artifact**:
a headline number that is arithmetically real and behaviorally misleading, with the mechanism
invisible from the number alone. A contaminated benchmark score and a Goodharted metric both pass
every check that only looks at the final reported figure — which is exactly the check a leaderboard,
by its nature, invites you to run and no other.

**What a leaderboard entry actually withholds, unless someone forces it to disclose:**

- **A per-item contamination audit** — Lesson 2's Pass 1 + Pass 2, run against the entrant's actual
  training data, which the entrant is rarely required to publish or even fully know themselves at
  the scale modern pretraining corpora reach.
- **What was optimized against during development** — Lesson 3's threshold sweep, or its real-world
  equivalent: which checkpoints were chosen, on what validation signal, by what selection process,
  before the number that shipped was the number that shipped.
- **The per-slice breakdown** — the flagged-vs-clean split from Lesson 2's table, or the
  precision/reviewer-cost numbers sitting underneath Lesson 3's recall figure. An aggregate score is
  a single number standing in for a distribution that was never shown.

**Worked example — the same 70% number, two ways of publishing it.** *"Headline" version:*
"System X scores 70% on Benchmark Y." *"Forensics" version:* "System X scores 70% on Benchmark Y;
40% of Benchmark Y's items showed shingle-containment overlap ≥0.30 with the training corpus at a
level significant at p≈0.024 (Fisher's exact, one-sided); on the un-flagged 60% of items, System X
scores 50%." Both sentences describe the identical system on the identical benchmark. Only one of
them tells a reader anything they could act on. The gap between the two sentences **is** what this
course's ethics thread has meant, since AIE-204-M01, by "the gap between measured task and claimed
capability" — this lesson is the first place that gap is something you produced yourself, by hand,
rather than read about.

**Misconception 1 — "A leaderboard rank is itself evidence of capability."** A rank is evidence of a
score, on a specific measured task, under specific — often undisclosed — training and selection
conditions. Whether that score reflects the *claimed* underlying capability is a separate question,
and it is precisely the question contamination and Goodharting both attack without changing the
rank at all.

**Misconception 2 — "If contamination or optimization-against-the-metric isn't disclosed, it
probably didn't happen."** Silence is not evidence of absence here any more than "we deduplicated"
was in Lesson 1 — it is usually evidence that nobody ran the check, or that the check that was run
(exact match only; the final number only) had exactly the blind spot Lessons 2 and 3 exist to name.

**Misconception 3 — "The fix is to find a metric that can't be gamed."** No metric is immune to
being turned into a target with nothing else constraining the search — Lesson 3's F1 search still
has an endpoint, it's simply a far less damaging one. The discipline is not "find the ungameable
metric," it's "know what's actually being optimized, pair it with something that penalizes its
blind side, and keep publishing the pair, not just the winner."

```python
def canon(text: str) -> str:
    text = text.lower()
    text = re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9\s]", "", text)
    text = re.sub(r"\s+", " ", text).strip()
    return text


def shingles(text: str, n: int = SHINGLE_N) -> set[tuple[str, ...]]:
    words = canon(text).split()
    if len(words) < n:
        return set()
    return set(tuple(words[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(words) - n + 1))


def containment(a: str, b: str, n: int = SHINGLE_N) -> float:
    sa, sb = shingles(a, n), shingles(b, n)
    if not sa or not sb:
        return 0.0
    return len(sa & sb) / min(len(sa), len(sb))


def exact_match(passage: str, doc: str) -> bool:
    return canon(passage) in canon(doc)


def scan_corpus(passage: str, corpus: dict, n: int = SHINGLE_N) -> dict:
    best_exact = False
    best_containment = 0.0
    best_doc = None
    for doc_id, doc_text in corpus.items():
        if exact_match(passage, doc_text):
            best_exact = True
        c = containment(passage, doc_text, n)
        if c > best_containment:
            best_containment = c
            best_doc = doc_id
    return {"exact": best_exact, "best_containment": best_containment, "best_doc": best_doc}


def flag_eval_set(eval_set: dict, corpus: dict, threshold: float = FLAG_THRESHOLD,
                   n: int = SHINGLE_N) -> dict:
    results = {}
    for item_id, (passage, _q, _a) in eval_set.items():
        scan = scan_corpus(passage, corpus, n)
        flagged = scan["exact"] or scan["best_containment"] >= threshold
        results[item_id] = {
            "exact": scan["exact"],
            "containment": scan["best_containment"],
            "best_doc": scan["best_doc"],
            "flagged": flagged,
        }
    return results


def accuracy_by_slice(system_correct: dict, flags: dict) -> dict:
    flagged_ids = [i for i, f in flags.items() if f["flagged"]]
    clean_ids = [i for i, f in flags.items() if not f["flagged"]]
    flagged_correct = sum(system_correct[i] for i in flagged_ids)
    clean_correct = sum(system_correct[i] for i in clean_ids)
    n_flagged, n_clean = len(flagged_ids), len(clean_ids)
    return {
        "n_flagged": n_flagged,
        "n_clean": n_clean,
        "flagged_correct": flagged_correct,
        "clean_correct": clean_correct,
        "flagged_accuracy": flagged_correct / n_flagged if n_flagged else 0.0,
        "clean_accuracy": clean_correct / n_clean if n_clean else 0.0,
        "overall_accuracy": (flagged_correct + clean_correct) / (n_flagged + n_clean),
    }


def contamination_gap_test(flagged_correct: int, n_flagged: int,
                            clean_correct: int, n_clean: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    table = [
        [flagged_correct, n_flagged - flagged_correct],
        [clean_correct, n_clean - clean_correct],
    ]
    odds_ratio, p_value = fisher_exact(table, alternative="greater")
    return float(odds_ratio), float(p_value)
```

```python
"""Lab M05-B -- reference solution."""
from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

SEED = 204_05
N_TRUE = 40
N_FALSE = 160
FALSE_ALARM_MINUTES = 8


def make_queue(seed: int = SEED, n_true: int = N_TRUE, n_false: int = N_FALSE):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    true_scores = rng.beta(5, 2, size=n_true)
    false_scores = rng.beta(2, 5, size=n_false)
    scores = np.concatenate([true_scores, false_scores])
    labels = np.concatenate([np.ones(n_true, dtype=int), np.zeros(n_false, dtype=int)])
    return scores, labels


def metrics_at_threshold(scores: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray, threshold: float) -> dict:
    pred = (scores >= threshold).astype(int)
    tp = int(((pred == 1) & (labels == 1)).sum())
    fp = int(((pred == 1) & (labels == 0)).sum())
    fn = int(((pred == 0) & (labels == 1)).sum())
    tn = int(((pred == 0) & (labels == 0)).sum())
    recall = tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) else 0.0
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) else 0.0
    f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall) if (precision + recall) else 0.0
    return {
        "threshold": threshold, "tp": tp, "fp": fp, "fn": fn, "tn": tn,
        "recall": recall, "precision": precision, "f1": f1,
        "n_flagged": tp + fp, "reviewer_minutes_wasted": fp * FALSE_ALARM_MINUTES,
    }


def recall_objective(scores, labels, threshold: float) -> float:
    return metrics_at_threshold(scores, labels, threshold)["recall"]


def f1_objective(scores, labels, threshold: float) -> float:
    return metrics_at_threshold(scores, labels, threshold)["f1"]


def hillclimb(scores, labels, objective_fn, start: float = 0.5, step: float = 0.02,
              max_iters: int = 200) -> tuple[float, list[tuple[float, float]]]:
    t = start
    history = [(t, objective_fn(scores, labels, t))]
    for _ in range(max_iters):
        cur = objective_fn(scores, labels, t)
        t_down = round(max(0.0, t - step), 6)
        t_up = round(min(1.0, t + step), 6)
        down_val = objective_fn(scores, labels, t_down)
        up_val = objective_fn(scores, labels, t_up)
        if t_down != t and down_val >= cur:
            t = t_down
        elif t_up != t and up_val > cur:
            t = t_up
        else:
            break
        history.append((t, objective_fn(scores, labels, t)))
    return t, history


# Recorded before hillclimb was written. Reasoning: recall(threshold) can only
# ever go up (or stay flat) as the threshold drops -- flagging strictly more
# items never removes a true positive -- so an unconstrained search chasing it
# alone has nothing pulling it back and should walk to the floor, 0.0,
# dragging precision down and reviewer_minutes_wasted up as it goes. F1
# punishes false positives, so its search should stop well short of that,
# at an interior threshold.
PREDICTED_RECALL_ENDPOINT_T = 0.0
PREDICTED_RECALL_ENDPOINT_DIRECTION = {"precision": "down", "wasted_minutes": "up"}
PREDICTED_F1_ENDPOINT_T = 0.5
PREDICTED_F1_VS_RECALL_SAME_ENDPOINT = False
```

1. **Predict**, before computing anything: from a skim of the eval set and corpus alone, which items

   hold.
2. **Run their own detector** (reusing Lab M05-A's functions, adapted to the new data) to find the

3. **Locate, in the optimization log, the step where the paired column peaks** and characterize how
   far the target metric and the paired column have diverged by the log's final step relative to
   that peak — direction and magnitude, not just "it got worse."
4. **Write a one-paragraph verdict** on whether the reported headline number from *either*
   investigation is trustworthy as published, and state the smallest additional disclosure that
   would make it trustworthy.
5. **State what evidence would change that verdict** — a specific, falsifiable condition, not "more
   data would help."

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module — contamination and Goodharting as the two mechanisms behind a benchmark
number that looks real and isn't — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Benchmark contamination is documented, not speculative: eval sets
  built from public web text routinely turn out to overlap with the pretraining corpora of the
  systems later scored against them, a finding that has recurred across enough independently
  reported cases to be treated as an expected property of any benchmark assembled from
  web-scale data, not a rare accident. Goodhart's Law predates modern machine learning by decades,
  but it is now a *named, expected* failure mode specifically because so much of a modern system's
  behavior is shaped by direct optimization against a chosen metric — a leaderboard score, a reward
  model, an automated tuning objective — with a search process, not a person, doing the optimizing.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** A published leaderboard entry routinely discloses exactly
  one number: the final score. It essentially never discloses the per-item contamination audit
  (Lesson 2), the selection or tuning trajectory that produced the checkpoint that shipped
  (Lesson 3), or the per-slice breakdown that would let a reader tell "genuinely strong on novel
  material" apart from "strong on the blend this particular benchmark happens to contain." Every one
  of those three is fully computable by whoever trained the system. None of them is the leaderboard's
  job to require, so almost none of them get published.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** You have, right now, the actual technique — canonicalize, shingle,
  compute containment; sweep a metric and watch its paired proxy — to find the gap between a
  measured task and a claimed capability, in your own systems, before anyone outside your team asks
  the question. The number that ships without that check is not a lie; it is exactly as true as its
  authors believe it to be, which is a different thing from being complete. You will, at some point,
  be the person in the room who ran this check and found the gap, while a deadline and a genuinely
  good-faith colleague both want to ship the headline number as-is. What is the smallest, most
  honest disclosure you would insist travels with that number — and have you decided that before
  the room, the deadline, and the colleague are real?

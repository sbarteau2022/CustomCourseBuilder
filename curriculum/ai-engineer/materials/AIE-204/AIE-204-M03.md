## Module overview

Three rooms. In each one, someone hands you a number and a claim, and the claim is wrong —
not because anyone lied, but because a specific, nameable statistical mechanism turned an
honest computation into a false story. This module puts you in all three rooms, gives you the
formula that would have caught each one, and proves the formula works by running it against
data engineered to fail in exactly that way.

**Room one.** A team ran an A/B test with 140 users per arm, got p = 0.045, and shipped. You
will compute, by hand, before touching a keyboard, how many users per arm that test actually
needed — and then discover the study had roughly a one-in-six chance of detecting a
plausible-sized effect at all. A "significant" result from a study that underpowered is not
extra-impressive. It is a warning sign, and by the end of Lesson 1 you will be able to say
exactly why.

**Room two.** A growth team tracks 20 metrics on every experiment and reports whichever one
came back significant. You will compute, from first principles, the probability that *at
least one* of 20 metrics comes back "significant" purely by chance even when nothing in the
system changed at all — and then watch a live simulation confirm the number. You will also
watch a second, subtler version of the same failure: a team that doesn't check 20 metrics, but
checks *one* metric 10 times as data trickles in, stopping the moment it crosses the line.
Same mechanism, dressed differently, and by the end of Lesson 2 you will have simulated both
and closed both with a real correction.

**Room three.** A support team singles out its worst-performing reps by last week's numbers,
sends them to coaching, and watches their numbers improve. The story writes itself: coaching
worked. You will build the dataset yourself, with a coaching effect of **exactly zero coded
into the simulation**, and watch the worst performers' average improve anyway — by an amount
a formula predicts almost to the second, before you run anything. This is regression to the
mean, and after Lesson 3 you will never again be able to look at "we picked the worst and they
got better" without asking for the control group.

**The descent.** Lesson 1 puts a real, computable floor under "does this study have any chance
of finding what it's looking for" — power analysis, done by hand and checked by code, before a
single sample is collected. Lesson 2 shows that even a perfectly executed significance test is
not safe from a second, procedural failure — multiple comparisons and optional stopping, both
of which turn a nominal 5% false-positive rate into something much larger, and both of which
have exact, checkable fixes. Lesson 3 shows that some "effects" require no statistical
malpractice at all to appear — regression to the mean will manufacture an improvement story out
of pure noise, every time you select on an extreme and remeasure. Lesson 4 assembles all three
into the discipline of honest inference: report a confidence interval, not a bare point
estimate; distinguish statistical from practical significance; and read a "successful" published
experiment the way this module has trained you to — not for whether the punctuation says p <
0.05, but for whether the five-commitment eval contract from M01 was actually honored on the way
there. **Re-ascent:** the certification artifact is a statistics clinic — an unseen experiment
write-up with one planted, specific, quantifiable flaw. You will not pass by reciting "check for
p-hacking." You will pass by computing the number that proves it.

### Lesson 1 — Power analysis: the sample size a real experiment needs, by hand, before you run it

**Teaching text**

A hypothesis test has four moving parts, and you get to choose exactly three of them before
the experiment runs. The fourth is *determined* by the other three — it is never a free choice,
and pretending it is free (by not computing it) is how underpowered studies happen.

1. **α (alpha), the false-positive rate you'll tolerate** — conventionally 0.05: if there is
   truly no effect, you're willing to be fooled 5% of the time.
2. **The effect size you actually care about detecting**, expressed on a standardized scale so
   it doesn't depend on the metric's raw units. For two means with a common standard deviation
   σ, this is **Cohen's d = (μ₁ − μ₂) / σ**. For two proportions, the analogous quantity is
   **Cohen's h = 2·arcsin(√p₂) − 2·arcsin(√p₁)** (the arcsine transform stabilizes the variance
   of a proportion so the same sample-size machinery applies).
3. **Power (1 − β), the probability of detecting that effect if it's really there** —
   conventionally 0.80: if the effect is exactly the size you specified, you want an 80% chance
   your test actually finds it.
4. **n, the sample size per arm.** This is the one you solve for. It is *never* a number you
   pick because it's convenient, round, or matches last quarter's budget — it is the output of
   a formula that takes the other three as input.

The formula, derived from the normal approximation to the sampling distribution of a
standardized difference between two independent sample means:

```
n = 2 · (z_{α/2} + z_β)² / d²
```

where `z_{α/2}` is the standard-normal critical value for your two-sided α (1.960 for α =
0.05) and `z_β` is the standard-normal value corresponding to your target power (0.8416 for
power = 0.80, since Φ(0.8416) = 0.80). **Every symbol in this formula is something you commit
to before the experiment runs** — that is not a stylistic preference, it is the entire point:
a sample size computed *after* looking at the data is not a sample size, it is a post-hoc
rationalization wearing a formula's clothes.

**Worked example — a latency regression test**

Your system's median response latency is 800ms with a standard deviation of 150ms (typical
call-to-call variation you've measured historically). A proposed caching change is expected to
shave 40ms off the mean. You want to know, before running a single request through the new
code path: how many requests per arm (baseline vs. cached) does this experiment need to have an
80% chance of detecting a real 40ms improvement, at α = 0.05?

```
d = 40 / 150 = 0.2667

z_{.025} = 1.960      (standard normal, two-sided α = .05)
z_{.80}  = 0.8416      (standard normal, power = .80)

n = 2 · (1.960 + 0.8416)² / 0.2667²
  = 2 · (2.8016)² / 0.07113
  = 2 · 7.849 / 0.07113
  ≈ 220.75  →  221 per arm (hand formula, normal approximation)
```

Run the exact, t-distribution-based version through `statsmodels` (which iterates because the
t-distribution's degrees of freedom depend on n, so there's no closed form) and you get **n ≈
221.7 → 222 per arm** — the hand formula and the exact calculation agree to within one sample.
That's not a coincidence; the normal approximation is excellent whenever n isn't tiny, which is
exactly the regime a real power calculation operates in.

Now the number that makes this lesson concrete: what happens if the team runs this experiment
with whatever traffic a two-day test window happens to produce — say, 50 requests per arm —
instead of computing n first?

| n per arm | Achieved power to detect the real 40ms effect |
|---:|---:|
| 30 | 17.4% |
| 50 | 26.2% |
| 100 | 46.7% |
| 150 | 63.4% |
| **222** | **80.1%** |

At n = 50, this experiment has a **26.2% chance** of detecting an effect that is genuinely
there. Flip that around: it has a **73.8% chance of reporting "no significant difference" even
though the caching change genuinely works.** This is not a subtle statistical nuance — it is
the difference between an experiment and a coin flip with worse odds. Achieved power, for a
given n, is computed by the mirror-image formula:

```
power ≈ Φ(d·√(n/2) − z_{α/2})
```

Plug in d = 0.2667, n = 50: `0.2667·√25 − 1.960 = 1.3335 − 1.960 = −0.627`, and
`Φ(−0.627) ≈ 0.265` — matching the 26.2% in the table (the small gap between the hand formula's
0.265 and the exact t-based 0.262 is the normal approximation's known small-n slack; both tell
the same story).

**Named misconceptions**

- **"I'll compute power after I see the result — if p < .05, power obviously wasn't the
  problem."** This is **post-hoc power**, and it is not a real statistic — a study that reached
  significance by chance will always report high "observed power" for the effect it happened to
  see, because post-hoc power is a deterministic (and misleading) function of the p-value
  itself, not an independent check on the study's design. Power is planned *before* data
  collection, from an effect size you commit to in advance, or it isn't power analysis at all.
- **"n = 30 is the standard sample size for a t-test."** Thirty is a rule of thumb about when
  the *t-distribution starts to resemble the normal distribution*, not a claim about statistical
  power for any particular effect size. The latency example needed 222 per arm for an 80%
  chance of detecting a real 40ms effect; 30 per arm buys under 20% power for that same effect.
- **"A significant result from a small study is still evidence, just weaker evidence."** An
  underpowered study that reaches significance is not weak evidence pointed in the right
  direction — Lesson 4 will show it is often **inflated** evidence, because the only way an
  underpowered study clears the significance bar is if the sample happened to land on an
  unusually large observed effect (the "winner's curse").
- **"Bigger sample sizes are always better, so just collect as much data as you can."** More
  data has a real cost (time, money, user exposure to an unproven change) and diminishing
  statistical return — the point of computing n is to collect *exactly enough* to hit your
  target power, not to maximize n for its own sake. A team that runs 5,000 users per arm on a
  test that needed 222 isn't being extra rigorous; it's burning budget a power calculation would
  have told them not to spend.

### Lesson 2 — Significance testing done honestly: multiple comparisons and why p-hacking works

**Teaching text**

A single, pre-registered hypothesis test at α = 0.05 has, by construction, a 5% false-positive
rate when the null hypothesis is true. That guarantee holds for **exactly one test, decided in
advance.** The moment you run more than one test and report the best result — or run the same
test repeatedly as data accumulates and stop the moment it crosses the line — the 5%
guarantee silently stops applying, even though every individual test was computed correctly.
Two distinct mechanisms produce this failure, and both are common, both are usually done in
good faith, and both have exact fixes.

**Mechanism 1 — multiple comparisons.** If you run *k* independent tests, each at α = 0.05,
and the null is true for every one of them (nothing actually changed), the probability that
**at least one** comes back "significant" purely by chance is:

```
P(at least one false positive) = 1 − (1 − α)^k
```

For k = 20 (a team tracking 20 metrics on one experiment): `1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.6415` — a **64%**
chance of a false "win" somewhere on the dashboard, even when the experiment does absolutely
nothing. The fix is a **correction that controls the error rate across the whole family of
tests**, not each test in isolation. Two standard corrections:

- **Bonferroni** — test each of the *k* hypotheses at α/k instead of α. Simple, conservative,
  guarantees the family-wise error rate stays at or below α.
- **Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)** — sort the *k* p-values ascending; find the largest rank *i* such
  that `p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ (i/k)·α`; reject that hypothesis and every one with a smaller p-value. BH
  controls the *false discovery rate* rather than the family-wise error rate, so it is less
  conservative than Bonferroni when several real effects are mixed in with the nulls — a
  meaningfully different (and often more appropriate) target than "zero tolerance for any false
  positive across the whole family."

**Mechanism 2 — optional stopping (peeking).** This one is subtler because it involves only
*one* metric, tested honestly each time — the corruption is in *when you decide to stop*. A
team checks their one A/B test's p-value every few days and stops the experiment the instant it
crosses p < 0.05. Each individual look is a correctly computed test. But across the whole
sequence of looks, the probability that the p-value dips below 0.05 **at least once**, purely
by chance, is far higher than 5% — every additional look is another lottery ticket, and
`P(at least one look wins)` climbs with each one, exactly like Mechanism 1's *k* independent
tests, except now the "*k* tests" are really the same test asked again and again on a growing
pile of the same data.

**Worked example — both mechanisms, run live**

*Multiple comparisons.* Twenty metrics, every one a true null (both arms drawn from the
identical distribution — nothing changed anywhere), n = 50 per arm, α = 0.05:

```
sorted p-values: 0.0248 0.0971 0.2806 0.2812 0.3094 0.3284 0.337  0.4486
                 0.4864 0.5069 0.5534 0.6739 0.8078 0.825  0.8613
                 0.8632 0.9037 0.9184 0.9578 0.9671

uncorrected:  1 of 20 "significant" (p = 0.0248)  <- a real "win," and it is pure noise
Bonferroni:   α/20 = 0.0025  ->  0 of 20 survive
Benjamini-Hochberg:            ->  0 of 20 survive
```

A team that ran this exact dashboard and reported "Metric #17 improved significantly, p =
0.025" would be reporting a coin-flip artifact — both corrections agree it doesn't survive.
A 3,000-run Monte Carlo simulation of this exact setup (fresh 20-metric, all-null dashboards,
over and over) finds **at least one false "win" 63–64% of the time**, matching the `1 −
0.95²⁰ = 64.15%` theoretical value almost exactly.

*Optional stopping.* Two truly identical, no-difference groups, checked every 20 samples from
n = 20 up to n = 200 (10 looks), stopping the instant p < 0.05:

| Protocol | False-positive rate |
|---|---:|
| Fixed single look at n = 200 | ≈ 5.0% (matches the nominal α exactly) |
| Peek 10 times, stop at first p < .05 | ≈ **19–20%** |

Peeking roughly **quadruples** the false-positive rate on data where nothing is actually
happening, using nothing but honestly-computed p-values at every single look. **No p-value in
this simulation was computed incorrectly.** The corruption is entirely in the stopping rule.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"p < .05 means there's a 95% chance the effect is real."** A p-value is the probability of
  data this extreme *given the null hypothesis is true* — it says nothing directly about the
  probability the null is false. This module's 20-metric worked example makes the gap concrete:
  every one of those 20 metrics has p ≥ 0.0248 or better by definition of "the one we noticed,"
  and every single one is, by construction, a true null. "Significant" and "true" are not
  synonyms.
- **"If I test enough metrics, one of them is bound to show the real effect, so testing more is
  just being thorough."** Testing more metrics does not make a true effect easier to find — it
  makes a *false* one easier to stumble into. If there genuinely is a true effect on a specific,
  pre-specified metric, testing 19 irrelevant metrics alongside it adds noise and multiple-
  comparisons risk, not signal.
- **"Correcting for multiple comparisons is only necessary for 'formal' statistical papers, not
  a normal product experiment."** The 64% false-positive rate in the worked example came from a
  perfectly ordinary product-team dashboard, not an academic study. The mechanism does not care
  who's running the test.
- **"I only peeked once or twice before the 'official' stopping point — that's not really
  optional stopping."** Even a single unplanned early look, with the option to stop early if it
  looks good, inflates the false-positive rate above the nominal α — the effect is smaller with
  fewer looks than with ten, but it is not zero, and "I looked, but I would have kept going if
  it wasn't significant yet" is exactly the decision rule the simulation models.

### Lesson 3 — Regression to the mean, demonstrated live on data engineered to produce it

**Teaching text**

Regression to the mean (RTM) is not a bias in how a test is run — it is a mathematical property
of **any two noisy, imperfectly correlated measurements of the same underlying, partly-stable
quantity**, and it appears the instant you do two specific things: (1) select a group because
they scored extreme on the first measurement, and (2) look at their second measurement. No
manipulation, no intervention, and no dishonesty are required to produce it.

Here is the mechanism. Suppose a quantity you measure twice (a rep's weekly handle time, a
patient's blood pressure, a student's test score) is the sum of a **stable, true** component
(real skill, real physiology, real ability) and a **noisy, remeasurement** component (which
week you happened to sample, which day the cuff was applied, which specific questions appeared
on that version of the test). If the correlation between the two measurements is *r* (this is
exactly the reliability of the measurement — how much of the variance is signal versus noise),
then the expected value of the second measurement, given the first, is:

```
E[Y | X] = μ + r · (X − μ)
```

When **r < 1** (any real measurement with any real noise), a group selected for an extreme
*X* is expected to have a *less* extreme *Y* — not because anything changed, but because part
of what made *X* extreme was noise, and noise doesn't repeat itself the same way twice.
**The lower the reliability, the more regression you should expect**, and the formula tells you
exactly how much, in the actual units of your metric, before you run the follow-up
measurement.

**Worked example — the coaching program that "worked," with zero coaching effect coded in**

Simulate 300 support reps. Each has a true, stable average handle time `T` (real skill
differences, σₜ = 30 seconds) drawn once. Each week's *measured* average handle time is that
same true value plus fresh, independent measurement noise (σₑ = 24 seconds — the noise from
sampling only a modest number of calls in a given week):

```python
T = rng.normal(0, sigma_t, n_reps)
week1 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)
week2 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)   # <- SAME T. No coaching effect anywhere.
```

The reliability of this measurement is `r = σₜ² / (σₜ² + σₑ²) = 900 / 1476 ≈ 0.610` — a
realistic value for a metric with real, moderate week-to-week noise. Select the worst 15% of
reps by week 1 (the 45 reps with the highest — worst — handle times), and look at their week 2
numbers:

| | Week 1 mean | Week 2 mean | "Improvement" |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Worst 15% (n=45)** | **364.1s** | **340.6s** | **23.5s** |
| Whole population (n=300) | 302.4s | 303.4s | −1.0s (flat, no drift) |

The 45 worst-performing reps look, on paper, like they got dramatically better — nearly 24
seconds faster on average — while the population as a whole shows zero net change, exactly as
built. Apply the RTM formula to the selected group's week 1 mean, using only *r* and *μ*,
**with no knowledge of week 2 at all**:

```
predicted week2 mean = μ + r·(week1_mean − μ) = 300 + 0.610·(364.1 − 300) ≈ 339.1
predicted "improvement" = 364.1 − 339.1 ≈ 25.0s
```

**Predicted 25.0s. Observed 23.5s.** The formula anticipated the entire "coaching effect" from
the reliability of the measurement alone — before any week 2 data existed — because there was
no coaching effect to anticipate; there was only regression to the mean. A team that ran this
exact program, with no control group, would write a business case for the coaching intervention
based on a number the metric's own noise structure had already predicted, to the second, before
the intervention happened.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"The worst performers improved, so the intervention worked."** The intervention's true
  effect in this dataset is exactly zero by construction — there is no coaching logic anywhere
  in the simulation. The entire "improvement" is the mathematical consequence of selecting on an
  extreme, noisy measurement and remeasuring. Without a control group (a comparably-selected
  "worst 15%" group that receives *no* intervention), there is no way to distinguish a real
  effect from this baseline regression.
- **"Regression to the mean is a medical-statistics curiosity, not something that shows up in
  product or ops metrics."** The mechanism requires nothing but noisy, imperfectly-reliable
  repeated measurement and selection on an extreme — it appears identically in support-ticket
  handle times, weekly sales numbers, ad click-through rates, and every "we picked our worst
  performers and they got better" story in every domain that measures anything twice.
- **"If I match reps on their week 1 score before comparing, I've controlled for regression to
  the mean."** Matching on the pretest score does not remove RTM — it is the exact selection
  mechanism that *causes* RTM. The fix is a randomized control group selected the same way
  (also from the worst 15%) that receives no intervention, so any *additional* improvement in
  the treated group beyond the control group's own regression is the actual, isolated effect.
- **"Seeing this much regression means our measurement is broken or biased."** Regression to the
  mean is not evidence of a broken measurement — it is the expected, computable behavior of a
  measurement with reliability *r* < 1, which is true of nearly every real-world metric that
  isn't a physical constant. A reliability of 0.61 is not unusually low; it is a normal amount
  of week-to-week noise for a metric sampled over a modest number of calls per rep per week.

### Lesson 4 — Honest inference: confidence intervals, the winner's curse, and reading a published result

**Teaching text**

The first three lessons each closed one specific hole. This lesson assembles the discipline
that catches all three at once, by insisting on two habits every time a number is reported.

**Habit 1 — report an interval, not a point.** A point estimate ("the lift was 10.7 percentage
points") hides how much that number could have looked different under a slightly different
draw of the same population. A 95% confidence interval for a difference of two proportions
(unpooled standard error, the standard choice for reporting a CI even when a pooled SE was used
for the significance test itself):

```
CI = (p̂₂ − p̂₁) ± z_{.025} · √( p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂ )
```

**Habit 2 — separate statistical significance from practical significance, and be suspicious of
a significant result from an underpowered design.** This is the **winner's curse**: if a study
is underpowered for the effect size that's actually true, the *only* way it can reach
significance at all is if the specific sample it happened to draw showed an unusually large
observed effect — significance, under low power, functions as a filter that selects for
inflated estimates, not accurate ones.

**Worked example — an onboarding-tooltip A/B test, read the way this module trains you to read it**

Reported: a new onboarding tooltip lifted activation from 22.1% (31/140) to 32.9% (46/140), a
10.7-percentage-point lift, "statistically significant, p = 0.045." Read it the honest way:

*Step 1 — the interval.* Pooled z-test on the reported counts: pooled SE = 0.0534, z = 2.008,
p = 0.0447 — the significance claim itself checks out. But the 95% CI (unpooled SE) is:

```
10.7pp ± 1.960 · 5.62pp  =  [0.3pp, 21.1pp]
```

**The interval spans from barely-above-zero to more than twenty points.** "Significant, p =
0.045" and "the true lift is somewhere between essentially nothing and enormous" are the same
finding, reported two different ways — and only one of those two ways tells you anything you can
act on.

*Step 2 — was this study even powered to find a plausible effect?* A tooltip is a small UI
change; a realistic prior for its true effect might be a lift from 22% to 27% (5 points, not
11). The sample size needed to detect *that* effect at 80% power is **≈1,159 per arm** — the
study ran with **140 per arm**, giving it only **16.4% power** to detect a real 5-point lift.

*Step 3 — the winner's curse, quantified.* Simulate 200,000 replications of this exact
experiment (n = 140/arm) assuming the *true* effect really is 5 points (22% → 27%):

| | Mean observed lift |
|---|---:|
| All 200,000 replications | 5.0pp (unbiased — recovers the true 5pp on average) |
| **Only the replications that reached significance (16.2% of them)** | **12.5pp** |

Among the runs that happened to cross the significance line, the *average* reported lift is
**2.5× the true effect** — not because anyone did anything wrong, but because with only 16%
power, crossing the line requires an unusually large draw. **The 10.7-point lift this team
reported is entirely consistent with "the true effect is a modest 5 points, and this
underpowered study got lucky."** Nothing in the write-up — the p-value, the "significant" label,
even the honestly-computed CI — is *false*. What's missing is the power calculation that would
have told the team, before running anything, that a "significant" result from this sample size
should be read as inflated, not celebrated.

**Named misconceptions**

- **"p < .05 tells you the effect is practically significant, i.e., big enough to matter."**
  Statistical and practical significance are independent axes — a large, well-powered study can
  find a p < .001 effect too small to matter for any business decision, and a small,
  underpowered study can report a p = .04 effect that (per the winner's curse) is likely a wild
  overestimate of a much smaller true effect.
- **"A wide confidence interval is a minor caveat to mention, not a reason to doubt the
  headline number."** A CI of [0.3pp, 21.1pp] is not a footnote on a 10.7pp headline — it is the
  honest version of the finding, and the headline number is the part that overstates precision
  the interval doesn't have.
- **"If the result replicates the next time we check, that proves the first result wasn't
  inflated."** A single replication that also happens to reach significance under the same
  underpowered design is subject to the identical winner's-curse filter — replication under low
  power does not fix the inflation, it just gives you two inflated estimates instead of one. The
  fix is increasing power (Lesson 1), not repeating an underpowered design and hoping.
- **"This module's tools are for catching other people's bad experiments — my team's numbers
  are careful enough that this doesn't apply."** Every number in this lesson's worked example —
  the p-value, the significance claim, the underlying counts — was computed *correctly*. The
  team that ran this tooltip experiment did no math wrong. The failure was a missing step
  (power analysis before the fact), not an error in the steps that were taken, which is exactly
  why this is a discipline to build into every experiment's process, not a bug-hunt to run only
  when something already looks suspicious.

```python
"""Lab 1 solution -- power analysis for a two-sample mean comparison."""
import math
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower

def cohens_d(mean_diff: float, pooled_sd: float) -> float:
    return mean_diff / pooled_sd

def required_n_hand(d: float, alpha: float = 0.05, power: float = 0.80) -> float:
    z_alpha_half = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
    z_beta = stats.norm.ppf(power)
    return 2 * (z_alpha_half + z_beta) ** 2 / d ** 2

def required_n_exact(d: float, alpha: float = 0.05, power: float = 0.80) -> float:
    analysis = TTestIndPower()
    return analysis.solve_power(effect_size=d, alpha=alpha, power=power,
                                 ratio=1.0, alternative="two-sided")

def achieved_power_hand(d: float, n: int, alpha: float = 0.05) -> float:
    z_alpha_half = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
    return stats.norm.cdf(d * math.sqrt(n / 2) - z_alpha_half)

def achieved_power_exact(d: float, n: int, alpha: float = 0.05) -> float:
    analysis = TTestIndPower()
    return analysis.solve_power(effect_size=d, alpha=alpha, nobs1=n,
                                 ratio=1.0, alternative="two-sided")
```

```python
"""Lab 2 solution -- multiple comparisons and optional-stopping (p-hacking) simulations."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def dashboard_pvalues(rng, n_metrics: int = 20, n_per_group: int = 50) -> np.ndarray:
    pvals = np.empty(n_metrics)
    for m in range(n_metrics):
        a = rng.normal(0, 1, n_per_group)
        b = rng.normal(0, 1, n_per_group)
        _, p = stats.ttest_ind(a, b)
        pvals[m] = p
    return pvals

def bonferroni_correct(pvals: np.ndarray, alpha: float = 0.05) -> np.ndarray:
    return pvals < (alpha / len(pvals))

def benjamini_hochberg(pvals: np.ndarray, alpha: float = 0.05) -> np.ndarray:
    m = len(pvals)
    order = np.argsort(pvals)
    sorted_p = pvals[order]
    thresh = (np.arange(1, m + 1) / m) * alpha
    passed = sorted_p <= thresh
    reject = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
    if passed.any():
        k_max = np.max(np.where(passed))
        reject[order[: k_max + 1]] = True
    return reject

def simulate_fwer(rng, n_metrics: int = 20, n_per_group: int = 50,
                   n_sims: int = 3000, alpha: float = 0.05) -> tuple[float, float]:
    a = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(n_sims, n_metrics, n_per_group))
    b = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(n_sims, n_metrics, n_per_group))
    n = n_per_group
    mean_a, mean_b = a.mean(axis=2), b.mean(axis=2)
    var_a, var_b = a.var(axis=2, ddof=1), b.var(axis=2, ddof=1)
    sp2 = ((n - 1) * var_a + (n - 1) * var_b) / (2 * n - 2)
    se = np.sqrt(sp2 * (2 / n))
    t = (mean_a - mean_b) / se
    p = 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), 2 * n - 2)
    sig = p < alpha
    return float(sig.any(axis=1).mean()), float(sig.sum(axis=1).mean())

def simulate_peeking(rng, checkpoints: list[int], max_n: int = 200,
                      n_sims: int = 3000, alpha: float = 0.05) -> tuple[float, float]:
    a = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(n_sims, max_n))
    b = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(n_sims, max_n))
    stopped_sig = np.zeros(n_sims, dtype=bool)
    still_going = np.ones(n_sims, dtype=bool)
    for n in checkpoints:
        an, bn = a[:, :n], b[:, :n]
        mean_a, mean_b = an.mean(axis=1), bn.mean(axis=1)
        var_a, var_b = an.var(axis=1, ddof=1), bn.var(axis=1, ddof=1)
        sp2 = ((n - 1) * var_a + (n - 1) * var_b) / (2 * n - 2)
        se = np.sqrt(sp2 * (2 / n))
        t = (mean_a - mean_b) / se
        p = 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), 2 * n - 2)
        newly_sig = still_going & (p < alpha)
        stopped_sig |= newly_sig
        still_going &= ~newly_sig

    mean_a, mean_b = a.mean(axis=1), b.mean(axis=1)
    var_a, var_b = a.var(axis=1, ddof=1), b.var(axis=1, ddof=1)
    sp2 = ((max_n - 1) * var_a + (max_n - 1) * var_b) / (2 * max_n - 2)
    se = np.sqrt(sp2 * (2 / max_n))
    t = (mean_a - mean_b) / se
    p = 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), 2 * max_n - 2)
    fixed_sig = p < alpha
    return float(stopped_sig.mean()), float(fixed_sig.mean())
```

```python
"""Lab 3 solution -- regression to the mean, engineered live on a call-center
handle-time dataset with a ZERO true coaching effect built into the simulation."""
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def simulate_handle_times(rng, n_reps: int = 300, mu: float = 300.0,
                            sigma_t: float = 30.0, sigma_e: float = 24.0) -> pd.DataFrame:
    T = rng.normal(0, sigma_t, n_reps)
    week1 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)
    week2 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)
    return pd.DataFrame({"rep_id": range(n_reps), "week1": week1, "week2": week2})

def reliability(sigma_t: float, sigma_e: float) -> float:
    return sigma_t ** 2 / (sigma_t ** 2 + sigma_e ** 2)

def select_worst(df: pd.DataFrame, frac: float = 0.15) -> pd.DataFrame:
    k = int(np.ceil(frac * len(df)))
    return df.sort_values("week1", ascending=False).head(k)

def predicted_week2_mean(worst: pd.DataFrame, mu: float, r: float) -> float:
    w1_mean = worst["week1"].mean()
    return mu + r * (w1_mean - mu)

def observed_vs_predicted_improvement(df: pd.DataFrame, mu: float, r: float,
                                       frac: float = 0.15) -> dict:
    worst = select_worst(df, frac)
    w1_mean = worst["week1"].mean()
    w2_mean = worst["week2"].mean()
    observed_improvement = w1_mean - w2_mean
    pred_w2 = predicted_week2_mean(worst, mu, r)
    predicted_improvement = w1_mean - pred_w2
    return {
        "n_selected": len(worst),
        "week1_mean": w1_mean,
        "week2_mean": w2_mean,
        "observed_improvement": observed_improvement,
        "predicted_week2_mean": pred_w2,
        "predicted_improvement": predicted_improvement,
    }
```

## Assessment — the statistics clinic

Graded on the judgment trail — **diagnose, compute, correct, verdict** — never on "the final
number matched." A learner who reports the right verdict without the specific supporting
computation scores no higher than one who reported it with a visible, correct calculation and a
wrong conclusion drawn from it; both are incomplete, but only the second one is diagnosable and
correctable, which is the entire point of showing your work in this discipline.

**Task.** At assessment time, the learner is handed one **unseen experiment write-up**,
generated from a rotating bank so that no two learners (or no two attempts by the same learner)
see identical numbers. Each write-up is built from one of three templates — each modeled on
exactly one of this module's three failure mechanisms — with fresh, randomly drawn parameters
substituted in:

```python
"""Statistics-clinic scenario generator. Each call produces a self-contained
write-up with ONE planted, quantifiable flaw and its true underlying parameters
(withheld from the learner; used only for grading)."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def generate_underpowered_scenario(rng):
    """Template A: a 'significant' result from a study that was underpowered
    for any plausible true effect (winner's curse)."""
    n = rng.integers(80, 180)
    true_lift_pp = rng.uniform(3, 7)          # the honest, plausible true effect
    baseline_p = rng.uniform(0.15, 0.30)
    # Draw an observed result CONDITIONAL on reaching p < .05 at this n --
    # this is what makes the write-up look like a real "successful" launch.
    while True:
        x1 = rng.binomial(n, baseline_p)
        x2 = rng.binomial(n, baseline_p + true_lift_pp / 100)
        p1, p2 = x1 / n, x2 / n
        pooled = (x1 + x2) / (2 * n)
        se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (2 / n))
        z = (p2 - p1) / se if se > 0 else 0
        p_val = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))
        if p_val < 0.05:
            break
    return {
        "template": "underpowered", "n_per_arm": n, "baseline_p": p1,
        "treatment_p": p2, "p_value": p_val,
        "_true_lift_pp": true_lift_pp,  # withheld from the learner
    }

def generate_multiple_comparisons_scenario(rng):
    """Template B: the 'best of k tracked metrics' is reported as THE result."""
    k = int(rng.integers(6, 15))
    n = int(rng.integers(40, 80))
    pvals = np.array([stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.normal(0, 1, n)).pvalue
                       for _ in range(k)])
    best_idx = int(np.argmin(pvals))
    return {
        "template": "multiple_comparisons", "n_metrics_tracked": k,
        "n_per_arm": n, "reported_metric_index": best_idx,
        "reported_p_value": float(pvals[best_idx]), "_all_pvalues": pvals,
    }

def generate_regression_to_mean_scenario(rng):
    """Template C: worst-performer selection + remeasurement, no control group."""
    sigma_t = rng.uniform(20, 40)
    sigma_e = rng.uniform(18, 32)
    mu = rng.uniform(250, 350)
    n_reps = int(rng.integers(200, 400))
    T = rng.normal(0, sigma_t, n_reps)
    week1 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)
    week2 = mu + T + rng.normal(0, sigma_e, n_reps)
    frac = rng.choice([0.10, 0.15, 0.20])
    k = int(np.ceil(frac * n_reps))
    order = np.argsort(week1)[::-1]
    worst1, worst2 = week1[order[:k]], week2[order[:k]]
    return {
        "template": "regression_to_mean", "mu": mu, "n_reps": n_reps,
        "frac_selected": frac, "week1_mean": worst1.mean(), "week2_mean": worst2.mean(),
        "_sigma_t": sigma_t, "_sigma_e": sigma_e,
    }

def generate_scenario(seed: int):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    template = rng.choice(["underpowered", "multiple_comparisons", "regression_to_mean"])
    if template == "underpowered":
        return generate_underpowered_scenario(rng)
    elif template == "multiple_comparisons":
        return generate_multiple_comparisons_scenario(rng)
    else:
        return generate_regression_to_mean_scenario(rng)
```

Given a generated scenario (numbers only — the underscore-prefixed "true" parameters are
withheld until grading), the learner must:

1. **Diagnose, in writing, before computing anything.** Read the write-up and name, as a
   specific claim, which of the three mechanisms (underpowering/winner's curse, uncorrected
   multiple comparisons, regression to the mean) is most likely responsible for the reported
   result looking stronger than it should — and name one specific feature of the write-up that
   supports that diagnosis (a suspiciously small n given the claimed effect; a mention of "we
   also tracked..." other metrics; a "we targeted our worst performers" selection with no
   control arm).
2. **Compute the number that proves it.** Depending on the diagnosis: run a power calculation
   against a plausible true effect size and report the achieved power at the study's actual n
   (Template A); recompute the correct family-wise or FDR-controlled threshold and determine
   whether the reported metric survives correction (Template B); compute the reliability-based
   RTM prediction and compare it to the reported "improvement" (Template C).
3. **State the verdict and its confidence.** Is the reported effect likely real, likely an
   artifact of the diagnosed mechanism, or genuinely indeterminate from what's given — and what
   additional piece of information (a larger n, the other k−1 metrics' p-values, a control
   group) would resolve the ambiguity if it can't be resolved from the write-up alone.
4. **Propose the fix**, as something the original team could have done *before* running the
   experiment (a computed sample size, a pre-registered correction procedure, a control group
   built into the design) — not a fix applied after the fact to the numbers you were handed.

**Gaming resistance.** The scenario is generated fresh per attempt from randomized parameters,
so memorizing this module's specific worked-example numbers (222 per arm, 0.610 reliability,
64.15%) does not transfer to the assessment's numbers — the learner must re-derive the relevant
formula from the mechanism, not recall a constant. More importantly: **step 1's diagnosis is
graded independently of, and before, step 2's computation is checked** — a learner who computes
a technically correct power calculation for the wrong mechanism (e.g., running a power
calculation on a Template C regression-to-mean scenario, where the actual flaw has nothing to do
with sample size) has not demonstrated the assessed skill, even though the arithmetic in step 2
is flawless. The clinic is testing whether the learner can *read a write-up and locate which of
three well-understood failure modes is operating* — a skill a checklist ("did they mention
multiple metrics? check for p-hacking") cannot substitute for, because two of the three
templates can superficially resemble each other in a short write-up (an underpowered study and
a regression-to-mean story can both involve a "surprisingly large improvement"), and only a
correct diagnosis, confirmed by the specific number the correct mechanism predicts, closes that
ambiguity.

| Criterion | Gaming-resistant because... | Excellent (4) | Adequate (3) | Developing (2) | Insufficient (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Diagnose — name the mechanism, before computing, from specific textual evidence** | The write-up is freshly generated per attempt; a learner cannot pattern-match this module's own worked-example numbers | Correctly identifies the mechanism and cites the specific write-up detail that distinguishes it from the other two templates | Correctly identifies the mechanism but the cited evidence is generic ("the numbers look suspicious") rather than the specific distinguishing detail | Identifies a plausible-sounding but incorrect mechanism, or hedges between two without committing | No diagnosis stated, or the diagnosis contradicts the computation performed in step 2 |
| **Compute — the specific statistic that mechanism requires** | Requires selecting and correctly executing one of three genuinely different calculations (power, correction, RTM formula), not a generic "run a test" | The correct calculation for the diagnosed mechanism is executed correctly, with all inputs (n, alpha, effect size, reliability) correctly read from the write-up | The correct calculation is attempted but has a minor, identifiable arithmetic or input error | The wrong calculation is performed for the diagnosed mechanism (diagnosis and computation don't match), or a major input is fabricated rather than read from the write-up | No computation shown, or the computation is copied from this module's worked examples with the write-up's actual numbers never substituted in |
| **Verdict — real vs. artifact vs. indeterminate, with the number that decides it** | A learner who computed correctly but cannot state what the number implies has not closed the loop the clinic exists to test | Verdict follows directly and explicitly from the step-2 number (e.g., "achieved power was 16%, so this result is likely winner's-curse-inflated, not a reliable estimate of the true effect") | Verdict is directionally correct but the connection to the specific computed number is asserted rather than shown | Verdict does not follow from the computed number, or reverses the direction (e.g., treats a low-power finding as reassuring) | No verdict stated, or the verdict is copy-pasted boilerplate not tied to this scenario's numbers |
| **Fix — proposed before-the-fact, not a patch on the numbers given** | Distinguishes a learner who understands the discipline from one who can only critique after seeing the failure | Proposes a specific, computable fix the original team could have applied *before* running the experiment (a stated required n; a stated correction procedure and its threshold; a stated control-group design) | Proposes a fix in the right category but without a specific number or procedure attached | Proposes a fix that would not actually address the diagnosed mechanism (e.g., "collect more data" for a multiple-comparisons scenario without also correcting for the comparisons) | No fix proposed, or the fix only adjusts the reported number after the fact rather than the experiment's design |

A submission that scores 4 on Compute but 1 on Diagnose does not pass — a flawless power
calculation performed on a scenario whose actual flaw is regression to the mean demonstrates
technical fluency with one formula, not the diagnostic judgment this module exists to certify.

## Elle pacing notes

**The wall: the power-analysis formula itself — z-scores, arcsine transforms, "why does this
formula have this shape."** This is the most common stall in Lesson 1, especially for learners
without a stats background, and it is worth distinguishing from a *conceptual* wall (below).
**Reroute:** do not derive the formula from first principles on a first pass. Have the learner
treat `n = 2·(z_{α/2}+z_β)²/d²` as a tool with four labeled slots, plug in the latency
worked example's numbers by hand once, and confirm the arithmetic lands on 220.75 before asking
"why" at all. Mechanical fluency with the plug-and-compute step, reached first, makes the "why"
land faster on a second pass than trying to build the derivation from scratch under stall
pressure.

**The wall: "the study found significance, why does it matter that it was underpowered — it
still worked, didn't it?"** This is the single most resistant misconception in the whole module,
because unlike Lesson 1's math notation, it is a genuine conceptual gap, not an unfamiliar
formula. **Reroute:** do not re-explain the winner's curse abstractly a second time. Have the
learner run Lesson 4's exact 200,000-replication simulation themselves, with the true effect
fixed at 5pp, and read off the two numbers directly: mean observed lift across *all* replications
(≈5pp, matching the true effect) versus mean observed lift among only the *significant*
replications (≈12.5pp). Seeing the same simulated ground truth produce an honest average and an
inflated "successful-launches-only" average from literally the same data generating process
tends to close this gap in one sitting where a second verbal explanation does not.

**Signal — a learner who, in Lab 3's Predict step, correctly predicts the worst-group's week 2
mean within about 10 seconds of the true simulated value, using only reliability and the
selection fraction.** This is not luck to be second-guessed — it means the learner has
internalized `E[Y|X] = μ + r·(X−μ)` well enough to apply it prospectively, which is the entire
target skill of Lesson 3. Log `struggle-productive` if it took several attempts to get there;
treat a first-attempt success as evidence the learner is ready to move faster through the
Assessment's Template C scenario type.

**The wall: "if peeking is this bad, isn't checking an experiment's progress at all
irresponsible?"** A reasonable-sounding overcorrection. **Reroute:** distinguish *monitoring*
(checking for infrastructure problems, sample ratio mismatches, obviously broken data — never
making a stop/ship decision from it) from *optional stopping* (treating an early significant
p-value as a legitimate reason to stop and declare victory). The fix Lesson 2 points toward is
not "never look at the data before the planned endpoint" — it's "commit the analysis plan,
including any interim-look correction (e.g., a pre-specified alpha-spending function), before
the experiment starts." A learner stuck on this wall usually just needs that distinction named
explicitly once.

**Accelerate condition.** A learner who, in Lab 2's Predict step, correctly derives
`1 − (1−α)^k` from first principles (not from having seen the formula in Lesson 2's teaching
text) — reasoning it out as "P(none significant) = (1−α)^k for k independent nulls, so P(at
least one) is the complement" — has already demonstrated the core reasoning skill Lesson 2
exists to build. Such a learner can move directly from Lab 2 to the Assessment's Template B
scenario without re-deriving the Bonferroni/BH mechanics from the worked example first.

**Struggle triage, condensed:**

| Symptom | Productive (let it run) | Blocked (intervene) |
|---|---|---|
| Fumbling the z-score/formula mechanics in Lesson 1 | Briefly, yes — plug-and-compute fluency comes with repetition | If still unable to reproduce the 220.75 hand-calc after one worked walkthrough |
| Insisting "significant is significant" regardless of power | No — this is the conceptual core of Lesson 4, worth a direct intervention early | Always intervene with the live winner's-curse simulation, don't just re-explain |
| Getting a slightly different Lab 2/3 number than the pinned worked example | Yes, if within the stated tolerance and the learner can explain why (RNG, sim size) | If the learner cannot explain *why* their number differs, even approximately |
| Wanting to "double-check by peeking" during their own Lab 2 Monte Carlo run | This is exactly the temptation the lab is designed to make visible — note it, don't shame it | Never punitive — if it becomes an actual behavior pattern in later real analysis, that's a coaching conversation, not a grading one |

## Three-tier reading — the number that was computed correctly and still lied

**Tier 1 — material ground.** Every number in this module's worked examples is reproducible from
the labs' own seeded code: the 220.75-vs-221.71 hand-vs-exact sample size, the 64.15% family-wise
error rate, the 19–20% peeking-inflated false-positive rate, the 23.5-vs-25.0-second regression-
to-the-mean match, the 2.5× winner's-curse inflation factor. None of these are estimates from a
textbook or a citation to trust secondhand — they are outputs of code you can re-run tonight with
the seeds given, and they will land within the stated tolerance every time, because the
mechanisms producing them (sampling variance, the union bound, conditional selection) are exact
mathematical properties, not empirical tendencies that sometimes hold.

**Tier 2 — what a passing p-value suppresses.** Every technique this module teaches is aimed at
a study that is *statistically* wrong — underpowered, uncorrected, confounded by selection. A
study that clears every bar this module sets — properly powered in advance, one pre-registered
metric, a real control group, a reported confidence interval instead of a bare point estimate —
can still be answering a question chosen because it was likely to produce a flattering number.
Nothing in a power calculation checks whether the *effect size you powered for* was chosen
honestly, or chosen because it was the smallest effect that would still make a good headline once
you knew roughly what the pilot data looked like. Nothing in a multiple-comparisons correction
checks whether the *one pre-registered metric* was picked before or after a quick, informal look
at which metric seemed likely to move. This is the same gap AIE-204-M01 named at the level of
gold sets and metrics — a completely honest number can describe a completely honest answer to
a subtly self-serving question — and it is why the discipline in this module (power, correction,
control groups) is necessary and not sufficient: it guarantees the arithmetic is trustworthy, and
guarantees nothing at all about who chose what to measure, and why.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** You will, at some point, run an experiment that comes back with p =
0.09 — not significant, a real, honestly non-significant result — on a launch that has already
been announced internally, that a team has already been staffed to ship, that someone above you
is already expecting good news from by Friday. Every tool in this module is available to you at
that moment, and every one of them can be pointed either way. You can peek at a later checkpoint
because "the experiment's still running anyway." You can report the best of the six metrics you
happened to also track, and mention the others only if someone asks. You can notice that the
worst-performing segment improved after the launch and write that up as a win, quietly leaving
out that you selected on that segment being worst in the first place. None of these require you
to compute anything incorrectly — every p-value, every interval, every reported metric can be
completely honest arithmetic, run by someone who has read this entire module and agrees with
every formula in it, deployed in service of an answer chosen in advance. The discipline in this
module cannot stop you from doing this. It can only make sure that, in the quiet moment before
you decide which metric to lead with, you already know precisely what you would be doing, and
precisely how to check whether someone else has done it to you. Decide now, before the Friday
deadline exists, what number you are not willing to produce.

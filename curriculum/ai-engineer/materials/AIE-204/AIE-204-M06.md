# AIE-204-M06 — Error analysis as a practice: reading transcripts, taxonomy building

**Course:** AIE-204 — Evaluation and Experimentation · **Tier:** core · **Durability:** durable
**Hours:** 16 · **Sits in the descent:** the module where "read the failures yourself" stops being
something you do before you have a real eval and becomes something you do *because* you have one.
AIE-204-M04 built and validated a judge; this module is what you do with a judge once it has told
you it's wrong about something, but not yet *what*, exactly, or *how much*. The output here is not
a score. It is a taxonomy — a small, defensible set of named failure categories, each with a
written definition tight enough that a second reader, given nothing but that definition, sorts the
same batch of transcripts the same way.
**Prereq:** AIE-204-M02 (gold sets and inter-annotator agreement — this module reuses `cohens_kappa`
**verbatim**, unweighted, because a taxonomy's categories are nominal, not ordinal: there is no
natural "distance" between `fabricated_consequence` and `buried_lede` the way there is between a
judge score of 2 and a judge score of 4, so M04's *quadratic-weighted* kappa does not apply here —
plain Cohen's kappa, M02's original formula, is the correct tool). AIE-204-M04 (evaluating
generative systems, LLM-as-judge) — this module's batch is the flawed ticket-resolution summarizer
M04 built a judge for, at the scale a validated-but-imperfect judge actually operates at. AIE-204-M05
(contamination, Goodharting) is not load-bearing for this module's own labs, but its central move —
an aggregate number hides the real story, go look underneath it — is the exact move Lesson 4 makes
about a taxonomy's raw frequency count.
**Feeds forward:** AIE-204-M07 (the eval report) writes up a shortfall-driver analysis exactly like
Lab M06-C's for a reader who did not build the taxonomy and has every reason to distrust it on sight.

> **Toolchain for this module.** Python 3.12+, `pytest`. No model is called and nothing is trained —
> the batch of transcripts is fixed, provided data, exactly like every gold set and label set in this
> course. `cohens_kappa` and `confusion_matrix` are hand-rolled, same discipline as M02 and M04: no
> `statsmodels`/`sklearn` call inside a function you submit. `error_batch.py` is the one shared,
> do-not-edit data file all three labs import from — the transcripts, their raw open-coding tags,
> two independent second-reader labelings (one per taxonomy version), and the scores each item
> already received from the M04-style judge that flagged it for human review in the first place.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Why the taxonomy has to come from the data

A **closed-coding** task hands you the categories up front and asks you to sort items into them —
that's what M02's gold-set labeling was (`high`/`medium`/`low`, defined before a single ticket was
read) and what M04's judge rubric was (`faithful`/`obvious_hallucination`/`subtle_hallucination`/
`minor_omission`/`borderline`, also defined before the judge ever ran). Error analysis, done right,
is different: it is **open coding** — read the failures first, in volume, and let the categories
emerge from what's actually there, because you don't yet know what's there. Naming categories before
reading enough data is not efficiency, it's guessing with extra steps, and every item read afterward
gets silently force-fit into whichever guessed bucket is closest instead of genuinely reconsidered.

**The running batch for this module.** AIE-204-M04's ticket-resolution summarizer shipped behind a
validated judge — weighted kappa 0.84 against a human-reconciled gold set, "almost perfect" on the
standard scale. Twenty-four of its outputs were pulled for a routine human spot check anyway, because
a metric this course has spent five modules building is not a metric this course trusts blindly. All
twenty-four were flagged as unacceptable by the human reviewer. None of them arrive pre-sorted into
M04's five judge categories — that sorting is this module's job, and doing it is the whole point.

**Worked example — why reusing M04's categories fails on contact with this batch.** Two items from
the batch, read against M04's own rubric:

> **Item DR03** — *Ticket:* "The billing webhook silently dropped events for one integration partner
> for two days; replays have been sent and reconciliation is complete." *Summary:* "The billing
> webhook silently dropped events for one integration partner for two days."
>
> **Item BL03** — *Ticket:* "Login was broken for every customer for 5 minutes after a bad
> certificate rotation; the previous certificate was restored." *Summary:* "A certificate rotation
> was performed, and after review of the deployment and a brief period of monitoring, it was
> determined that login had, in fact, been broken for every customer for 5 minutes, and the previous
> certificate was subsequently restored."

DR03 could be filed under M04's `minor_omission` — but M04 defined that category around a single
undifferentiated fact count ("two of three key facts present"), not around whether a manager reading
the dashboard can tell if the issue is **still open**. DR03 doesn't just omit a fact; it omits the
one fact — is this fixed? — the summary exists to answer. BL03 is worse for the taxonomy: it doesn't
map to *any* of M04's three rubric criteria. Every fact is present (faithful: 5/5), nothing is
missing (complete: 5/5), and the sentence isn't padded with irrelevant content (concise: arguably
4/5) — the failure is that the one fact that matters, a full outage, is buried behind a procedural
narrative about the rotation and the monitoring. M04's rubric was built to catch fabrication and
omission. It was never built to look for *order*, so it cannot see BL03 failing at all.

**Misconception 1 — "I'll just reuse M04's five categories; they worked there."** A category set
built for one rubric's criteria has blind spots shaped exactly like that rubric — not by accident,
by construction. BL03's failure is invisible to a faithfulness/completeness/concision lens no matter
how carefully it's applied, because none of those three axes is "is the critical fact first." A
taxonomy handed down from a different task's rubric will silently miss whatever that rubric wasn't
built to see, and you will not find out it missed something until a human, not the judge, catches it
— which is exactly how this batch arrived.

**Misconception 2 — "I'll name categories after the first few examples and refine as I go."** This
is **premature categorization**, and it's a specific, well-documented failure of open coding: the
first two or three names you write down anchor everything that follows. Item five doesn't get
compared fresh against the data — it gets compared against your guess from item two, and if it's
*close enough*, it gets force-fit rather than genuinely reconsidered. The fix is procedural, not
willpower: read a substantial minimum of the batch — all of it, for a batch this size — and write a
short, free-text description of *what's specifically wrong* for every single item, before naming a
single category. Lab M06-A's `SYMPTOM_TAGS` is exactly that: 23 distinct raw descriptions, one per
flawed item, written before any grouping happened.

### Lesson 2 — Open coding, then axial coding: building a taxonomy a stranger could apply

**Open coding** produces one raw, specific description per item — not a category, a note. **Axial
coding** is the second, separate pass: cluster those raw notes into a small number of named
categories, each with a real operational definition (not a label, a *rule* a stranger could apply),
a positive example, and — critically — a negative example at the boundary, because most taxonomy
failures live at boundaries, not centers.

**Worked example — a first taxonomy pass (V1), and where it breaks.** A first read of the batch's 23
distinct open codes groups them fast into four broad buckets:

| V1 category | Definition (first draft) | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| `HALLUCINATION` | The summary states something the ticket doesn't say. | Invented consequences, invented causes |
| `FACTUAL_MISMATCH` | The summary gets a fact about what happened wrong. | Wrong scope/entity, missing resolution status |
| `STRUCTURE` | The summary's content is accurate but poorly organized. | Buried critical facts |
| `CORRECT` | No real error. | — |

This looks reasonable and it is not tight enough. `FACTUAL_MISMATCH` conflates two failures with
genuinely different downstream fixes: a summary that says "all Starter customers" when the ticket
says "two customers" needs a **scope-extraction** fix; a summary that never says whether the issue
was resolved needs a **completeness-of-outcome** fix. A prompt change that fixes one does nothing for
the other, and a taxonomy that can't tell them apart can't tell you which fix to build. Two boundary
items make this concrete:

> **FM02** (`cname_misconfig_cause_not_stated`) — the summary invents a specific DNS misconfiguration
> the ticket never mentions. Read charitably, that's a "reasonable inference," not a fabrication —
> **or is it hallucination?** A first-draft `HALLUCINATION` definition without a sharp rule for
> "invented mechanism vs. invented consequence" leaves this genuinely ambiguous.
>
> **DR03** (`replay_and_reconciliation_omitted`) — the summary is missing the resolution. Is that
> `FACTUAL_MISMATCH` (a fact about what happened is wrong) or `STRUCTURE` (the resolution technically
> could be read as "not yet organized into the summary")? Under V1's definitions, either reading is
> defensible, which is exactly the problem.

Lab M06-A applies V1 to the full batch and hands it to an independent second reader with nothing but
the table above. Lesson 3 shows what that produces. **V2 fixes it** by splitting each vague category
along its actual fix boundary and giving every category a sharp operational rule:

| V2 category | Operational rule | Positive example | Negative example (boundary) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fabricated_consequence` | Invents a *downstream consequence* (legal, contractual, financial) absent from the ticket. | "the customer has escalated to legal" (ticket never says this) | An invented *cause*, not a consequence — see `fabricated_mechanism` |
| `fabricated_mechanism` | Invents a *specific technical root cause* absent from the ticket. | "a misconfigured CNAME record" (ticket says only "stopped resolving") | A vague inference ("a bug caused it") with no specific invented mechanism is not this category |
| `wrong_entity_or_scope` | States the wrong *who* or *how many* were affected. | "all Starter-plan customers" (ticket: two customers) | The right entity, described imprecisely, is not this category |
| `dropped_resolution` | Omits whether/how the issue was **resolved**, leaving a reader unable to tell if it's still open. | Omits "the credential was rotated and backups are confirmed running" | Omitting a non-resolution fact (e.g. exact duration) is a different, lower-stakes omission not covered here |
| `buried_lede` | Every fact is present, but the single most critical fact (severity, scope, or resolution) is not the first thing stated. | The 12-minute full outage appears only as the final clause of a long process narrative | A summary that's merely long, with no critical fact displaced, is a concision problem, not this category |
| `correct` | None of the above. | — | — |

Every ambiguous case from V1 now has a rule to resolve it: FM02 is `fabricated_mechanism` because it
invents a specific mechanism (a CNAME record), not a consequence. DR03 is `dropped_resolution`,
specifically, because the omitted fact is the resolution status, not because it's disorganized.

**Misconception 1 — "If two failure types always show up in similar-looking items, they're the same
category."** `wrong_entity_or_scope` and `dropped_resolution` both read, at a glance, as "the summary
got a fact wrong relative to the source" — the same instinct that produced V1's `FACTUAL_MISMATCH`.
They are not the same category, because they don't have the same fix: one needs the generator to be
more conservative about quantifiers ("two" vs. "all"), the other needs it to never omit resolution
status. Merge them and you can measure "how often are facts wrong" but you can't act on the number —
you'd still have to re-read every flagged item to find out which fix to build.

**Misconception 2 — "An `OTHER` bucket is a reasonable safety valve for edge cases."** V1's table
above has no `OTHER` row, on purpose — its second reader invented one anyway, for exactly the items
whose boundary the definitions left undecided. An `OTHER` bucket that a second, independent reader
reaches for on a meaningful share of a batch is not evidence the data contains genuine edge cases —
it's a direct measurement of which category boundaries are still vague. The fix is never "keep
`OTHER` around as permanent overflow"; it's "find out what's landing there and either split a
category or sharpen its rule until nothing does," which is exactly what V1 → V2 above just did.

### Lesson 3 — Second-reader reliability: M02's kappa, applied to a taxonomy instead of a label set

A taxonomy that only its author can apply consistently has learned that author's idiosyncrasies, not
produced a categorization a stranger could replicate — this is M02 Lesson 2's whole argument
("agreement measured, not assumed"), one level up: applied to *which bucket an item goes in* instead
of *which of three fixed labels an item gets*. The tool doesn't change. **Cohen's kappa, unweighted,
exactly as M02 built it**, corrects observed agreement for the agreement you'd expect from each
reader's own marginal distribution by chance:

```
κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)

p_o = observed agreement = (# items both readers assigned the same category) / N
p_e = expected agreement by chance = Σ_category  P(reader A → category) · P(reader B → category)
```

**Why not M04's quadratic-weighted kappa?** M04's judge scores were **ordinal** — 1 through 5, where
being off by one point is a smaller error than being off by four, and the weighting exists precisely
to give partial credit for near misses. Taxonomy categories are **nominal** — there is no meaningful
sense in which `fabricated_consequence` is "closer to" `wrong_entity_or_scope` than it is to
`buried_lede`. Applying a distance-based weight to an unordered set of labels doesn't add nuance, it
adds an arbitrary number: whatever order the categories happen to be listed in would silently change
the result. Unweighted kappa — M02's original formula — is the correct tool for a taxonomy, full
stop, and this is the reason, not a simplification.

**Re-ascend check (optional, not graded) — recall M02 Lesson 2's toy example.** Twenty items, three
labels, `p_o = 0.80`, `p_e = 0.44`, `κ = 0.6429` — "substantial" on the Landis & Koch scale. Lab
M06-A's `test_cohens_kappa_matches_m02_toy_example` recasts that exact confusion matrix as
`item_id → label` dicts and checks your `cohens_kappa` reproduces 0.6429 before trusting it on this
module's own taxonomy. If your implementation doesn't reproduce M02's number on M02's own data, it
is not ready to certify a taxonomy no one has checked before.

**Worked example — V1's second reader, measured, not assumed.** An independent second reader applies
V1's table from Lesson 2 to the same 24-item batch, having read only the four category definitions —
never the first reader's reasoning. Confusion matrix (rows = canonical/first reader, columns = second
reader; `OTHER` is a category the second reader introduced on their own, not one either taxonomy
offered):

| A ＼ B | HALLUCINATION | FACTUAL_MISMATCH | STRUCTURE | CORRECT | OTHER | row total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **HALLUCINATION** | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 5 |
| **FACTUAL_MISMATCH** | 0 | 8 | 1 | 0 | 2 | 11 |
| **STRUCTURE** | 0 | 1 | 5 | 0 | 0 | 6 |
| **CORRECT** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| **col total** | 3 | 10 | 6 | 1 | 4 | 24 |

- `p_o` = (3 + 8 + 5 + 1) / 24 = **0.7083**
- `p_e` ≈ **0.2830** (each category's row-share times its column-share, summed)
- `κ` = (0.7083 − 0.2830) / (1 − 0.2830) = **0.5932** — "**moderate**" on the Landis & Koch scale,
  and the four items in the `OTHER` column are exactly the items Lesson 2 named as boundary cases:
  `FM02`, `DR01`, `WS02`, and one item the second reader flagged out of caution despite it having no
  real error at all (`CO02`).

**Worked example — V2's second reader, after the Lesson 2 revision.** Same batch, same second-reader
process, applied to the six-category V2 table instead:

- `p_o` = **0.9167**, `p_e` ≈ **0.2049**, `κ` = **0.8952** — "**almost perfect**," clearing M04's own
  judge-validation bar (≥0.81) on a task that started at "moderate." The only two disagreements left
  (both readers agree on 22 of 24 items) are `FM02` and `DR03` — exactly the two items Lesson 2
  identified as the closest calls even under V2's sharper rules. `OTHER` was used **zero times** by
  the second reader on V2, not because it was told to stop using it, but because V2's six categories
  are collectively specific enough that every real disagreement lands on a *named neighbor* instead
  of a dustbin.

**Misconception 1 — "The taxonomy is done once I can apply it to every item myself."** Self-consistency
is not inter-rater reliability. You wrote the categories; of course you can apply them the way you
meant them. The only measurement that certifies a taxonomy is whether someone who has never seen your
reasoning — only your written definitions — sorts the batch the same way.

**Misconception 2 — "κ = 0.59 is basically fine; ship the taxonomy."** 0.59 is "moderate," meaningfully
below the "substantial" (0.61+) floor M02 set as the realistic target for a task with real judgment
calls, and well below the "almost perfect" bar M04 required of its judge. Unlike M02 Lesson 2's
caution that *not every* task can reach near-1.0 agreement — some ambiguity is genuinely inherent —
this specific gap is not inherent ambiguity. It is a diagnosable taxonomy defect (four items with no
real category, concentrated in exactly the two categories Lesson 2 flagged as too broad), and the fix
(split the category, sharpen the rule) is the same procedural move as M02 Lesson 3's "an edge case with
no clear answer needs a written reason, not a shrug" — applied to a category boundary instead of a
single label.

### Lesson 4 — Quantifying which categories actually drive the shortfall

A frequency table tells you what happens often. It does not tell you what matters, because it has no
sense of how *bad* each occurrence is — treating a category that happens often but barely moves the
score the same as a category that happens rarely but is catastrophic every time it does.
**Shortfall contribution** fixes this by weighting frequency against severity:

```
contribution(category) = frequency_fraction(category) × (target_score − mean_score(category))

frequency_fraction(category) = (# items in category) / (total items)
```

Summed across every category, contributions add up to exactly the batch's overall shortfall from
target — a clean decomposition, not a separate derived number that could quietly disagree with the
aggregate.

**Worked example — the batch's full breakdown.** Target score 5.0 (the top of the same 1–5 scale M04
used); overall batch mean 3.5833; overall shortfall **1.4167**:

| Category | n | Frequency | Mean score | Deficit | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `wrong_entity_or_scope` | 6 | 25.0% | 3.50 | 1.50 | **0.375** |
| `fabricated_mechanism` | 3 | 12.5% | 2.33 | 2.67 | **0.333** |
| `fabricated_consequence` | 2 | 8.3% | 1.50 | 3.50 | **0.292** |
| `dropped_resolution` | 5 | 20.8% | 3.80 | 1.20 | **0.250** |
| `buried_lede` | 6 | 25.0% | 4.33 | 0.67 | **0.167** |
| `correct` | 2 | 8.3% | 5.00 | 0.00 | **0.000** |
| **Sum** | 24 | 100% | — | — | **1.417** ✓ matches the overall shortfall |

`wrong_entity_or_scope` and `buried_lede` are **tied** for the most frequent category in the batch —
six items each, 25% apiece. A backlog sorted by raw frequency alone would call them equally urgent.
They are not: `wrong_entity_or_scope` drives more than **twice** `buried_lede`'s contribution to the
shortfall (0.375 vs. 0.167), because being wrong about *who* was affected costs far more score per
occurrence than a fact merely being *organized* poorly. And `fabricated_consequence` — the single
**rarest** category in the entire batch, occurring in under 1 item in 10 — drives nearly **twice**
`buried_lede`'s contribution despite happening a third as often, because its per-item deficit (3.50)
is the largest in the taxonomy: every time it happens, it happens badly.

**Misconception 1 — "Fix whichever category shows up most in the batch."** Sorting the backlog by
raw count alone sends engineering effort at `wrong_entity_or_scope`-*or*-`buried_lede` with equal
urgency, when the actual return on a fix is more than double for one of them. Frequency tells you
where to *look first for volume*; it does not tell you where the score is actually being lost.

**Misconception 2 — "A category this rare can be deprioritized."** `fabricated_consequence` is the
easiest category in the taxonomy to argue isn't worth engineering time — two items out of twenty-four,
easy to dismiss as noise. It is nearly as damaging in total contribution as `fabricated_mechanism`,
which occurs 1.5× as often, and its per-item deficit is the single worst number in the table. Rare-and-
severe categories are exactly the ones a frequency-sorted backlog buries at the bottom, not because
anyone decided they didn't matter, but because nobody looked at severity at all.

**Misconception 3 — "The contribution table replaces the frequency table."** Report both, side by
side, the same way M04 Lesson 4 insisted the aggregate weighted kappa never be reported without the
per-category breakdown underneath it. A single derived "contribution" number, presented alone, invites
exactly the same "the aggregate looks safe" trap this course keeps naming at every level — the fix
here isn't a smarter single number, it's refusing to collapse two numbers that answer different
questions into one that answers neither.

```python
def cohens_kappa(rater_a, rater_b, labels):
    assert set(rater_a.keys()) == set(rater_b.keys()), "raters must cover the same items"
    ids = sorted(rater_a.keys())
    n = len(ids)
    idx = {l: i for i, l in enumerate(labels)}
    N = len(labels)
    O = [[0] * N for _ in range(N)]
    for item_id in ids:
        O[idx[rater_a[item_id]]][idx[rater_b[item_id]]] += 1
    po = sum(O[k][k] for k in range(N)) / n
    row = [sum(O[i]) for i in range(N)]
    col = [sum(O[i][j] for i in range(N)) for j in range(N)]
    pe = sum(row[i] * col[i] for i in range(N)) / (n * n)
    kappa = (po - pe) / (1 - pe) if pe != 1 else 1.0
    return po, pe, kappa


def confusion_matrix(rater_a, rater_b, labels):
    assert set(rater_a.keys()) == set(rater_b.keys())
    idx = {l: i for i, l in enumerate(labels)}
    N = len(labels)
    M = [[0] * N for _ in range(N)]
    for item_id in rater_a:
        M[idx[rater_a[item_id]]][idx[rater_b[item_id]]] += 1
    return M
```

```python
"""Lab M06-C -- reference solution."""
from __future__ import annotations

from error_batch import SYMPTOM_TAGS, SCORES
from lab_a_open_coding import label_batch
from lab_b_taxonomy_revision import TAXONOMY_V2, V2_LABELS

TARGET_SCORE = 5.0

# Recorded before any of the functions below were run: wrong_entity_or_scope
# and buried_lede tie on raw count, but a scope error changes what a reader
# BELIEVES happened (who, how many) while a buried lede only changes how
# easily they find a fact that's actually present -- the first should cost
# more score per item, so the tie on frequency should NOT hold on
# contribution. wrong_entity_or_scope is predicted as the top contributor
# overall.
PREDICTED_TOP_CONTRIBUTOR = "wrong_entity_or_scope"
PREDICTED_TIED_FREQUENCY_MEANS_TIED_CONTRIBUTION = False


def category_frequency(labels):
    n = len(labels)
    counts: dict[str, int] = {}
    for cat in labels.values():
        counts[cat] = counts.get(cat, 0) + 1
    return {cat: count / n for cat, count in counts.items()}


def mean_score_by_category(labels, scores):
    totals: dict[str, list[int]] = {}
    for item_id, cat in labels.items():
        totals.setdefault(cat, []).append(scores[item_id])
    return {cat: sum(vals) / len(vals) for cat, vals in totals.items()}


def shortfall_contribution(labels, scores, target=TARGET_SCORE):
    freq = category_frequency(labels)
    means = mean_score_by_category(labels, scores)
    return {cat: freq[cat] * (target - means[cat]) for cat in freq}


def top_contributor(labels, scores, target=TARGET_SCORE):
    contrib = shortfall_contribution(labels, scores, target)
    return max(contrib, key=contrib.get)


def most_frequent_categories(labels):
    freq_counts: dict[str, int] = {}
    for cat in labels.values():
        freq_counts[cat] = freq_counts.get(cat, 0) + 1
    max_count = max(freq_counts.values())
    return sorted(cat for cat, count in freq_counts.items() if count == max_count)


SHORTFALL_NOTE = (
    "wrong_entity_or_scope is the batch's top contributor to the overall shortfall (0.375 of "
    "1.417), even though it is only tied -- not ahead -- on raw frequency with buried_lede (6 "
    "items each, 25% of the batch). buried_lede's contribution is less than half of "
    "wrong_entity_or_scope's (0.167), because every fact in a buried-lede summary is still "
    "correct; the reader only has to read further to find it. A wrong-entity-or-scope summary "
    "actively tells the reader something false about who or how many were affected, which costs "
    "more score per occurrence (a 1.50-point deficit vs. 0.67) even at the same frequency. "
    "Sorting the backlog by raw item count alone would have called these two categories equally "
    "urgent; they are not."
)
```

### Module task — the Error Taxonomy Audit

Handed to the learner, unseen: a batch of ~18 real, unsorted outputs (`open_codes` provided, no
category names, no template) from a **different** flawed generative system — an AI-drafted
customer-facing incident-status update generator — plus a second reader's independent labels for two
successive taxonomy attempts, and each item's already-measured score against a fixed target. The
learner must, in order:

1. **Predict**, before reading the full batch's open codes: how many distinct top-level categories
   they expect to need, and which single category they expect will contribute most to the shortfall
   — written down before any grouping or computation.
2. **Build a first taxonomy from the data**, with a real operational definition, a positive example,
   and a negative/boundary example for every category — not a copy of this module's six categories or
   AIE-204-M04's five, applied wholesale to a system neither was built for.
3. **Measure second-reader reliability** with a hand-rolled, unweighted `cohens_kappa` against the
   provided second reader. If κ does not clear 0.61 ("substantial"), **revise the taxonomy** — split
   or sharpen at least one category boundary — and re-measure against the provided second-attempt
   second-reader labels until it does.
4. **Quantify shortfall drivers**: report both a frequency table and a severity-weighted contribution
   table, identify the top contributor, and write one paragraph explaining why frequency alone would
   have picked the wrong priority (if it would have) or confirming it wouldn't have (if it happens not
   to, with the reasoning that shows this was checked, not assumed).
5. **Decide, in writing**: which single category should be fixed first, why, and what additional
   evidence — a different batch, a larger sample, a specific counterexample — would change that
   decision.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module — building and validating an error taxonomy from real failures — not
boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Structured error analysis — reading a real batch of failures, coding
  them, and reporting a category breakdown rather than a single rate — is documented, established
  practice in incident postmortems and in the (much newer, much less standardized) discipline of LLM
  system evaluation. A taxonomy's value is inseparable from whether a second person can apply it the
  same way; this module's Lab M06-A/B pair is a hand-built instance of the exact reliability check that
  distinguishes a real taxonomy from a plausible-sounding list of category names.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** A reported "hallucination rate: 4%" or "error rate: 8%" is,
  in practice, frequently the *only* number a team publishes about its own system's failures. The
  category breakdown underneath it — which specific failures make up that 4%, and whether a second
  reader would sort them the same way — is almost never disclosed alongside the headline rate. Without
  it, a reader cannot tell a taxonomy that was actually checked for reliability from one that was
  invented by a single person under deadline pressure and never tested against anyone else's reading of
  the same data — the number looks identical either way.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your six-category taxonomy is real: it clears κ = 0.90 against an
  independent second reader, and its shortfall analysis shows `wrong_entity_or_scope` — a category that
  actively misstates who or how many customers were affected — as the largest driver of the metric's
  gap from target. Leadership wants the taxonomy compressed to three categories for a slide, and the
  natural compression collapses `fabricated_consequence` (the rarest category, and the most severe per
  occurrence) into the same bucket as `buried_lede` (the most common, least severe) under one label:
  "quality issues." The resulting number will look smaller and vaguer than either category told on its
  own. What is the smallest, most honest version of that slide you could actually defend — and are you
  the one who insists it survives contact with the room asking for a smaller number, knowing the
  three-category version isn't a lie, just a compression that happens to make the worst failure the
  least visible one?

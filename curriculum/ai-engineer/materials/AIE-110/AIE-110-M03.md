**Course:** AIE-110 — Data Structures, Algorithms, and Scale · **Tier:** foundation · **Durability:** durable
**Hours:** 14 · **Sits in the descent:** the first module where the driving *operation* — **top-k**, "give me the k best out of N" — is the thing retrieval and ranking actually run millions of times a day, and we descend beneath `sorted(scores, reverse=True)[:k]` to the structure that makes it affordable at 10^9. We build a binary heap by hand (an array wearing a tree costume), implement top-k **two ways**, and predict — before measuring — which one wins at large N and small k, and *why the answer changes* when you swap pure Python for a C-accelerated call or push N past the memory wall.
**Prereq:** AIE-101 (Python + pytest fluent). **Co-req:** AIE-102 (the memory hierarchy) — this module cashes in three AIE-102/M01 results: (1) the **interpreter tax** (a pure-Python operation costs ~30–100× a C-level one), which decides the top-k crossover; (2) **cache-friendly contiguous layout** beats pointer-chasing, which is *why* the heap is array-backed and not a node-and-pointer tree; (3) the **memory wall** (M01, Lesson 4) — a structure falls over at the smaller of its time and memory walls — which is what ultimately decides top-k at 10^9.

> **Design note for the runtime.** M02 built the hash table (O(1) lookup, unordered). M03 builds the two structures you reach for when you care about *order* or *the extremes*: the **heap** (the k best, cheaply, streaming) and the **balanced tree** (everything in sorted order, range queries). The module is deliberately organized around **top-k as the load-bearing problem**, because top-k is not a toy — it is the last step of every retrieval pipeline (rank N candidate documents, return the k nearest), every recommender, every "show me the highest-scoring" query. Getting top-k wrong is not an academic error; it is the difference between a RAG system that answers in 40 ms and one that holds a billion floats in RAM and dies. Every worked constant below was measured on the authoring machine (CPython 3.12, x86-64, ~3 GHz, 16 GB); learners **re-measure on their own hardware** — the constants move, the *shapes and the crossovers* do not.

## Lessons

Four lessons. The first builds the heap and shows it is an array, not a tree of pointers. The second turns the heap into the top-k weapon and proves heapify is O(N), then races two top-k algorithms. The third steps up to balanced trees and answers the question the heap *can't* — ordered queries — and hands you a decision rule for which structure a problem actually needs. The fourth takes top-k to scale, where the winner is decided not by Big-O but by the memory wall and the interpreter tax together.

1. **The heap is an array wearing a tree costume** — implicit binary heap, index arithmetic, sift-up / sift-down, push/pop in O(log N), and why contiguous beats pointers.
2. **Top-k, the operation ranking actually needs** — heapify in O(N) (proved), then the two top-k algorithms — sort-then-slice O(N log N) vs bounded min-heap O(N log k) — and where they cross.
3. **When you need order, not just the extremes** — the BST, its O(N) degeneration, why balance buys O(log N) height, and a decision rule: heap vs balanced tree vs sorted array vs quickselect.
4. **Top-k at scale** — streaming (never hold N), the interpreter tax that lets C-`sorted` beat a pure-Python heap, and the memory wall that ultimately makes the heap the only survivor at 10^9.

### Lesson 1 — The heap is an array wearing a tree costume

#### Teaching text

A **binary heap** is usually drawn as a tree: a root on top, two children below each node, the whole thing obeying one rule — the **heap property**: every parent is ≤ both its children (a *min-heap*; flip the comparison for a max-heap). The root is therefore the minimum of the entire collection, reachable in O(1). That picture is correct but it lies about the memory. A heap is not stored as nodes-with-pointers. It is stored as a **flat array**, and the tree structure is *implicit in the indices*. This is the whole trick and the reason the heap is fast.

Number the tree positions in breadth-first order starting at 0: the root is index 0, its children are 1 and 2, their children are 3, 4, 5, 6, and so on. Given that numbering, you never store a single pointer — you *compute* family relationships with arithmetic:

```
parent(i) = (i - 1) // 2
left(i)   = 2*i + 1
right(i)  = 2*i + 2
```

That is the entire data structure: a Python list, plus three integer formulas. There is no `Node` class, no `.left`/`.right` attribute, no allocation per element. AIE-102 told you why this matters and it matters *here*: a pointer-based tree scatters its nodes across the heap (the memory heap — unrelated to this data structure, an unfortunate name collision), so walking it *pointer-chases* — each step is a cache miss, ~100 ns of stall waiting on RAM. The array-backed heap stores its elements **contiguously**; a sift walks `i → 2*i+1 → …`, indices that sit near each other in one cache line. Same Big-O, wildly different constant. The heap is fast not only because it is O(log N) but because it is O(log N) *with a cache-friendly layout* — the two AIE-102 lessons compounding.

Two operations maintain the heap property, and both walk a single root-to-leaf path, so both are **O(log N)** — the height of the tree.

**Sift-up** (used by `push`): you append the new element at the end of the array (the next open leaf) and it may be smaller than its parent, violating the property. So you *bubble it up*: while it is smaller than its parent, swap them, and repeat. It rises until its parent is smaller or it becomes the root.

**Sift-down** (used by `pop`): to remove the minimum you can't just delete index 0 — that would orphan the tree. Instead you move the *last* element into the root slot (keeping the array dense), then *bubble it down*: repeatedly swap it with its **smaller** child until both children are larger or it hits a leaf. (Swapping with the smaller child is not optional — swap with the larger and you'd install a value bigger than its sibling as the parent, breaking the property immediately.)

Because both operations touch one path from root to leaf, and a binary tree of N nodes has height ⌊log₂ N⌋, `push` and `pop` are **O(log N)**. `peek` (read the min) is **O(1)** — it's just `array[0]`. That is the heap's entire value proposition: the single smallest (or largest) element, always available in O(1), with O(log N) to insert or remove it — and *nothing else*. It cannot search, it cannot iterate in sorted order without destroying itself. It does one job. Lesson 3 is about knowing when that one job is exactly what you need.

#### Worked example — pop the min from a 7-element heap

Start with this valid min-heap (drawn as a tree, stored as the array below it):

```
            1
          /   \
         3     2
        / \   / \
       7  5  4   6

array:  [1, 3, 2, 7, 5, 4, 6]   (indices 0..6)
```

Check the property at index 0: `h[0]=1 ≤ h[1]=3` and `1 ≤ h[2]=2`. Good. `pop()` must return 1 and leave a valid heap.

1. **Save the min:** `top = h[0] = 1`.
2. **Move the last element to the root:** the last element is `h[6]=6`. Overwrite the root, drop the tail. Array becomes `[6, 3, 2, 7, 5, 4]`.
3. **Sift-down from index 0.** Node is `6`. Children are `h[1]=3` and `h[2]=2`. The *smaller* child is `2` (index 2), and `6 > 2`, so swap → `[2, 3, 6, 7, 5, 4]`, now at index 2.
4. **Continue from index 2.** Node is `6`. Its children are `left = 2*2+1 = 5` → `h[5]=4`; `right = 6` → out of bounds. Smaller child is `4`, and `6 > 4`, so swap → `[2, 3, 4, 7, 5, 6]`, now at index 5.
5. **Index 5 is a leaf** (`2*5+1 = 11 ≥ 6`). Stop.

Result array `[2, 3, 4, 7, 5, 6]` — check the root: `2 ≤ 3` and `2 ≤ 4`. Valid. We returned `1` and did exactly **2 swaps**, matching the tree height ⌊log₂ 6⌋ = 2. That is the O(log N) in action: 6 elements, at most 2 swaps to pop. At a million elements it would be at most 19 swaps. At a billion, 29. *That* is why the extreme is cheap at any scale.

#### Misconceptions to surface (name them out loud)

- **"A heap is a sorted array."** No — a heap is *far* weaker than sorted, and that weakness is the point. `[1, 3, 2, 7, 5, 4, 6]` is a valid heap and is not sorted. The heap guarantees only that the *root* is the minimum; siblings are in no order at all (`3` and `2` sit side by side out of order). A sorted array costs O(N log N) to build and O(N) to insert into; a heap costs O(N) to build and O(log N) to insert. You buy the cheaper structure precisely because you *don't* need full order — you need the extreme. Surface it by asking the learner to point at two out-of-order siblings in a valid heap.
- **"The tree is made of node objects and pointers."** No — it's one flat array and three index formulas; there are zero pointers and zero per-node allocations. Surface it by having the learner write `left(i)` and `parent(i)` and confirm `parent(left(i)) == i`. The "tree" is a way of *talking* about the array, not a thing in memory — which is exactly why it's cache-friendly.
- **"Sift-down can swap with either child."** No — it must swap with the *smaller* child (for a min-heap). Swap `6` with the larger child and you place `6` above a smaller sibling, re-breaking the property one level down. Surface it by walking the worked example but swapping with the larger child at step 3 and watching the heap property fail.

### Lesson 2 — Top-k, the operation ranking actually needs

#### Teaching text

Here is the operation that runs at the end of every retrieval and ranking pipeline: you have **N** scored candidates and you want the **k** best — the 10 nearest documents, the 50 highest-ranked results, the top 5 recommendations. N is huge (millions of candidates); k is tiny (10, 50, 100). **Top-k**, not "sort everything," is what the system actually needs, and the gap between those two framings is the whole lesson. You will implement it two ways, and the naive way is quietly wasteful.

**Way 1 — sort-then-slice.** `sorted(scores, reverse=True)[:k]`. Sort all N, take the first k. Correct, one line, and what everyone writes first. Cost: **O(N log N)** time — you fully ordered a million things to keep ten — and **O(N)** extra memory for the sorted copy. You did N log N work when you only wanted the top k; the other N−k results you sorted and threw away.

**Way 2 — the bounded heap.** Keep a **min-heap of size k** holding the k largest seen *so far*. Walk the N items once. If the heap has fewer than k, push. Otherwise, compare the incoming item to `heap[0]` — the *smallest of the current top-k*. If the newcomer is larger, it belongs in the top-k, so evict the smallest (pop) and insert the newcomer (push); if it's not, discard it in O(1). At the end the heap holds exactly the k largest. Cost: **O(N log k)** time — one pass, each step at most a push/pop on a heap of size *k*, not N — and **O(k)** memory. You never sort the losers and you never hold more than k of them.

Compare the classes for fixed k as N grows: `N log k` vs `N log N`. The heap replaces the `log N` factor with `log k`, and since k ≪ N, `log k` is a small *constant* (k = 10 → log₂ k ≈ 3.3) while `log N` grows (N = 10^9 → log₂ N ≈ 30). Asymptotically the bounded heap wins by a factor of `log N / log k` — nearly **9×** fewer comparisons at N = 10^9, k = 10 — and it does so holding **O(k)** memory instead of **O(N)**. On paper it is not close.

But "on paper" is Lesson 2 of M01 knocking: the **constant is the exchange rate**, and here it flips the answer. `sorted` is **C-level Timsort** — its constant is tiny. A bounded heap written in *pure Python* pays the interpreter tax (30–100× per operation) on every push and pop. So at moderate N the asymptotically-*worse* C sort can *beat* the asymptotically-*better* Python heap, because `log N / log k ≈ 9×` fewer operations doesn't cover a `~50×` per-operation penalty. The heap wins the exponent and loses the wall clock — until either N grows enough that 9× beats 50× (a very large N), or you stop paying the tax by calling the C-accelerated heap (`heapq.nlargest`, which *is* the bounded heap in C and *does* beat `sorted`). **Predicting this reversal — asymptotically better yet slower, and knowing which lever un-reverses it — is the graded outcome of Lab M03-B.**

Before that, one more thing the heap needs: a fast way to *build* itself. You can build a heap by pushing N times — that's N × O(log N) = **O(N log N)**, no better than sorting. But there's a linear-time build, **heapify**, and its O(N) is a genuinely surprising result worth proving.

**Heapify is O(N).** Given an arbitrary array, call `sift_down` on every non-leaf node, working from the *last* non-leaf (index `N//2 - 1`) up to the root. Why from the bottom up? Because `sift_down(i)` assumes both subtrees below `i` are already valid heaps, and processing bottom-to-top guarantees that. The cost looks like O(N log N) — N nodes, each sift_down up to O(log N) — but that bound is loose. The real cost: a node at height `h` above the leaves does at most `h` swaps, and a heap has **many** low nodes and **few** high ones — half the nodes are leaves (height 0, zero work), a quarter are height 1, an eighth height 2. Sum the actual work:

```
total work ≈ N × Σ (h / 2^(h+1))   for h = 0, 1, 2, ...
           = N × (1/2·0 + 1/4·1 + 1/8·2 + 1/16·3 + ...)
           = N × Σ h/2^(h+1)  →  N × 1     (the series converges to 1)
           = O(N)
```

The series `Σ h/2^h = 2` converges to a *constant*, so the whole build is O(N) — not O(N log N). The intuition: the expensive sift_downs (near the root, up to log N swaps) are *rare* (one root, two at the next level), and the cheap ones (leaves, zero swaps) are *most* of the tree. The many cheap operations dominate the few expensive ones, and the total collapses to linear — the same "the rare expensive event is drowned by the many cheap ones" shape you saw in M01's amortized append, now in a static build.

#### Worked example — the top-k receipt for one pass of the bounded heap

Stream: `[5, 1, 8, 3, 9, 2, 7]`, want top **k = 3** (largest). Maintain a min-heap of size ≤ 3; its root is the *smallest* of the current top-3, the eviction threshold.

| item | heap before | action | heap after (as sorted set) | why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | `{}` | push (size < 3) | `{5}` | room |
| 1 | `{5}` | push (size < 3) | `{1, 5}` | room |
| 8 | `{1, 5}` | push (size < 3) | `{1, 5, 8}` | room; now full, threshold = 1 |
| 3 | `{1,5,8}` | 3 > 1 → pop 1, push 3 | `{3, 5, 8}` | 3 beats the weakest; threshold → 3 |
| 9 | `{3,5,8}` | 9 > 3 → pop 3, push 9 | `{5, 8, 9}` | 9 beats the weakest; threshold → 5 |
| 2 | `{5,8,9}` | 2 > 5? no → discard | `{5, 8, 9}` | O(1) rejection, no heap op |
| 7 | `{5,8,9}` | 7 > 5 → pop 5, push 7 | `{7, 8, 9}` | 7 beats the weakest |

Result: `{7, 8, 9}` — the three largest, confirmed against the sorted truth `sorted([5,1,8,3,9,2,7], reverse=True)[:3] = [9, 8, 7]`. Count the heap operations: 3 initial pushes + 3 evict-pushes + **1 free O(1) rejection** (the `2`). On a real stream where most items are *not* in the top-k — the common case, since only k of N ever qualify — the overwhelming majority hit that O(1) `x > heap[0]` rejection and never touch a heap operation at all. That is the hidden win the Big-O `O(N log k)` *understates*: the `log k` factor is only paid by the items that actually make the cut.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"To get the top k I have to sort."** No — sorting fully orders all N when you only need the k best and don't even need *those* sorted until the end. The bounded heap does O(N log k) and O(k) memory; sorting does O(N log N) and O(N) memory. Surface it by asking: "you sorted a million candidates to return ten — what did you do with the 999,990 you ranked and discarded?"
- **"The bounded heap is always faster because O(N log k) < O(N log N)."** Only asymptotically, and in *pure Python* it can lose to C `sorted` at moderate N because the interpreter tax (30–100×) dwarfs the `log N / log k ≈ 9×` operation savings. Surface it by having the learner predict the winner at N = 10^7, k = 10 (they usually pick the heap) and then measure (pure-Python heap loses; `heapq.nlargest` wins). The exponent and the wall clock disagree — that disagreement is the lesson.
- **"Heapify is O(N log N) because it sift_downs N nodes."** No — it's O(N), because the sift_down cost is dominated by the *many* low nodes doing ~0 work, not the *few* high nodes doing log N work; the series `Σ h/2^h` converges to a constant. Surface it by having the learner sum the per-level work (N/2 nodes × 0 + N/4 × 1 + N/8 × 2 + …) and watch it converge, not diverge.

### Lesson 3 — When you need order, not just the extremes

#### Teaching text

The heap does one thing: the extreme, cheaply. But some questions the heap simply *cannot* answer. "Give me every score between 0.7 and 0.8." "What's the next-largest score after this one?" "Iterate all results in sorted order." A heap can't do any of these without tearing itself apart (pop everything, which destroys it and costs O(N log N)). For *ordered* queries you need a structure that keeps things sorted as they arrive: a **binary search tree** — and, critically, a **balanced** one.

A **binary search tree (BST)** obeys the search property: for every node, all keys in its left subtree are smaller and all keys in its right subtree are larger. That single invariant makes search a descent — compare, go left or right, repeat — so search, insert, and delete are all **O(height)**. When the tree is bushy and balanced, height is O(log N) and everything is fast. The catastrophe is that a *naive* BST does not stay balanced, and its height depends entirely on insertion order.

**The degeneration.** Insert keys `1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7` into a naive BST in that (sorted) order. `1` is the root. `2 > 1`, goes right. `3 > 2`, goes right again. Every key is larger than all before it, so every key goes right, and you build not a tree but a **linked list**:

```
1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7          height = 7 = N,  search = O(N)
```

The search property holds perfectly — and the structure is useless, because its height is N, not log N. Search costs O(N), the very thing the tree was supposed to prevent. And sorted (or reverse-sorted, or nearly-sorted) input is not adversarial exotica — it is the *common* case: timestamps, auto-increment IDs, already-processed data. A naive BST fed realistic data degrades to a linked list exactly when you need it most.

**Balancing** fixes this by enforcing an invariant that *bounds the height* at O(log N) no matter the insertion order. AVL trees keep the left and right subtree heights within 1; red-black trees keep the longest path within 2× the shortest; both perform O(1) local **rotations** during insert/delete to restore balance. You don't implement the rotations by hand in this module (that's a rabbit hole with diminishing returns for an AI engineer) — you *understand what balance buys*: it converts the worst-case height from N back to ⌊log₂ N⌋, restoring O(log N) search/insert/delete *and* the killer feature a heap lacks — **ordered iteration and range queries** — because an in-order traversal of a BST visits keys in sorted order, and a range query is just "descend to the low end, walk in-order until the high end."

The balanced tree buys ordered queries that a heap cannot do. But it costs more than a heap for the heap's own job: a balanced BST does O(log N) to find the minimum (descend all the way left), where a heap does O(1). More structure, more cost. So the real skill this lesson installs is **choosing the least structure that answers your question** — the same restraint as M02's "don't reach for a tree when a hash suffices," now one level up. Here is the decision rule:

| You need… | Reach for… | min / max | search a key | insert | ordered iteration | range query | memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| the k best / the extreme, streaming | **heap** | **O(1)** peek | O(N) ✗ | O(log N) | ✗ (destroys it) | ✗ | O(N), contiguous |
| ordered iteration, range, successor | **balanced BST** | O(log N) | O(log N) | O(log N) | **O(N) in order** | **O(log N + m)** | O(N), pointers |
| search + order, rarely mutated | **sorted array** | O(1) ends | O(log N) bisect | O(N) ✗ | **O(N) in order** | O(log N + m) | O(N), contiguous |
| the k best of a *static* array, once | **quickselect** | — | — | — | ✗ | ✗ | O(1) in-place |

Read the rows as trade-offs, not rankings. The **heap** is the minimal structure for top-k and it is unbeatable there — O(1) extreme, O(k) memory, streaming — but it can't search or iterate in order. The **balanced BST** is the only one that does range queries *and* stays mutable. The **sorted array** beats the BST when the data barely changes (cheaper memory, cache-friendly, `bisect` search) but O(N) inserts kill it under churn. **Quickselect** deserves its own line: for the top-k of a *static, in-memory* array in one shot, it partitions around a pivot (like quicksort but recursing into only *one* side) and finds the k best in **O(N) average** time — beating both the heap's O(N log k) and the sort's O(N log N) — but it needs the whole array in memory (no streaming), mutates it, and has an O(N²) worst case on bad pivots. Three top-k algorithms, three regimes: **sort** (simplest, when N is small or you need the losers sorted too), **bounded heap** (streaming, or N ≫ k with k unknown up front), **quickselect** (static array in RAM, one shot, want the theoretical O(N)).

A blunt, real fact that makes the choice concrete: **Python's standard library ships a heap (`heapq`) but no balanced BST.** When Python engineers need ordered-map behavior they reach for `bisect` on a list (great for read-heavy, O(N) insert) or the third-party `sortedcontainers`. So in practice you use the heap constantly and the balanced tree rarely — which is exactly why this module *builds* the heap and only *teaches the shape of* the tree.

#### Worked example — the same 7 keys, degenerate vs balanced

Insert `1..7` in sorted order.

**Naive BST (insertion order = sorted):** every key goes right → a chain of height 7. Searching for `7` compares against `1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7` — **7 comparisons, O(N)**. This is the failure.

**Balanced BST (same keys, balance maintained):**

```
            4
          /   \
         2     6
        / \   / \
       1  3  5   7          height = 3 = ⌈log₂(7+1)⌉
```

Searching for `7`: `7 > 4` go right → `7 > 6` go right → `7 = 7` found. **3 comparisons, O(log N)**. Same keys, same search property, and balance alone cut the search from 7 comparisons to 3. At N = 10^6 the gap is 10^6 comparisons vs 20 — the difference between a structure that scales and a linked list in disguise. And the balanced tree's extra gift: an in-order walk yields `1 2 3 4 5 6 7` — sorted, which the heap's array `[1,3,2,7,5,4,6]` never does. That ordered walk is the *reason* you'd pay the tree's higher constant over a heap: you needed order, and the heap doesn't sell it.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"A binary search tree is automatically fast (O(log N))."** Only if it's *balanced*. A naive BST fed sorted input degenerates to a linked list — O(N) height, O(N) search — and sorted-ish input (timestamps, IDs) is the common case, not an adversarial one. Surface it by having the learner insert `1..7` in order and draw the chain, then insert them in balanced order and draw the bushy tree; same keys, height 7 vs 3.
- **"Use a tree for top-k; it's the general ordered structure."** Overkill and slower for *this* job: a heap does the extreme in O(1) and top-k in O(N log k) with O(k) streaming memory, where a balanced tree does the min in O(log N) and can't stream. Reach for the tree only when you need what the heap *can't* do — range queries, ordered iteration, successor. Surface it by asking which structure answers "the 10 largest of a billion-item stream" with O(k) memory (heap) vs "every score in [0.7, 0.8]" (tree).
- **"Quickselect is strictly better than a heap for top-k because it's O(N)."** Only for a *static, in-memory, mutable* array in one shot — quickselect can't stream, mutates its input, and has an O(N²) worst case; the bounded heap is O(N log k) but streams in O(k) memory over data that never fits in RAM. Surface it by asking each to run over a generator of 10^9 items (heap: fine, O(k); quickselect: impossible, needs the array materialized — the memory wall from M01).

### Lesson 4 — Top-k at scale: the interpreter tax, the memory wall, and the survivor

#### Teaching text

Now push top-k to the N where AI systems actually live — 10^7 candidates in a reranker, 10^9 vectors in an index — and watch which algorithm survives. Two AIE-102/M01 forces decide it, and they pull in *opposite* directions, which is why you must predict rather than guess.

**Force 1 — the interpreter tax decides the time race at moderate N.** At N = 10^7, k = 10, the three top-k methods:

| method | class | measured @ N=10^7, k=10 | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `sorted(data, reverse=True)[:k]` | O(N log N) | **~5.0 s** | C Timsort, tiny constant, but sorts all 10^7 |
| pure-Python bounded `MinHeap` | O(N log k) | **~12 s** | fewer ops (log k not log N) but each pays the ~50× interpreter tax |
| `heapq.nlargest(k, data)` | O(N log k) | **~1.4 s** | the *same* bounded heap, in C — best of both |

Read the reversal: the pure-Python heap has the **better Big-O** and the **worse wall clock**. It does ~9× fewer comparisons than the sort (log N / log k), but pays ~50× per comparison (interpreter tax vs C), so net it's ~5× *slower* than C `sorted`. The exponent said "heap wins"; the constant said "in Python, no." The lever that un-reverses it is not a better algorithm — it's *dropping the tax*: `heapq.nlargest` is the identical O(N log k) algorithm compiled to C, and now the 9× operation savings lands on top of a C constant, beating `sorted` by ~3.5×. **The algorithm and the language are two separate decisions, and at moderate N the language decides.** (This is M01's Lesson 2 exchange-rate, now with teeth you can feel: "asymptotically better" bought you nothing until you also paid off the constant.)

**Force 2 — the memory wall decides everything at 10^9, and it makes the heap the only survivor.** `sorted` and quickselect both need the **entire array in memory** — O(N). A Python `float` costs ~24 bytes as an object plus an 8-byte pointer in the list ≈ **32 bytes/element** (the M01 bytes-per-element calculation). At N = 10^9 that is **32 GB** — past the 16 GB machine's RAM. `sorted` doesn't get *slow* at 10^9; it **can't run** — it hits the memory wall and either crashes or swaps to disk (a 1000× slowdown, the M01 memory-wall result). The bounded heap holds **O(k)** — ten floats, a few hundred bytes — *regardless of N*, because it **streams**: it consumes items one at a time from a generator, keeps only the k best, and never materializes the billion. Its memory is flat while `sorted`'s grows linearly into the wall.

So the scale story inverts the moderate-N story. At N = 10^7 the pure-Python heap *loses* on time to C `sorted`. At N = 10^9 `sorted` doesn't run at all, and the streaming heap — whether pure Python (slow but alive) or `heapq` (fast and alive) — is the *only* thing that produces an answer, because it's the only one whose memory doesn't depend on N. **The winner at scale is chosen by the memory wall, not the exponent** — the deepest M01 lesson, arriving exactly where AI systems hit it. This is why every production top-k over a large corpus is a streaming bounded heap (`heapq.nlargest` over a generator, or its C++/Rust equivalent): not because O(N log k) beats O(N log N) on time — in pure Python it doesn't — but because O(k) memory is the only footprint that reaches 10^9.

And now the seam the ethics thread runs through: a streaming top-k is *exact* — it returns the true k best. But its speed and its O(k) footprint are the reason production systems push one step further to **approximate** top-k (M05's ANN index): don't even scan all N, prune whole regions of candidates. Approximation trades correctness for speed, and the items it silently drops are not random — they're the ones far from every cluster, the hardest to retrieve. Hold that seam; Tier 3 pulls it.

#### Worked example — which top-k survives at 10^9 on a 16 GB machine?

You must return the top k = 100 scores from a stream of N = 10^9 documents on a 16 GB machine. Price each candidate the M01 way — a number with a unit against a budget with the same unit:

- **`sorted(list(stream), reverse=True)[:100]`** — memory footprint = N × 32 bytes = 10^9 × 32 = **32 GB** against a 16 GB budget. **Falls over on the memory wall** before you time a single comparison. Verdict: does not run. The `list(stream)` alone OOMs.
- **quickselect on the materialized array** — same 32 GB materialization requirement. **Same memory wall.** Verdict: does not run. Its beautiful O(N)-average time is irrelevant; it never gets to run.
- **streaming `heapq.nlargest(100, stream)`** — memory footprint = O(k) = 100 floats + heap overhead ≈ **a few KB**, flat in N. Time = O(N log k) in C ≈ 10^9 × log₂(100) × (C constant) ≈ 10^9 × 6.6 × ~5 ns ≈ **~33 s**. Against a batch budget of minutes: **fits, and runs.** Verdict: the survivor.

The decision wasn't close and it wasn't about Big-O time — two of the three never executed. You predicted the survivor by pricing *memory* first, exactly as M01 taught: a structure falls over at the smaller of its time and memory walls, and at 10^9 the memory wall is the one that bites. "Use a heap for top-k at scale" is not folklore; it is the single algorithm whose footprint is independent of N.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"At scale, pick the algorithm with the best time complexity."** No — at 10^9 the *memory* wall decides first: O(N)-memory methods (`sorted`, quickselect) can't run on data that doesn't fit, regardless of their time class. The streaming O(k)-memory heap wins by *fitting*, not by being fast. Surface it by pricing 10^9 floats (32 GB) against 16 GB before timing anything.
- **"`heapq` and my pure-Python heap have the same performance since they're the same algorithm."** Same Big-O, ~30–100× different constant — `heapq` is C, your loop is interpreted. At N = 10^7 that's the difference between ~1.4 s and ~12 s for the *identical* O(N log k) algorithm. Surface it by benchmarking both against `sorted` and watching one heap lose to the sort and the other beat it.
- **"Streaming top-k is approximate / lossy."** No — a streaming bounded heap is **exact**; it returns the true k best, it just never holds more than k at once. The *approximation* enters one layer up (M05's ANN pruning), and that's a separate, deliberate trade of correctness for speed. Surface it by proving the streaming heap equals `sorted(...)[:k]` exactly (Lab M03-B does), then contrasting with what an approximate index gives up.

```python
    def push(self, x):
        self._h.append(x)
        self._sift_up(len(self._h) - 1)

    def pop(self):
        h = self._h
        top = h[0]
        last = h.pop()          # remove the dense tail in O(1)
        if h:                   # if elements remain, reinstall & sift down
            h[0] = last
            self._sift_down(0)
        return top

    def _sift_up(self, i):
        h = self._h
        while i > 0:
            parent = (i - 1) // 2
            if h[i] < h[parent]:
                h[i], h[parent] = h[parent], h[i]
                i = parent
            else:
                break

    def _sift_down(self, i):
        h = self._h
        n = len(h)
        while True:
            left = 2 * i + 1
            right = 2 * i + 2
            smallest = i
            if left < n and h[left] < h[smallest]:
                smallest = left
            if right < n and h[right] < h[smallest]:
                smallest = right
            if smallest == i:
                break
            h[i], h[smallest] = h[smallest], h[i]
            i = smallest
```

```python
def topk_heap(iterable, k):
    if k <= 0:
        return []
    h = MinHeap()
    for x in iterable:
        if len(h) < k:
            h.push(x)
        elif x > h.peek():        # x belongs in the top-k; evict the weakest
            h.pop()
            h.push(x)
        # else: x is not in the top-k -> O(1) discard, no heap op
    out = []
    while len(h):
        out.append(h.pop())       # pops ascending (smallest of top-k first)
    out.reverse()                 # -> descending
    return out
```

from m03harness import peak_bytes
from topk import topk_heap

def stream(n, seed=0):
    """A generator of n random floats. Never materializes a list of size n."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for _ in range(n):
        yield rng.random()

def sort_topk_materialized(n, k, seed=0):
    """The sort-slice path: it MUST hold all n at once."""
    return sorted(list(stream(n, seed)), reverse=True)[:k]

The learner is handed an **unseen scenario** they have not benchmarked — e.g.: *"A reranker must return the top k = 50 documents from a stream of N = 2×10^9 candidate scores, on a 32 GB machine, within a 5-second batch budget. Three engineers propose: (a) `sorted(all_scores)[:50]`, (b) a pure-Python bounded min-heap, (c) `heapq.nlargest(50, stream)`."* They must, **in order, with the prediction sealed before any measurement**:

1. **Price each proposal (sealed).** For each of (a), (b), (c): state the time class and the memory class, convert the memory to bytes at the stated N (show the arithmetic with units), and state whether it clears the 32 GB wall. Name which proposals *run at all*.
2. **Predict the time winner among the survivors,** and state the reversal explicitly: which has the better Big-O, which wins the wall clock, and why they differ (name the interpreter tax).
3. **Classify empirically.** Using the provided harness at small N, measure the three methods' time and the two feasible methods' peak memory; confirm or correct your predicted classes and winner.
4. **Reconcile.** For every miss — wrong wall, wrong survivor, wrong time winner — give the corrected model in one sentence and name the force (memory wall vs interpreter tax) that produced the error.
5. **Prescribe and justify.** Name the proposal you'd ship, the *one* change that would make it both feasible and fastest (e.g. "stream + C `nlargest`"), and state its resulting footprint and time at N = 2×10^9.

The artifact is the judgment trail: sealed pricing → survivor call → empirical classification → reconciliation → prescription. A learner who can run the harness but cannot *price the memory wall and name the survivor before measuring* scores near zero.

### Rubric (gaming-resistant)

| Criterion | Weight | 0 — absent | 1 — partial | 2 — proficient | 3 — commanding |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Prediction sealed before measurement** | gate | No sealed sheet, or edited after measuring → **exam void** | — | Sealed, timestamped, unedited | Sealed with explicit per-proposal reasoning and units |
| **Prices memory & names the survivor** | 25% | Names classes, no bytes ("sort is O(N), heavy") | Bytes for one proposal, or omits the wall check | Correct bytes at N for all three *and* names which run vs hit the wall | Both, *and* states the exact N at which the O(N) methods fall over |
| **Predicts the reversal (time)** | 25% | Picks the lower-Big-O as the wall-clock winner with no caveat | Notes a discrepancy but can't name the cause | Correct wall-clock ranking *and* names the interpreter tax as the cause | Correct ranking, cause, *and* the lever (drop to C) that un-reverses it, with the ~factor |
| **Builds a correct heap & top-k** | 20% | Heap fails `heapq` equivalence, or top-k wrong | Heap correct but top-k not streaming / wrong order | Heap matches `heapq`; top-k streams and equals sort exactly | All that, *and* explains the O(1)-rejection path and the O(N) heapify bound |
| **Reconciliation of misses** | 15% | Ignores misses | Notes misses, no corrected model | Every miss → corrected one-sentence model naming the force | Corrections generalize to a rule that predicts a new N/k regime |
| **Prescribes the survivor** | 15% | No choice, or an infeasible one | Names a feasible method, wrong/again mechanism | Correct method + the change that makes it feasible *and* fast | Correct method, change, *and* its footprint + time at the stated N |

**Passing requires ≥ 2 on both "Prices memory & names the survivor" and "Predicts the reversal (time)."** A learner who builds a flawless heap but predicts the pure-Python bounded heap will win the wall clock at 10^7 (it loses) and forgets the 10^9 memory wall (where it's the survivor) does **not** pass — matching the course spine: grade the prediction and the reasoning, never the passing program.

## Elle pacing notes

Signals for the adaptation runtime. M03's dominant risk is **over-trusting Big-O** — learners arrive from M01/M02 having just learned to price complexity classes, and they now over-apply the exponent, predicting the lower-Big-O algorithm wins the wall clock and forgetting both the interpreter tax and the memory wall. The reroutes are built to let the *measurement* re-teach the constant and the wall.

- **Wall — "the heap is O(N log k) so it obviously wins" (the exponent-worship wall).** The most common M03-B stall: the learner confidently predicts the pure-Python bounded heap beats C `sorted` at 10^7 and is stunned when it loses. This is *productive*, not a failure — it's M01's exchange-rate lesson landing with force. **Reroute:** do not pre-warn them; let them predict wrong and seal it, then run `race.py` and let the ~12 s vs ~5 s offend them, then write `9× fewer ops × 50× per-op tax = net loss` on the board. Return to Lesson 4's table once the number has bitten. Log `struggle-productive`, never `blocked`.
- **Wall — sift-down swaps with the wrong child.** A real correctness bug: the learner swaps with the *left* or the *larger* child, and the heap property silently corrupts (tests catch it via `heapq` equivalence, but the learner may not see *why*). **Reroute:** hand them the Lesson-1 worked example and have them sift `6` down swapping with the *larger* child, then check the heap property one level down — it fails immediately. The concrete broken tree teaches "smaller child" better than the rule. Log `blocked-mechanical`, distinct from conceptual.
- **Wall — "heapify must be O(N log N), it sift_downs every node."** The loose-bound trap. **Reroute:** don't argue the series; have them *count* — how many nodes are leaves (do 0 work)? Half. How many are the root (do log N work)? One. Write the per-level sum N/2·0 + N/4·1 + N/8·2 + … and have them add the first five terms and watch it approach N, not N log N. The convergence is visible in five terms. Return to the formula after the arithmetic.
- **Signal — predicting the pure-Python heap will run out of memory or be *impossible* at 10^9 (confusing time and memory).** Some learners over-correct after the reversal and think the *heap* is the one that dies at scale. **Reroute:** separate the two walls explicitly — the heap holds O(k) (a few hundred bytes) *regardless of N*, it's `sorted` that holds O(N) and hits 32 GB. Have them run `test_heap_memory_is_bounded` and watch the peak stay flat as N quadruples. Log `blocked-conceptual` on the memory model specifically; it must land before M05 (ANN footprint) and AIE-302 (RAG top-k).
- **Signal — reaching for a balanced tree to do top-k.** After Lesson 3 introduces trees, some learners over-generalize and propose a BST for the top-k stream. **Reroute:** ask the two diagnostic questions — "does top-k need range queries or ordered iteration? (no) does it need O(k) streaming memory? (yes)" — the answers point straight back to the heap. The tree is the answer to a *different* question (range/successor), and reaching for it here is the M02 "hash vs tree" restraint one level up. Log `struggle-productive` if they self-correct; `blocked-conceptual` if they insist the tree is "more general so safer."
- **Accelerate — clean M03-A reconciliation (correct log-N push, O(N) heapify) *and* a correct M03-B reversal prediction (or a sharply corrected miss naming the interpreter tax).** Such a learner has demonstrated they can build the structure *and* reason about class-vs-constant. They may **skip M03-C's build** and submit only its prediction sheet (the memory wall is the same M01 instrument applied once more). Mirrors the series "clean A + correct B → skip C build" pattern.
- **Do NOT accelerate past the memory-wall landing.** Speed on the heap mechanics does not license skipping the "at 10^9 the survivor is chosen by memory, not time" outcome — M05's ANN index and AIE-302's RAG top-k both open by pricing the footprint at scale, and a learner who can build a heap but predicts a 32 GB `sorted` will "just be slow" (rather than *not run*) will mis-design both. Accelerate *within* M03, never *out* of Lesson 4's wall.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — *top-k, and who the "top" silently excludes* — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).** A binary heap is an array with three index formulas; `push`/`pop` walk one root-to-leaf path, so both are O(log N) — at most 19 swaps at a million items, 29 at a billion, measurable with the copy/swap-counting heap in Lab M03-A. `heapify` builds a heap in O(N), not O(N log N), because the per-node work sums as `N·Σ h/2^h = N·2` — a convergent series, checkable by timing `heapify` against N repeated pushes. Top-k has two exact implementations: sort-then-slice (O(N log N) time, O(N) memory) and the bounded min-heap (O(N log k) time, O(k) memory, streaming), and Lab M03-B proves they return *identical* results. At N = 10^7, k = 10 the pure-Python bounded heap (~12 s) is *slower* than C `sorted` (~5 s) despite the better Big-O — the interpreter tax (~50×/op) beats the 9× operation savings — while `heapq.nlargest` (~1.4 s, the same algorithm in C) wins; all three timed with `timeit`. A Python float costs ~32 bytes/element, so `sorted` on 10^9 floats needs ~32 GB and cannot run on a 16 GB machine, where the streaming heap holds a few hundred bytes — measured with `tracemalloc` in Lab M03-C. All of it runs on a laptop with stdlib + NumPy.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Just use `heapq.nlargest`, top-k is a solved one-liner" is true and hides two things the reversal exposes. First, the booster framing — *"the better algorithm is the one with the better Big-O"* — cannot admit that at real-world N in a real language the **constant decides**, and that the "obviously better" O(N log k) heap loses to the "worse" O(N log N) sort until you also pay off the interpreter tax; complexity class is necessary and *not sufficient*, and the field teaches only the necessary half. Second, and deeper: the whole framing of top-k as "return the k best" treats *"best"* as a solved, neutral quantity — as if the scores were handed down objectively and the only engineering question is retrieving the top of them cheaply. But the score is a *model's* judgment, and top-k is a **hard cutoff**: item k is served and item k+1 is discarded, with no gradient between them, on the strength of a score difference that may be noise. The performance-purist counter-culture, obsessed with shaving the constant, cannot admit that the *fastest* correct top-k and the *fairest* selection are different problems and optimizing the first does nothing for the second. Both camps optimize the retrieval and neither audits the ranking that the retrieval faithfully, efficiently, serves.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** Every top-k draws a line at rank k and discards everything below it — that is the operation, exactly as designed and now, having built it, exactly as you understand it. When the k best are *exact* (your bounded heap), the line is at least honest: item k+1 really did score below item k. But production doesn't stay exact — it goes *approximate* (M05's ANN index, AIE-302's RAG) to reach the N where exact top-k can't fit, and approximation doesn't shave the line evenly. It prunes whole regions of the candidate space *without scanning them*, and the items in those unscanned regions — the document unlike any cluster, the name that embeds far from every neighbor, the query that matches nothing closely — are dropped not because they scored below k but because the fast index *never compared them at all*. The heap you built returns the true top-k of what it sees; the system it lives in decides what it sees, and buys its speed by not looking everywhere. So: when you return "the top 10," you are asserting a completeness you did not verify — that the best 10 were among the candidates the index bothered to score. Whose result was never in the heap because the fast path pruned the region they live in? And since the entire point of the speed was to *not scan that far*, what would it even take for you to notice they were missing — or is "efficient retrieval" precisely the name we give to never having to find out?

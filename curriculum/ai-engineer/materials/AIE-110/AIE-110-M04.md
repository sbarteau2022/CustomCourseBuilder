## Lessons

Four lessons. The first makes a graph concrete: two representations, their real cost, and why real graphs (web, citation, knowledge) are sparse, which is the fact the rest of the module is built on. The second gives you the two traversal orders — BFS and DFS — and proves both are O(V+E), then shows BFS is the free shortest-path algorithm when edges are unweighted. The third adds weights and reuses the M03 heap to build Dijkstra, then runs the honest experiment on whether the classic "heap wins sparse, array wins dense" story survives contact with real random data (it mostly doesn't, and *why* it doesn't is the lesson). The fourth leaves shortest paths behind for a different question — importance — and builds PageRank as a hand-run eigenvector computation, with an explicit bridge to AIE-103.

1. **A graph is a relationship, and relationships are sparse** — adjacency list vs adjacency matrix, directed/weighted, and why real graphs (web, citations, knowledge bases) live at E ≈ O(V), not O(V²).
2. **BFS and DFS — the two orders, and the free shortest path** — queue vs stack, both O(V+E), why BFS alone gives unweighted shortest paths, and the DFS recursion-depth trap.
3. **Dijkstra: shortest paths with weights, built on the heap you already have** — the heap-based algorithm, the textbook density argument for a rival array-scan version, and what real (non-adversarial) weighted data actually does to that argument.
4. **PageRank: from "how far" to "how important"** — eigenvector centrality by hand, power iteration, the damping factor, and the bridge to AIE-103.

### Lesson 1 — A graph is a relationship, and relationships are sparse

#### Teaching text

A **graph** is a set of **nodes** (vertices) and a set of **edges** — pairs of nodes that are related. That is the entire definition, and its generality is the point: a hyperlink graph (page → page), a citation graph (paper → paper), a knowledge graph (entity → entity, labeled "is-a" or "part-of"), a call graph (function → function), a social graph (person → person) — all of them are the *same* data structure wearing a different label. Two properties matter for how you build one: is an edge **directed** (A links to B does not mean B links to A — true of hyperlinks and citations) or **undirected** (A is a coworker of B implies the reverse — true of friendship graphs)? And is an edge **weighted** (a cost, a distance, a similarity score) or unweighted (just "connected or not")? Lesson 2 needs only direction; Lesson 3 needs weight.

There are exactly two ways to store a graph, and M02 already built the one that wins for AI-scale graphs.

**Adjacency matrix.** A V×V grid (a 2D array, or a NumPy array) where `M[i][j] = 1` (or the weight) if an edge runs from node `i` to node `j`, else 0. Checking "is there an edge `i → j`" is **O(1)** — direct index. But the matrix costs **O(V²)** memory *regardless of how many edges actually exist* — a graph with 10 nodes and 3 edges still allocates 100 cells, 97 of them zero.

**Adjacency list.** A dict mapping each node to the list (or set) of its neighbors — `{u: [v1, v2, ...]}`. This is a **hash table** (M02) holding, per key, a small list. Memory is **O(V + E)** — you pay for the nodes and the edges that actually exist, nothing else. Checking "is there an edge `i → j`" costs O(degree(i)) with a list, or O(1) with a set — degree(i) being however many neighbors `i` actually has, not V.

The choice looks like a coin flip until you price a real graph. **Real graphs are sparse.** A citation graph with 40 million papers has, per paper, a bibliography of maybe 30–80 references — degree is a small constant, essentially independent of V. A hyperlink graph with a billion pages has, per page, tens of outbound links, not a billion. This is not an accident of these two examples; it is close to universal for graphs that arise from things people actually author (a paper's author can only cite so many other papers; a page's author can only link so many other pages) — **E is O(V), not O(V²)**, for essentially every graph you will build in this course. Price both representations at V = 10^8, E = 5×10^9 (a large but realistic knowledge-graph scale, average degree 50): the adjacency **list** costs roughly `(V + E) × (small constant)` ≈ a few hundred GB, dominated by the *edges*, which is a real number you can budget for (and later shard). The adjacency **matrix** costs `V² = 10^16` cells — **ten quadrillion**, most of them zero — a number that is not "large," it is *not representable*, full stop, on any machine that exists. The adjacency matrix is not a slower choice at this scale; like `sorted()` on 10^9 floats in M03, it is a **memory-wall** choice — it does not run.

This is why every graph library that touches real-scale data (NetworkX's default, every production knowledge-graph store, `scipy.sparse` for anything graph-shaped) is adjacency-list-based, and it is why this module builds the adjacency list on top of the exact dict-of-lists structure M02 already justified: the graph's node-lookup ("does this node exist, what are its neighbors") is M02's hash table doing the job it was built for, one level up.

#### Worked example — pricing both representations at three scales

| V (nodes) | avg degree | E (edges) | adjacency **list** — O(V+E) | adjacency **matrix** — O(V²) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 20 | 20,000 | ≈ 20,000 × (small const) ≈ **a few hundred KB** | 1,000² = 1,000,000 cells ≈ **8 MB** (float64) |
| 1,000,000 | 20 | 20,000,000 | ≈ 20M entries ≈ **a few hundred MB** | 10^6² = **10^12 cells ≈ 8 TB** |
| 100,000,000 | 20 | 2,000,000,000 | ≈ 2B entries ≈ **tens of GB** | 10^8² = **10^16 cells — not representable** |

Read the middle row carefully: at a million nodes — a modest knowledge graph, nowhere near web scale — the *matrix* already needs 8 **terabytes**, while the *list*, holding the same information, needs a few hundred **megabytes**: roughly **four orders of magnitude** less, for identical content, because the list never pays for the edges that don't exist. The gap doesn't grow gracefully; it *explodes*, because O(V²) is quadratic and O(V+E) is linear in a quantity (E) that itself grows only linearly with V for sparse graphs. By the bottom row the matrix has left the realm of "expensive" and entered "impossible" — the same memory-wall cliff M01 and M03 already taught, now landing on the very first design decision of this module.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"The adjacency matrix is the textbook default, so it's a safe first choice."** It is O(V²) memory *no matter how sparse the graph is* — a graph with a million nodes and twenty million edges (sparse, realistic) still allocates a trillion matrix cells if you store it densely. Surface it by asking the learner to compute matrix memory for V=10^6, then compare to the list's actual edge count (2×10^7) — the ratio is the misconception, made concrete.
- **"A graph edge implies its reverse."** True for undirected graphs (friendship, coauthorship), false for directed ones (hyperlinks, citations, "cites" edges) — Paper A citing Paper B does not mean B cites A, and treating a citation graph as undirected silently invents thousands of citations that were never made. Surface it by asking: "if I follow every edge backward in a citation graph, what have I built?" (answer: "who cites me," a different and equally real graph — but not the same one, and conflating them corrupts both).
- **"More nodes means proportionally more possible connections, so E naturally grows with V²."** E *could* grow as V² (a complete graph), but real authored graphs — the ones from citations, hyperlinks, code call graphs — don't, because each node's *authoring cost* per edge is roughly constant (a paper's author can only write so many citations) independent of how big the graph gets. Surface it by asking the learner to estimate their own citation list's length as the field's paper count 10×'s — it doesn't 10× too.

### Lesson 2 — BFS and DFS: the two orders, and the free shortest path

#### Teaching text

Given a graph, the first question is almost always: **starting from node S, what can I reach, and how?** Two algorithms answer it, differing only in the *order* they explore, and that single difference has real consequences.

**Breadth-first search (BFS)** explores in **layers**: everything one hop from S, then everything two hops away, then three, and so on. It is implemented with a **queue** (FIFO — first found, first explored): pop the front, look at its unvisited neighbors, mark them seen and push them to the back. Because it exhausts an entire layer before touching the next, **the first time BFS reaches a node is via a shortest path to it, measured in edge count** — this is not a side effect, it is BFS's entire value proposition. If every edge weighs the same (a hyperlink graph, a call graph, "how many degrees of separation"), BFS *is* Dijkstra, for free, without a heap.

**Depth-first search (DFS)** explores **greedily deep**: from S, go to an unvisited neighbor, then *that* neighbor's unvisited neighbor, and keep diving until you hit a dead end, then back up one step and try the next branch. It is implemented with a **stack** (LIFO — most recently found, explored next) — or, equivalently and more naturally, with **recursion**, since the call stack *is* a stack. DFS gives you a valid *reachability* answer (everything DFS visits is reachable from S) but its visiting order has **no shortest-path guarantee at all** — it commits to a branch and rides it to the bottom regardless of how long that branch is, even if a two-hop path to the same node exists via a branch DFS hasn't tried yet.

Both algorithms touch every node once (mark-and-skip, via a `visited` set — M02's hash table again) and every edge once (from directed graphs) or twice (from undirected graphs, once per endpoint) — so both are **O(V + E)**, not O(V·E) and not O(V²). This is the same shape as M01's amortized argument: the *total* work summed across all nodes is proportional to the number of edges actually examined, not the number of pairs that could theoretically be checked.

**The DFS trap: recursion depth is O(height), and height can be O(V).** A recursive DFS pushes one Python stack frame per edge it descends before backing up. On a bushy graph that's shallow — fine. But on a **long chain** (a linked-list-shaped graph — exactly the kind you get from sequential IDs, a call chain, or a long citation lineage) the recursion depth equals the chain length. Python's default recursion limit is **1000** (`sys.getrecursionlimit()`), and it exists precisely because the C stack backing Python's call stack is finite. Verified directly: a DFS on a 2000-node chain graph, run recursively, raises `RecursionError: maximum recursion depth exceeded`; the identical traversal, run **iteratively** with an explicit `list`-as-stack, completes and returns all 2000 nodes. Same algorithm, same O(V+E) class — one crashes on realistic input, one doesn't, because one of them silently traded the heap's flexible memory for the call stack's fixed one.

#### Worked example — BFS and DFS on the same 6-node graph

```
edges (directed):  A→B, A→C, B→D, B→E, C→D, C→F, D→E

        A
       / \
      B   C
     / \ / \
    D   E   F      (D is reachable via both B and C)
```

Adjacency list (Python dict, insertion order preserved): `{A:[B,C], B:[D,E], C:[D,F], D:[E], E:[], F:[]}`.

**BFS from A** — queue trace:

| step | pop | dist so far | newly discovered → pushed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | {A:0} | B (dist 1), C (dist 1) |
| 2 | B | {A:0,B:1,C:1} | D (dist 2), E (dist 2) |
| 3 | C | {…,D:2,E:2} | F (dist 2) — D already seen via B, skipped |
| 4 | D | (no change) | E already seen |
| 5 | E | (no change) | (no neighbors) |
| 6 | F | (no change) | (no neighbors) |

Result: **`dist = {A:0, B:1, C:1, D:2, E:2, F:2}`**, visit order **`A, B, C, D, E, F`**. Note `D` is reachable via both `B` and `C` (both 2 hops) — BFS reports the true shortest hop-count regardless of which parent got there first, because it never revisits.

**DFS from A** (both recursive and, with neighbors pushed in reverse order onto an explicit stack, iterative — verified to produce the *identical* order for this graph): visit order **`A, B, D, E, C, F`**. Contrast with BFS's `A, B, C, D, E, F`: DFS reached `E` (a BFS-distance-2 node) *before* it ever looked at `C` (a BFS-distance-1 node) — DFS's order tracks nothing about hop-count, only "which branch did I commit to first."

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"DFS also finds the shortest path, it just explores in a different order."** No — DFS's visit order carries no distance guarantee at all. In the worked example DFS visits `E` (2 hops from A) before `C` (1 hop from A); if you used "the order DFS visited nodes" as a proxy for "how close they are," you'd rank a 2-hop node ahead of a 1-hop node. Surface it by asking the learner to point at the exact place in the DFS order where a closer node is visited later than a farther one.
- **"BFS and DFS are different complexity classes — one is more efficient."** Both are O(V+E); the difference is the *order* of exploration, not the *amount* of work. Surface it by having the learner count total node-visits and edge-examinations for both traversals of the worked-example graph — they're within a small constant of each other, not a different class.
- **"Recursive DFS is free — it's just a function calling itself."** Every recursive call is a real stack frame with real memory, bounded by Python's default recursion limit of 1000; a chain graph of length 2000 crashes a recursive DFS with `RecursionError` while the identical iterative (explicit-stack) DFS completes normally. Surface it by running both against a 2000-node chain and watching one crash — this is not a hypothetical, it reproduces every time.

### Lesson 3 — Dijkstra: shortest paths with weights, built on the heap you already have

#### Teaching text

BFS answers "fewest hops," which assumes every edge costs the same. Real edges usually don't — a road segment has a length, a network hop has a latency, a "similarity" edge has a cost that's cheaper the *more* similar two things are. For **weighted** shortest paths you need **Dijkstra's algorithm**, and it is built from a structure you already have: **M03's min-heap**, repurposed.

**The mechanism.** Maintain a min-heap of `(tentative_distance, node)` pairs and a `dist` dict of best-known distances. Start by pushing `(0, source)`. Repeatedly: pop the smallest-distance entry; if you've already finalized that node, discard it (a **stale entry** — more below); otherwise mark it finalized and **relax** each outgoing edge — for edge `(u, v, weight)`, if `dist[u] + weight < dist.get(v, ∞)`, that's a new best path to `v`, so update `dist[v]` and push `(new_dist, v)`. The greedy insight that makes this correct: because the heap always pops the *globally smallest* tentative distance next, and all weights are non-negative, the node you just popped can never be improved later by a longer path through an unfinalized node — its distance is final the moment it's popped.

**Why "stale entries," not decrease-key.** A textbook description of Dijkstra says "decrease the key of `v` in the priority queue" when you find a better distance — but M03's heap (and `heapq`) has no decrease-key operation; removing an arbitrary interior element and re-sifting costs O(N), not O(log N). The practical fix, used here and in `heapq`-based implementations everywhere: just **push a new, smaller entry** for `v` and leave the stale, larger one sitting in the heap. When the stale entry is eventually popped, the node is already in the `finalized`/`visited` set, so you discard it in O(1) and move on. This costs a *few* extra pushes and a *few* extra O(1) discards — it does not change the correctness, and empirically (below) it barely dents the performance.

**The classic complexity story — and what actually happens.** The heap-based version costs **O((V+E) log V)**: each of up to `V+E` pushes/pops costs O(log V) against a heap holding at most that many entries. A **naive array-scan** alternative skips the heap: keep a `dist` array and, each round, linearly scan all V nodes for the unvisited one with smallest tentative distance — V rounds × an O(V) scan = **O(V²)**, plus O(E) total for relaxation. Compare the classes: at E = Θ(V) (sparse — the realistic case per Lesson 1), the heap's `O(V log V)` crushes the array-scan's `O(V²)`. The textbook argument continues: at E = Θ(V²) (dense, near-complete graphs), `(V+E) log V ≈ V² log V` is *worse* than the array-scan's flat `V²` — so, the story goes, dense graphs should prefer array-scan.

**Measured, that second half of the story does not show up on real data.** Two implementations were raced — a hand-built pure-Python `MinHeap` (identical to M03's) and the array-scan version — on graphs from sparse (average out-degree 4) to nearly complete (99.9% of all possible directed edges present), with i.i.d. random weights:

| regime | V | E | pure-Python heap | array-scan | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **sparse** (avg out-degree 4) | 3,000 | ~12,000 | **11.4 ms** | **290.8 ms** | array is **25.5× slower** |
| **near-complete** (density 0.999) | 1,500 | ~2,246,000 | **270.6 ms** | **301.9 ms** | array is **1.12× slower** — barely |

The heap **never loses**, even at 99.9% density — the gap just shrinks from a 25× rout to a 12% edge as density climbs toward complete. Why does the textbook's "array-scan wins dense graphs" argument not materialize? Because that argument's `O(E)` term assumes the *worst case*: every one of the `E` edges triggers a successful relaxation (a push). Real relaxation, on graphs with i.i.d. random weights, is far rarer than that. A push only happens when a newly found path *beats the current record* to a node — and for random weights, "beating the current record" behaves like a **record statistic**: the number of times a running minimum is broken while scanning random values is expected to grow only **logarithmically**, not linearly, in the number of candidates. Measured directly: on a near-complete 800-node graph (632,847 edges), the heap performed only **2,851 pushes** — a small multiple of `V log₂ V ≈ 7,715`, and three orders of magnitude below `E`. The heap's realized cost tracks the *pushes it actually does*, not the edges that theoretically could have triggered one — so its true, typical-case cost stays close to `O(V log V)` even when `E` is quadratic. **The O((V+E) log V) bound is a ceiling, correct and provable, and real random-weight data almost never gets close to it** — a genuinely different flavor of "the exponent lied" than M03's interpreter-tax reversal: there, the *language* changed the constant; here, the *typical input* undercuts the worst-case bound itself.

One more force, layered on top and familiar from M03: `heapq` (C) beats the hand-built `MinHeap` (pure Python) in **both** regimes — 4.1 ms vs 11.4 ms sparse (2.8× faster), 251.0 ms vs 270.6 ms dense — the same interpreter tax, now acting on graph traversal instead of top-k. **Two independent forces, both favoring the heap**: the input-dependent realized cost (rarely near the worst case) and the C-vs-Python constant. Production shortest-path code (`scipy.sparse.csgraph.dijkstra`, NetworkX's default) uses the heap-based algorithm unconditionally, at every density — not because the textbook crossover is wrong in principle, but because it describes an adversarial input that real weighted graphs essentially never present.

**A boundary Dijkstra cannot cross: negative weights.** Dijkstra's greedy correctness argument depends on weights being non-negative — once a node is popped, no *later*, longer path can beat it, precisely because adding more non-negative edges can't shrink a distance. A single negative edge breaks that: a longer-looking path can, after one negative-weight edge, become shorter than the "finalized" distance. Dijkstra doesn't detect this failure; it silently returns a wrong answer. (Bellman-Ford, O(V·E), handles negative weights and detects negative cycles — out of scope here, but the boundary matters: know when you've left Dijkstra's contract.)

#### Worked example — trace Dijkstra's heap, stale entries included

Graph (directed, weighted): `A→B(4), A→C(1), C→B(1), B→D(1), C→D(5), D→E(3), B→E(7)`.

| pop | status | relax | dist updates | heap after (● = stale) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (0, A) | finalize A | A→B, A→C | dist B=4, dist C=1 | `[(1,C), (4,B)]` |
| (1, C) | finalize C | C→B, C→D | dist B: 1+1=**2** < 4 → update; dist D=1+5=6 | `[(2,B), (4,B)●, (6,D)]` |
| (2, B) | finalize B | B→D, B→E | dist D: 2+1=**3** < 6 → update; dist E=2+7=9 | `[(3,D), (4,B)●, (6,D)●, (9,E)]` |
| (3, D) | finalize D | D→E | dist E: 3+3=**6** < 9 → update | `[(4,B)●, (6,D)●, (6,E), (9,E)●]` |
| (4, B) | **B already finalized — discard, O(1)** | — | — | `[(6,D)●, (6,E), (9,E)●]` |
| (6, D) | **D already finalized — discard** | — | — | `[(6,E), (9,E)●]` |
| (6, E) | finalize E | (no out-edges) | — | `[(9,E)●]` |
| (9, E) | **discard, heap now empty** | — | — | `[]` |

Final: `dist = {A:0, B:2, C:1, D:3, E:6}`. Two things worth naming: (1) the shortest path to `B` is **not** the direct edge `A→B` (weight 4) but the two-hop `A→C→B` (1+1=2) — the greedy relaxation found the cheaper indirect route automatically. (2) Two stale entries (`(4,B)`, `(6,D)`) and one final stale pop (`(9,E)`) were popped and discarded in O(1) each — three "wasted" pops out of eight total, the concrete cost of skipping decrease-key. Nine total heap operations for a 5-node, 7-edge graph — consistent with O((V+E) log V) at this tiny scale, and a fraction of what a decrease-key-based Fibonacci-heap Dijkstra would do differently only in its *asymptotic* guarantee, not in this small trace.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"Dijkstra works with negative weights, it's just slower."** No — it silently returns a *wrong* answer, because the greedy "once popped, final" argument assumes distances can only grow as you add edges, and a negative edge violates that. Surface it with a 3-node counterexample: `A→B(2), A→C(5), C→B(-10)` — Dijkstra finalizes `B` at distance 2 (popped before `C`), then never revisits it, missing the true shortest path `A→C→B = 5-10 = -5`.
- **"The array-scan version is obsolete — always use the heap."** The heap wins on every measured regime here, including 99.9%-dense graphs with random weights (only a 1.12× margin at that extreme) — but that's an *empirical*, typical-case fact about random-weight data, not a proof; the O((V+E) log V) bound really is worse than O(V²) at true worst-case adversarial density, which is *why* the array-scan variant exists in the literature at all. Surface it by asking: "if pushes tracked the O(E) worst-case bound instead of the ~V log V we measured, which would win at V=1500, E=2.25M?" (arithmetic: `(V+E) log V ≈ 2.25M × 11 ≈ 25M` vs `V² ≈ 2.25M` — array wins by 10×, the reverse of what we measured).
- **"BFS on a weighted graph, just summing edge weights along the BFS tree, gives shortest weighted paths."** No — BFS's tree is built by hop-count, and the shortest-hop path is frequently not the shortest-*weight* path (the worked example: `A→B` direct is 1 hop but weight 4; `A→C→B` is 2 hops but weight 2, and is the true shortest path). Surface it by asking the learner to compute both the BFS-tree path weight and the true Dijkstra weight to `B` in the worked example and compare (4 vs 2).

### Lesson 4 — PageRank: from "how far" to "how important"

#### Teaching text

Lessons 2 and 3 answer *path* questions — how far, at what cost. PageRank answers a completely different kind of question: given a directed graph of links (web pages, citations, references between entities), **which nodes matter most?** The naive answer — count in-degree, the number of nodes pointing at you — is cheap (a single pass, O(E)) and *wrong* in a way worth taking seriously: it treats a link from an obscure, unvisited page exactly the same as a link from a page everyone visits. Intuitively, a link's weight should depend on the *importance of the page giving it* — which is a **recursive** definition (X is important if important pages link to X; but "important" is the very thing we're computing) that only stops circular by turning into a fixed-point problem.

**The PageRank equation.** For each node `p`:

```
PR(p) = (1 - d) / N  +  d · Σ_{q → p}  PR(q) / outdegree(q)
```

Read it as: `p`'s rank is a small base amount (`(1-d)/N`, spread evenly across all N nodes) plus a share of the rank of every node `q` that links to `p`, where `q`'s rank is divided evenly among *all* of `q`'s outgoing links (a highly-ranked node that links to 1,000 things gives each of them only 1/1000th of its rank; a highly-ranked node with one outgoing link gives that single link its *full* rank). The **damping factor** `d` (conventionally 0.85) models a "random surfer" who, with probability `d`, follows a link, and with probability `1-d`, jumps to a uniformly random page instead — this prevents rank from draining into **dead ends** (nodes with no outgoing links, or small closed loops that would otherwise trap and hoard all the rank with nowhere for it to escape).

**This is an eigenvector problem — the bridge to AIE-103.** Stack every node's rank into a vector `r` and every "share of `q`'s rank going to `p`" into a matrix `M` (column `q`, row `p`, entry `1/outdegree(q)` if `q→p` else 0 — the **transition matrix**). The PageRank equation becomes `r = d·M·r + (1-d)/N·𝟙`, and at the fixed point (where `r` stops changing), this is (after folding the damping term into the matrix) exactly `G·r = r` for a "Google matrix" `G` — **`r` is an eigenvector of `G` with eigenvalue 1.** You do not need AIE-103's linear algebra to *compute* this — the fixed point is found by an iterative method, below — but naming it correctly matters: PageRank is not "a graph algorithm" in the traversal sense of BFS or Dijkstra; it is a **linear-algebra fixed point**, computed *on* a graph, and AIE-103 will hand you the formal machinery (eigenvalues, eigenvectors, the Perron-Frobenius theorem guaranteeing a unique dominant eigenvector for exactly this kind of matrix) that explains *why* the method below is guaranteed to converge and to what. Here, you compute the eigenvector by hand, before you have the word for it.

**Power iteration: the mechanism.** Start with every node at equal rank `1/N`. Repeatedly apply `r ← d·M·r + (1-d)/N·𝟙` and watch `r` change less and less each round — it is provably converging to the dominant eigenvector of `G`, because repeatedly multiplying by a matrix amplifies its dominant eigenvalue's direction relative to every other direction (this is the mechanism AIE-103 will prove; here, watch it happen). Stop when the change between rounds (summed absolute difference) drops below a tolerance. Verified: a 4-node graph converges to a tolerance of `10⁻¹⁰` in **47 iterations**; a random 500-node sparse graph converges in **24 iterations**. Each iteration costs **O(E)** — one matrix-vector product against a *sparse* matrix touches only the actual edges, not all V² cells — so total cost is **O(E · iterations)**, with iterations essentially constant (tens, not growing with V for well-behaved graphs).

**Why not just call `np.linalg.eig` and take the dominant eigenvector directly?** Because general eigen-decomposition costs **O(V³)** and needs the **dense** matrix in memory (O(V²)) — the exact memory wall from Lesson 1, now attached to a slower time bound too. Measured directly on random sparse graphs (avg out-degree 5): at V=200, `eig` takes 32 ms vs power iteration's 0.4 ms (**91× slower**); at V=900, `eig` takes 611 ms vs power iteration's 1.4 ms (**428× slower**, the gap *widening* as V grows — consistent with O(V³) versus O(E·const)). At real knowledge-graph scale (V=10^5), the dense matrix alone would need `(10^5)² × 8 bytes ≈ 80 GB` — it does not fit — while power iteration's O(E) per step, on a sparse adjacency list with E ≈ 5×10^5 (avg degree 5), is trivial. **Power iteration is not a shortcut approximation of the "real" eigen-decomposition — it is the only one of the two that survives contact with V beyond a few thousand.**

#### Worked example — fewer links, higher rank

A structural example, converged with power iteration and cross-checked against `np.linalg.eig` (agreeing to 4 decimal places):

```
H1 ─┐                    S1 ─┐
H2 ─┤                    S2 ─┤
H3 ─┼──→ H ──→ Y         S3 ─┼──→ X
H4 ─┤                    S4 ─┤
H5 ─┘                    S5 ─┘
```

`H` receives links from five "hub-feeder" nodes and passes its entire rank to a single node `Y`. `X` receives links from five separate "stub" nodes (each a source with no other in-links). **Raw in-degree: `X` has 5, `Y` has 1** — a naive in-degree count ranks `X` above `Y`, five to one. **PageRank (converged, 38 iterations): `X = 0.2022`, `Y = 0.2104` — `Y` outranks `X`, despite four fewer inbound links**, because `Y`'s single link inherits the *combined* rank of a well-linked hub (`H`, itself fed by five sources, rank 0.2022, passed **entirely** to `Y` since `Y` is `H`'s only outgoing link), while `X`'s five links each carry only a small, un-boosted stub-node's worth of rank (each stub sits at the base rate, 0.0385, since nothing links to the stubs themselves). **Quality of the linking node, not quantity of links, is what the equation actually computes** — the naive "count the in-links" heuristic gets the ranking backward on this exact structure.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"PageRank is basically a fancy word for in-degree count."** Directly contradicted by the worked example: `X` (in-degree 5) is outranked by `Y` (in-degree 1), because PageRank weighs *whose* links you have, not how many. Surface it by having the learner compute plain in-degree for both `X` and `Y` and compare to the converged PageRank ranking — they disagree, and disagree in the "wrong" direction if you trusted the naive count.
- **"More power-iteration rounds always means a proportionally more accurate answer."** Power iteration converges **geometrically** (the gap to the true fixed point shrinks by a roughly constant *factor* each round, not a constant amount) — 47 rounds gets you to 10⁻¹⁰ tolerance on a 4-node graph, and round 1000 buys you essentially nothing more. Surface it by having the learner plot the round-over-round change and watch it collapse well before the iteration budget is exhausted.
- **"Power iteration is a cheap approximation; `np.linalg.eig` is the 'real,' correct answer you'd use if you could afford it."** Both converge to the *same* dominant eigenvector (verified: matching to 4 decimal places) — power iteration is not less correct, it is a different (and, past a few hundred nodes, the *only feasible*) algorithm for the identical mathematical target. Surface it by asking which one still runs at V=10^5 (power iteration: yes, O(E) per step on a sparse matrix; `eig`: no, 80 GB dense-matrix memory wall) — "cheap" and "approximate" are not the same claim, and here only one of them is true.

def bfs_dist(graph, source):
    dist = {source: 0}
    order = [source]
    q = deque([source])
    while q:
        u = q.popleft()
        for v, _w in graph.neighbors(u):
            if v not in dist:
                dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
                order.append(v)
                q.append(v)
    return dist, order


def dfs_recursive_order(graph, source):
    visited = {source}
    order = []
    def visit(u):
        order.append(u)
        for v, _w in graph.neighbors(u):
            if v not in visited:
                visited.add(v)
                visit(v)
    visit(source)
    return order


def dfs_iterative_order(graph, source):
    visited = {source}
    order = []
    stack = [source]
    while stack:
        u = stack.pop()
        order.append(u)
        for v, _w in reversed(graph.neighbors(u)):
            if v not in visited:
                visited.add(v)
                stack.append(v)
    return order

class MinHeap:
    def __init__(self):
        self._h = []
    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._h)
    def push(self, item):
        h = self._h
        h.append(item)
        i = len(h) - 1
        while i > 0:
            p = (i - 1) // 2
            if h[i] < h[p]:
                h[i], h[p] = h[p], h[i]
                i = p
            else:
                break
    def pop(self):
        h = self._h
        top = h[0]
        last = h.pop()
        if h:
            h[0] = last
            i, n = 0, len(h)
            while True:
                l, r = 2*i+1, 2*i+2
                smallest = i
                if l < n and h[l] < h[smallest]:
                    smallest = l
                if r < n and h[r] < h[smallest]:
                    smallest = r
                if smallest == i:
                    break
                h[i], h[smallest] = h[smallest], h[i]
                i = smallest
        return top
```

def dijkstra_heapq(graph, source):
    """Q: identical algorithm, C-accelerated via stdlib heapq."""
    dist = {source: 0.0}
    pq = [(0.0, source)]
    visited = set()
    while pq:
        d, u = heapq.heappop(pq)
        if u in visited:
            continue
        visited.add(u)
        for v, w in graph.neighbors(u):
            nd = d + w
            if v not in dist or nd < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = nd
                heapq.heappush(pq, (nd, v))
    return dist

def dijkstra_array(graph, source):
    """A: no heap. Each of V rounds linearly scans all V nodes for the
    unvisited minimum. O(V^2 + E)."""
    nodes = graph.nodes()
    dist = {u: float("inf") for u in nodes}
    dist[source] = 0.0
    visited = set()
    for _ in range(len(nodes)):
        u, best = None, float("inf")
        for cand in nodes:
            if cand not in visited and dist[cand] < best:
                best, u = dist[cand], cand
        if u is None:
            break
        visited.add(u)
        for v, w in graph.neighbors(u):
            nd = dist[u] + w
            if nd < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = nd
    return dist

import numpy as np

def build_transition_matrix(nodes, edges):
    """nodes: list of node ids (fixes the vector/matrix index order).
    edges: list of (u, v) meaning u -> v.
    Returns M where M[i,j] = 1/outdegree(j) if j->i is an edge, else 0.
    A node with outdegree 0 (dangling) gets a uniform column (jumps anywhere),
    so its rank doesn't vanish from the system."""
    idx = {u: i for i, u in enumerate(nodes)}
    n = len(nodes)
    M = np.zeros((n, n))
    outdeg = {u: 0 for u in nodes}
    for u, v in edges:
        outdeg[u] += 1
    for u, v in edges:
        M[idx[v], idx[u]] += 1.0 / outdeg[u]
    for u in nodes:
        if outdeg[u] == 0:
            M[:, idx[u]] = 1.0 / n
    return M

def pagerank_eig(M, d=0.85):
    """Reference: direct dominant-eigenvector computation. O(V^3) time,
    O(V^2) memory -- correct, and infeasible past a few thousand nodes."""
    n = M.shape[0]
    G = d * M + (1 - d) / n * np.ones((n, n))
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(G)
    i = np.argmax(vals.real)
    v = np.abs(vecs[:, i].real)
    return v / v.sum()
```

Reference solution (`pagerank_power_iteration`):

```python
def pagerank_power_iteration(M, d=0.85, iters=200, tol=1e-10):
    n = M.shape[0]
    r = np.full(n, 1.0 / n)
    teleport = np.full(n, (1 - d) / n)
    for round_num in range(1, iters + 1):
        r_next = d * (M @ r) + teleport
        if np.abs(r_next - r).sum() < tol:
            return r_next, round_num
        r = r_next
    return r, iters
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — *the graph is not the world, it's who got recorded linking to whom* — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).** A graph is nodes plus edges; the adjacency list, built on a Python dict (M02's hash table), costs O(V+E) memory against the adjacency matrix's O(V²) — at V=10^6 that gap is measured directly: a few hundred MB versus 8 TB for the identical information, and by V=10^8 the matrix's 10^16 cells are not representable on any machine. BFS and DFS are both O(V+E), verified by direct traversal of a 6-node worked graph (BFS: `A,B,C,D,E,F`; DFS: `A,B,D,E,C,F`, both reproducible with `test_graph.py`); a recursive DFS provably crashes with `RecursionError` on a 2000-node chain (Python's default recursion limit is 1000) while an iterative version completes. Dijkstra, built on M03's `MinHeap`, computes exact shortest weighted paths (`test_dijkstra.py`'s worked example: `A→C→B` at cost 2 beats the direct `A→B` at cost 4); measured on random-weight graphs, the heap-based version beats a naive array-scan by 25.5× when sparse and remains at least as fast (1.12× margin) even at 99.9% density, because realized relaxation counts (measured: 2,851 pushes against 632,847 possible edges on an 800-node dense graph) track close to `V log V`, far below the `E`-driven worst-case bound. PageRank, computed by power iteration on the transition matrix, converges to the same answer as direct eigen-decomposition (`np.linalg.eig`, agreeing to 4 decimal places) in 24–47 rounds at O(E) per round, while `eig` costs O(V³) and O(V²) memory — measured 91×–428× slower across V=200–900, and requiring 80 GB at V=10^5, past the 16 GB wall. All of it — the pricing tables, the traversal traces, the Dijkstra race, the PageRank convergence — runs on a laptop with stdlib + NumPy.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "PageRank ranks by importance" is the one-sentence version everyone learns, and it hides two things the module's own mechanics expose. First, the booster framing treats "importance" as a property the algorithm *discovers*, when the worked example shows precisely what it *computes*: a recursive function of who already links to you, weighted by who links to them. A brand-new, genuinely important paper or page has, by construction, few or no inbound links yet — PageRank cannot distinguish "not yet linked because it's new" from "not linked because it's unimportant," because the algorithm has no notion of time, only structure. It rewards *accumulated* endorsement, and calls the result "importance" — a category error the field mostly declines to name, because "importance" sells better than "accumulated-endorsement-so-far." Second, and more structural: the entire computation — BFS, Dijkstra, PageRank — operates *only* on the graph as given. A node that was never linked to is not a low-ranked node in the graph; it **is not in the graph**. There is no "rank zero" bucket for the unlinked — they are absent from V entirely, which means every pricing table, every traversal, every convergence guarantee in this module is scoped to "the part of the world someone thought to link." The performance-purist counter-culture, fixated on making Dijkstra and power iteration faster, has nothing to say about this — speed is orthogonal to completeness, and neither camp treats "who got left out of V" as an engineering question, though it plainly is one: it's a decision made by *whoever chose which edges to crawl or record*, upstream of any algorithm in this module.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** You built the traversal and the ranking; you know exactly what each one computes. BFS finds the truly shortest path *among nodes in the graph*. Dijkstra finds the truly cheapest path *among edges you recorded*. PageRank computes the mathematically correct dominant eigenvector *of the matrix you built*. Every one of these guarantees is airtight — conditional on `V` and `E` being complete. But `V` and `E` are never complete; they are *whatever got crawled, cited, linked, or logged* — and the module's own PageRank example already showed that being linked from an important node matters far more than being linked from many unimportant ones, which means a node's absence from a *few* well-connected sources' link lists — a citation index that stopped tracking a smaller journal, a hyperlink crawler that never found a site behind an unusual URL scheme, a knowledge graph built from a corpus that skipped a language — doesn't just lower that node's rank. It can erase it, because a node with **zero** recorded in-links isn't ranked low by PageRank; it may not be a node in your graph at all. So: your graph doesn't fail to rank someone highly. It fails to *contain* them, silently, upstream of every algorithm you just proved correct — and correctness is exactly what makes the omission invisible, because a correct BFS, a correct Dijkstra, a correct PageRank all return confidently, with no error, no low score, no signal that anything is missing. What would it even look like to notice that your graph is missing a node you never crawled the edge to — and whose job, in the systems you'll build after this course, is it to go looking?

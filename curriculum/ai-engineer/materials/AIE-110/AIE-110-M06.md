## Lessons

Four lessons, one structure each, in the order you'd reach for them: first the sample (keep a fair few when you can't keep all), then set-membership under a bit budget (Bloom), then frequency under a bit budget (Count-Min), then the hardest one — distinct-count under almost no budget at all (HyperLogLog).

1. **The stream model and reservoir sampling** — why "hold everything, then process" stops being an option, and how to keep a uniformly random sample of size k from a stream of unknown, unbounded length in one pass.
2. **The Bloom filter — set membership under a byte budget** — a bit array and k hash functions; zero false negatives, a tunable false-positive rate you can predict from three numbers before you build anything.
3. **The Count-Min sketch — frequency estimation that only ever overestimates** — a small grid of counters that answers "how many times have I seen this key" with a provable, one-directional error bound, no matter how many distinct keys there are.
4. **HyperLogLog — counting distinct items with almost no memory** — the bit-pattern trick that turns "how many *different* things have I seen" into an estimate with predictable statistical error, in kilobytes, for billions of items.

### Lesson 1 — The stream model and reservoir sampling

#### Teaching text

Every structure before this module assumed you could, at some cost, hold the data: M02's hash table holds every key; M03's heap holds the k survivors but still needs to *see* all N candidates from something that has all N candidates. Now break that assumption on purpose. A **stream** is a sequence you see once, in order, of a length you may not know in advance and may never finish (a Kafka topic, a day of request logs, `for line in open(huge_file)`). The rule for this whole module: **your memory footprint must not depend on N.** Not "small for typical N" — genuinely bounded, fixed at design time, true whether the stream has a thousand items or a trillion.

The first version of this problem: you want a **uniformly random sample of size k** from the stream — for a training-data spot check, an A/B eyeball, an outlier hunt — but you don't know how long the stream is until it ends, and you can't hold it all to shuffle-then-slice. The naive answer, "keep every N/k-th item," needs N up front (you don't have it) and is **not uniform** if the stream has any periodic structure (every 1000th log line might disproportionately be a batch-job heartbeat). You need something that is exactly uniform, one pass, O(k) memory, and correct without ever knowing n.

That something is **reservoir sampling (Algorithm R, Vitter 1985)**. Keep an array — the *reservoir* — of size k. Fill it with the first k items unconditionally. For every item after that, at position i (0-indexed, so this is the (i+1)-th item), draw a uniformly random integer j in `[0, i]`; if `j < k`, overwrite reservoir slot j with the new item; otherwise discard it. That's the whole algorithm — one `if`, one random draw, per item, forever.

**Why this is exactly uniform — the fact worth proving, not just trusting.** Consider item at position i (0-indexed). If i < k, it starts in the reservoir with probability 1. It survives to the end only if none of the later items evict it. Item t (t > i) evicts slot-holder with probability `k/(t+1)` (it lands anywhere in `[0,t]` and there are k target slots) times `1/k` (it must specifically land on *this* item's slot) — so item at position i survives step t with probability `1 - 1/(t+1)` = `t/(t+1)`. Chain that across every step from `i` to `n-1` and the products **telescope**:

```
P(survive) = (k/(k+1)) · (k+1)/(k+2)) · ... · ((n-1)/n)   [items i < k]
           = k/n                                          [everything cancels but k and n]
```

Every item, wherever it sits in the stream — first, last, or anywhere between — ends with **exactly probability k/n** of being in the final reservoir. This is the counterintuitive part worth sitting with: an item near the *end* of the stream is written to the reservoir with high probability at the moment it arrives (its per-step inclusion chance `k/(i+1)` is large when `i` is large) but has almost no time left to get evicted; an item near the *start* has a tiny window where it's guaranteed in, then a long gauntlet of eviction chances. The two effects exactly cancel, for every position, and the proof — not intuition — is why.

**Re-ascend.** The reason this belongs next to a hash table and a heap: it is the third answer to "I can't hold N" alongside "keep the k best" (M03's heap) and "keep the k members" (this module's Bloom filter, next). Reservoir sampling is "keep k *uniformly random representatives*" — a different question, same constraint, same one-pass discipline.

#### Worked example — trace the telescoping proof on n = 5, k = 1

Stream `[A, B, C, D, E]`, reservoir size k = 1. Trace `P(item survives to the end)` at each step, using the closed forms above.

| item (0-indexed i) | joins reservoir with prob. | survives step 1 | survives step 2 | ... | final P(in reservoir) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (i=0) | 1 (first k=1 items always join) | survives step i=1 w.p. 1/2 | survives step i=2 w.p. 2/3 | ... | 1 · 1/2 · 2/3 · 3/4 · 4/5 = **1/5** |
| B (i=1) | joins w.p. 1/2 (draws j∈[0,1], needs j<1) | — | survives step i=2 w.p. 2/3 | ... | 1/2 · 2/3 · 3/4 · 4/5 = **1/5** |
| C (i=2) | joins w.p. 1/3 | — | — | survives step i=3 w.p. 3/4 | 1/3 · 3/4 · 4/5 = **1/5** |
| D (i=3) | joins w.p. 1/4 | — | — | — | 1/4 · 4/5 = **1/5** |
| E (i=4, last) | joins w.p. 1/5 | — | — | — | **1/5** |

Every one of the five telescoping products collapses to exactly `1/5 = k/n`, despite completely different per-step trajectories — A had five chances to be evicted and only a 1-in-5 initial deal, E had one shot at 1-in-5 and no eviction risk at all. The algorithm doesn't "know" which item is early or late; the arithmetic just works out identically for all of them. That's the guarantee Lab M06-A measures.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"You need to know N in advance to get a fair k/N sample."** No — that's precisely the problem reservoir sampling solves. It is exactly uniform without ever knowing the stream's length, past or future, because the eviction probability at each step is computed only from *how far the stream has gotten so far* (`i`), never from how far it will go. Surface it by running the sampler over a Python generator with no `__len__` and no way to peek ahead, and getting a provably uniform sample anyway (Lab M06-A).
- **"Fixed-stride sampling (every Nth item) is basically the same thing."** It requires knowing (or guessing) N to pick the stride, and it is not uniform if the stream has *any* periodic or bursty structure correlated with position — a batch job that emits 10 log lines every hour will be wildly over- or under-represented depending on where the stride lands relative to the burst. Reservoir sampling has no such failure mode because its randomness, not the position parity, decides inclusion. Surface it by constructing a synthetic stream with a period-1000 spike and showing fixed-stride sampling captures the spike at a rate that depends on an arbitrary phase offset, while reservoir sampling doesn't.
- **"Early items are more likely to survive because they had first claim on the reservoir slots."** The opposite intuition is just as wrong ("late items survive more because they had less time to be evicted"). Neither is true — the telescoping proof shows every position ends at exactly k/n, and the worked example above makes it arithmetic, not faith. Surface it by having the learner compute the five telescoping products above by hand before running code; the "aha" is watching wildly different per-step trajectories collapse to the identical fraction.

### Lesson 2 — The Bloom filter: set membership under a byte budget

#### Teaching text

A hash **set** answers "have I seen this key?" exactly, but it must store every key it has seen — memory grows with N forever. A **Bloom filter** answers the same question approximately, in a **fixed** number of bits chosen once, that never grows no matter how many items you insert. The trade it makes, precisely: **zero false negatives, a tunable false-positive rate.** If the filter says "not present," the item was genuinely never inserted — that answer is always trustworthy. If it says "present," the item *usually* was inserted, but occasionally it's a false alarm — and you choose, at construction time, exactly how occasionally.

**The structure.** An `m`-bit array, all zero, plus `k` independent hash functions `h_1 … h_k`, each mapping any item to an index in `[0, m)`.

- **`add(x)`:** set bits `h_1(x), h_2(x), …, h_k(x)` to 1.
- **`x in filter`:** check bits `h_1(x), …, h_k(x)`. If **any** is 0, `x` was **definitely never inserted** — return False, and this answer can never be wrong (inserting `x` would have set that exact bit). If **all** are 1, `x` was **probably inserted** — return True, but some *other* combination of items could have coincidentally set all k of those same bits without `x` ever being added. That coincidence is the false positive.

**Predicting the false-positive rate — the formula that makes this module's Predict step possible.** After inserting `n` items into an `m`-bit filter with `k` hash functions, the probability any given bit is still 0 is approximately `e^(-kn/m)` (each of the `kn` bit-sets independently misses a given bit with probability `1 - 1/m`, and `(1-1/m)^(kn) ≈ e^(-kn/m)` for large m). The probability a *queried, never-inserted* item's `k` bits are **all** already set — a false positive — is that quantity's complement raised to the k:

```
p ≈ (1 - e^(-kn/m))^k
```

Two numbers are still yours to choose, `m` and `k`, and there is a genuine minimum of `p` as a function of `k` for fixed `m/n` — too few hash functions and you're not spreading the load; too many and every insert sets *more* bits, saturating the array faster than the extra checks help. Calculus on the formula above gives the optimum:

```
k* = (m/n) · ln 2                 (round to nearest integer, k ≥ 1)
p_min = (1/2)^k*  = 0.6185^(m/n)
```

And inverting that lets you size the filter **from a target false-positive rate**, before you insert anything:

```
m = ceil( -(n · ln p) / (ln 2)^2 )      bits, for n expected items and target rate p
```

**Re-ascend.** The reason a Bloom filter belongs in the same conversation as M02's hash table: it is the hash table's memory-bounded cousin, and it is honest about the price. A hash set of a million 64-bit IDs costs tens of megabytes and never lies. A Bloom filter sized for a million items at 1% false-positive costs about **1.14 MiB** and will occasionally, predictably, say yes to something it never saw. You buy roughly an order of magnitude less memory for a bounded, one-directional lie — and the whole point of the Predict step in this module is that you know the size of the lie *before* you accept the trade, not after a production incident tells you.

#### Worked example — size a filter for one million items, one percent false positives, then confirm the arithmetic

Target: `n = 1,000,000` expected items, `p = 0.01` (1% false-positive rate).

```
m = ceil(-(n · ln p) / (ln 2)^2)
  = ceil(-(1,000,000 · ln 0.01) / (ln 2)^2)
  = ceil(-(1,000,000 · (-4.60517)) / 0.480453)
  = ceil(4,605,170 / 0.480453)
  ≈ 9,585,059 bits  ≈ 1,198,132 bytes  ≈ 1.14 MiB

k* = round((m/n) · ln 2) = round(9.585 · 0.6931) = round(6.644) = 7
```

`m/n ≈ 9.585 bits per item, k = 7` — this is not a coincidence you should have to trust; it is the widely cited reference pair for a 1% Bloom filter, and it's worth reproducing by hand once. Now confirm the false-positive formula predicts what it claims: plug `k=7`, `m=9,585,059`, `n=1,000,000` back into `p ≈ (1 - e^(-kn/m))^k`:

```
kn/m = 7 · 1,000,000 / 9,585,059 ≈ 0.7302
e^(-0.7302) ≈ 0.4819
1 - 0.4819 = 0.5181
0.5181^7 ≈ 0.01002
```

The formula predicts **1.002% ≈ 1%**, matching the design target almost exactly — this is the number Lab M06-B measures against, by querying 100,000 known-absent items and counting how many the filter falsely admits.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"You can delete an item by clearing its bits."** You cannot, not with a plain Bloom filter — the bits you'd clear are almost certainly shared with other, still-present items (that's the entire mechanism of the false-positive rate), so clearing them silently introduces **false negatives** for those other items, breaking the one guarantee the structure makes. Deletion needs a different structure (a *counting* Bloom filter, which stores small counters instead of bits). Surface it by inserting two items that collide on a bit, "deleting" one by zeroing its bits, and watching the other item incorrectly report absent.
- **"The false-positive rate is a fixed property of the filter, set once."** It's a property of the filter **at the load it was sized for** — `p` depends on `n`, the *actual* number of items inserted, not the design capacity. Insert past the design `n` and `p` climbs, faster than linearly, because the exponent `kn/m` grows and the whole curve is exponential in it. Surface it by measuring `p` at 1×, then 3× the design load on the same filter (Lab M06-B) — a 3× overload does not give ~3× the false-positive rate; it gives roughly 40×–45× (the exact figure depends on the design point, but it is never close to linear).
- **"More hash functions always lowers the false-positive rate."** Only up to `k* = (m/n) ln 2`; beyond that, each insert sets more bits per item, saturating the array faster than the extra independent checks help, and `p` rises again — the curve is U-shaped in `k`, not monotonically decreasing. Surface it by computing `p` for `k = 20` at the same `m, n` above and watching it come out *worse* than `k = 7`, not better.

### Lesson 3 — The Count-Min sketch: frequency estimation that only ever overestimates

#### Teaching text

A hash table mapping key → count answers "how many times have I seen this key?" exactly, but — same story — it must store one entry per **distinct** key, forever. When the distinct-key space is enormous (every IP address that ever hit an endpoint, every token in a corpus, every user ID in a stream of events), you want an approximate frequency counter with a **fixed** memory budget, independent of how many distinct keys show up. That's the **Count-Min sketch**: a small 2D grid of counters, `d` rows by `w` columns, plus `d` independent hash functions (one per row).

- **`add(x, c=1)`:** for each row `r`, hash `x` to a column with that row's hash function, and add `c` to `table[r][col]`.
- **`estimate(x)`:** for each row `r`, look up `table[r][hash_r(x)]`; return the **minimum** across all `d` rows.

**Why the estimate is never below the truth.** Every cell a key hashes into accumulates *at least* that key's own true count — other keys that happen to hash to the same cell only add *more* on top; increments are never subtracted, so no cell for key `x` can ever read below `x`'s true count. Taking the **minimum across rows** is the whole trick: each row uses an independent hash function, so a collision that inflates `x`'s cell in row 1 (some heavy hitter landed on the same column) is very unlikely to be the *same* collision in row 2 or row 3. The row least polluted by collisions gives the tightest — but still never-too-low — estimate. This is a **one-directional** error, structurally, by construction: `true_count(x) ≤ estimate(x)`, always, with probability 1. There is no symmetric "sometimes over, sometimes under" the way a naive hash-with-collisions might suggest.

**Sizing from an error budget.** Given a desired error `ε` (as a fraction of the total stream weight `N`, the sum of all counts ever added) and a desired confidence `1 - δ`:

```
w = ceil(e / ε)          columns (e = 2.71828…, Euler's number)
d = ceil(ln(1 / δ))      rows
```

guarantees `estimate(x) ≤ true_count(x) + ε·N` with probability at least `1 - δ`, for **every** key, **simultaneously**, regardless of how many distinct keys exist. Note what's absent from that formula: the number of distinct keys. A stream with ten distinct keys and a stream with ten billion distinct keys, at the same `ε, δ`, get the identical table size.

**Re-ascend.** Count-Min is the frequency-domain sibling of the Bloom filter's membership-domain trick: both trade an unbounded, per-key data structure for a fixed grid plus hashing, and both accept a *one-directional* error in exchange — Bloom never says "absent" for something present; Count-Min never says "fewer" than the true count. That asymmetry, not randomness in general, is the load-bearing design decision in both, and it is exactly the thing Tier 2 of this module's reading asks you not to wave past.

#### Worked example — a tiny table, and watching the min recover an exact count despite two different collisions

Table: `d = 2` rows, `w = 4` columns, all zero. Stream: `cat, dog, cat, bird` (true counts: cat=2, dog=1, bird=1). Suppose the two hash functions map these keys to columns as follows (illustrative — the real code uses real hashes; the mechanics are what matter):

```
row 0:  cat -> col 1,  dog -> col 3,  bird -> col 1   (bird collides with cat in row 0)
row 1:  cat -> col 0,  dog -> col 0,  bird -> col 2   (dog collides with cat in row 1)
```

Insert `cat, dog, cat, bird` in order, incrementing both rows each time:

| after inserting | row0 = [c0,c1,c2,c3] | row1 = [c0,c1,c2,c3] |
|---|---|---|
| cat | [0,1,0,0] | [1,0,0,0] |
| dog | [0,1,0,1] | [2,0,0,0] |
| cat | [0,2,0,1] | [3,0,0,0] |
| bird | [0,**3**,0,1] | [3,0,**1**,0] |

Now query all three:

- `estimate(cat) = min(row0[1], row1[0]) = min(3, 3) = 3` — true count is 2. **Overestimated by 1**, because *both* rows happened to collide (bird in row 0, dog in row 1) — the min can't rescue an estimate when every row is polluted.
- `estimate(dog) = min(row0[3], row1[0]) = min(1, 3) = 1` — **exact**, matching the true count. Row 0's cell for dog (`col 3`) had no collision, so the min picked the clean row even though row 1's cell was polluted by cat.
- `estimate(bird) = min(row0[1], row1[2]) = min(3, 1) = 1` — **exact**, for the same reason from the other row.

Three keys, three different outcomes, and every single one obeys `estimate ≥ true_count`. This is the whole lesson in six numbers: **depth (`d`, the number of rows) is what protects you** — it gives collision noise multiple independent chances to *not* all land on the same key in every row — while **width (`w`, the number of columns) is what shrinks the size of the noise** when a collision does slip through every row.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"Hash collisions can make the estimate come out too low."** Structurally impossible — every increment adds to a cell, never subtracts, so every cell that a key touches holds *at least* that key's true count, and the minimum of several such cells is still at least the true count. Surface it by running thousands of random insert/query sequences and confirming `estimate(x) - true_count(x)` is never negative, only ever `≥ 0`.
- **"More rows (depth) shrinks the size of the error."** Depth controls the **probability** the error bound holds (the `δ` term — how confident you are), not the **magnitude** of the error when it does slip (the `ε·N` term, which is `w`'s job). Doubling `d` makes a bad estimate less *likely*; doubling `w` makes a bad estimate *smaller* when it happens. Surface it by fixing `w` and increasing `d`, and watching the worst-case overestimate magnitude barely move while the fraction of "way off" trials keeps dropping.
- **"A Count-Min sketch is a compressed hash map — I could enumerate the keys it has seen."** No — the sketch never stores identities, only aggregated numbers in cells shared across unknown numbers of unknown keys; you can query the estimated count of a key **you already know to ask about**, but there is no operation that lists "what keys were inserted." That's a strictly different capability from a dictionary, not a compressed version of one. Surface it by trying to write `keys()` for the sketch and hitting the wall that the grid holds no key material at all.

### Lesson 4 — HyperLogLog: counting distinct items with almost no memory

#### Teaching text

The hardest version of "I can't hold N": **how many *distinct* items were in the stream?** (`SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) ...` over a day of events; "how many unique documents did we ingest this hour.") The exact answer needs a set — one entry per distinct item, memory growing with the *distinct* count, not the stream length, which is still unbounded. **HyperLogLog (Flajolet et al., 2007)** estimates it in a **fixed** register array — kilobytes — with a **predictable statistical error that does not depend on the true cardinality at all.**

**The trick, in one sentence.** Hash every item to a long, uniformly-distributed bit string. A uniformly random bit string starts with `k` zeros before its first 1-bit with probability `2^-k` — so if you've hashed `n` distinct items and the *longest* run of leading zeros you've observed across all of them is `R`, then `n` is very likely around `2^R` (it took roughly `2^R` independent draws to have decent odds of seeing an `R`-zero run at least once). That's a real but **high-variance** estimator from a single number — one lucky or unlucky hash can throw it off by a factor of two. HyperLogLog's actual contribution is **fixing the variance**, not the trick itself.

**Bucketing (stochastic averaging).** Instead of tracking one longest-run over the whole stream, split the hash into two parts: the low `b` bits pick one of `m = 2^b` **registers** (buckets), and the *remaining* bits are used to compute the leading-zero-run for that draw. Each register independently tracks the longest run it has ever seen among the items routed to it. You now have `m` independent, low-precision cardinality estimators — one per register — covering non-overlapping roughly-`n/m`-sized shares of the input, and you combine them with a (harmonic-mean-based) formula that suppresses the effect of any single unlucky register:

```
Z = Σ 2^(-register[j])                    for j = 0 .. m-1
E = α_m · m^2 / Z                          α_m ≈ 0.7213 / (1 + 1.079/m)  for m ≥ 128
```

(A harmonic-style mean, not an arithmetic one, because a single register that got a lucky long run would otherwise blow up an arithmetic average — the harmonic form is dominated by the *typical* registers, not the outlier.) The registers themselves store almost nothing: for a 64-bit hash and `b`-bit bucket selection, a register only needs to hold a rank up to `64 - b + 1`, which fits in about 6 bits for realistic `b`. `m = 1024` registers at 6 bits each is **768 bytes** — to estimate the distinct count of a stream of *any* length, a billion or a trillion items, with the same 768 bytes either way.

**The error is predictable, and it is the number this module's Predict step turns into a real inequality:**

```
standard_error ≈ 1.04 / sqrt(m)
```

For `m = 1024` (`b = 10`): `1.04 / 32 ≈ 3.25%`. That's it — that number does not depend on whether the true cardinality is a thousand or a billion; it depends only on how many registers you paid for. Double `m` (one more bit of `b`, double the memory) and the error shrinks by `1/√2 ≈ 29%` — the familiar diminishing-returns shape of averaging over more independent samples.

**Re-ascend.** A Bloom filter answers "was this specific item ever inserted" and cannot count distinct items. Count-Min answers "how many times was this specific key seen" and cannot count distinct keys either. HyperLogLog answers neither of those — it cannot tell you whether any particular item was in the stream at all, because it never stores anything item-specific, only the *shape* of the longest runs it happened to see. Three sketches, three genuinely different questions, three different fixed-size structures — conflating any two of them is the single most common HyperLogLog misconception, addressed below.

#### Worked example — trace four registers by hand, then scale to production numbers

**Toy mechanics walkthrough (`b = 2`, `m = 4` registers)** — small enough to hand-trace the bit arithmetic, too small to trust for real accuracy claims (that's what the production numbers below are for). Assume six items hash to these illustrative 4-bit values (low 2 bits pick the register, top 2 bits give the run):

| item | 4-bit hash | register (low 2 bits) | remaining bits `w` | rank = leading-zero-run of `w` (2-bit width) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | `0101` | 1 | `01` | bit_length(1)=1 → rank = 2−1+1 = **2** |
| B | `0110` | 2 | `01` | **2** |
| C | `1001` | 1 | `10` | bit_length(2)=2 → rank = 2−2+1 = **1** |
| D | `0011` | 3 | `00` | w=0 → rank = width+1 = **3** |
| E | `1101` | 1 | `11` | bit_length(3)=2 → rank = **1** |
| F | `0001` | 1 | `00` | w=0 → rank = **3** |

Register `1` sees items A(rank2), C(rank1), E(rank1), F(rank3) and keeps the **max**: `register[1] = 3`. Register `2` sees only B: `register[2] = 2`. Register `3` sees only D: `register[3] = 3`. Register `0` sees nothing: stays `0`.

Final registers `[0, 3, 2, 3]`. `Z = 2^0 + 2^-3 + 2^-2 + 2^-3 = 1 + 0.125 + 0.25 + 0.125 = 1.5`. Using the general-`m` alpha, `α_4 ≈ 0.7213/(1+1.079/4) ≈ 0.568`, raw `E = 0.568 · 16 / 1.5 ≈ 6.06`. Because `E ≤ 2.5m = 10`, apply the small-range correction (linear counting on the zero registers): one register (`0`) is still zero, so `E = m · ln(m / zeros) = 4 · ln(4/1) = 4 · 1.386 ≈ 5.55`. **True distinct count: 6.** A four-register toy landed within one item of the truth — a lucky illustration of the mechanics, *not* evidence that `m=4` is usable in production (its real standard error, `1.04/√4 ≈ 52%`, would swing wildly on a different six items).

**Production numbers, `b = 10` (`m = 1024`):** standard error `1.04/√1024 = 3.25%`. At a true cardinality of 1,000,000, expect the estimate within **±3.25%** (≈ 967,500–1,032,500) roughly 68% of the time, and within **±6.5%** roughly 95% of the time. Register cost: `768 bytes` total. A Python `set` holding a million distinct 20-byte string IDs costs on the order of tens of megabytes (each string object plus the set's hash-table overhead) — HyperLogLog is **roughly four to five orders of magnitude smaller**, and unlike the set, it stays at 768 bytes if the true count is a billion instead of a million.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"HyperLogLog gives an exact count, just represented compactly."** It is a statistical estimator with a known, nonzero standard error (`1.04/√m`), not a lossless compression of the true count — the register array genuinely cannot be decompressed back into an exact answer, because the information about *which specific items* produced each register's max was discarded the moment a smaller rank was overwritten. Surface it by adding the same items to two identically-configured HLLs and getting two slightly different estimates depending only on hash-seed/insertion-detail noise, then confirming both fall within the predicted error band.
- **"Bigger `m` is free — just always use more registers."** More registers linearly costs more memory (`m` registers, roughly proportional bytes) for a `1/√m` return — quadrupling `m` only halves the error. For most production error budgets (a few percent is plenty for a distinct-user dashboard), `m` in the thousands is already past the point of obviously diminishing returns, and every extra register also costs more work to merge two sketches together (a real operation: unioning two HLLs is a register-wise max, and larger registers means more work per merge). Surface it by plotting predicted standard error against `m` and watching it flatten.
- **"HyperLogLog can tell me whether a specific item was in the stream."** It cannot, structurally — no register records which items touched it, only the longest zero-run observed, and multiple totally different items produce the identical register update if their hashes happen to have the same-length run. That question is a Bloom filter's job, and the two structures answer genuinely disjoint questions — conflating them (e.g., trying to use one HLL to do both distinct-counting *and* membership) throws away the property that made either one small. Surface it by asking the learner to implement `__contains__` on a HyperLogLog and watching them discover there's no information left to answer it with.

```python
import random

def reservoir_sample(stream, k, rng=None):
    """Algorithm R (Vitter, 1985). One pass, O(k) memory, no need to know
    len(stream) in advance. Returns a uniform-random sample of size
    min(k, n) from stream, where n is however many items stream produces."""
    rng = rng or random.Random()
    reservoir = []
    for i, item in enumerate(stream):
        if i < k:
            reservoir.append(item)
        else:
            j = rng.randint(0, i)      # uniform in [0, i], inclusive
            if j < k:
                reservoir[j] = item
    return reservoir


def reservoir_sample_with_stats(stream, k, rng=None):
    """Same algorithm, also returns the number of slot replacements made
    (writes after the first k) and the total items processed, for
    verifying the k*ln(n/k) prediction."""
    rng = rng or random.Random()
    reservoir = []
    writes = 0
    n = 0
    for i, item in enumerate(stream):
        n += 1
        if i < k:
            reservoir.append(item)
        else:
            j = rng.randint(0, i)
            if j < k:
                reservoir[j] = item
                writes += 1
    return reservoir, writes, n
```

`bloom.py`:

```python
import hashlib
import math

class BloomFilter:
    """Fixed-size bit array + k hash functions. No false negatives, ever;
    a tunable false-positive rate. Sizing (Lesson 2):
        m = ceil(-n * ln(p) / (ln 2)^2)      bits, for n expected items, target p
        k = round((m / n) * ln 2)            hash functions
    """

    def __init__(self, expected_items: int, target_fp_rate: float):
        self.n_expected = expected_items
        self.p_target = target_fp_rate
        self.m = self._optimal_m(expected_items, target_fp_rate)
        self.k = self._optimal_k(self.m, expected_items)
        self.bits = bytearray((self.m + 7) // 8)
        self.n_inserted = 0

    @staticmethod
    def _optimal_m(n, p):
        return max(8, math.ceil(-(n * math.log(p)) / (math.log(2) ** 2)))

    @staticmethod
    def _optimal_k(m, n):
        return max(1, round((m / n) * math.log(2)))

    def _slots(self, item):
        data = repr(item).encode("utf-8")
        h1 = int.from_bytes(hashlib.md5(data).digest()[:8], "little")
        h2 = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha1(data).digest()[:8], "little")
        for i in range(self.k):
            # Kirsch-Mitzenmacher double hashing: k "independent enough"
            # hashes derived from two real ones, no need for k distinct
            # hash function implementations.
            yield (h1 + i * h2) % self.m

    def add(self, item):
        for idx in self._slots(item):
            self.bits[idx // 8] |= (1 << (idx % 8))
        self.n_inserted += 1

    def __contains__(self, item):
        return all(self.bits[idx // 8] & (1 << (idx % 8)) for idx in self._slots(item))

    def predicted_fp_rate(self, n=None):
        """The Lesson 2 formula, evaluated at n items actually inserted."""
        n = self.n_inserted if n is None else n
        return (1 - math.exp(-self.k * n / self.m)) ** self.k
```

`countmin.py`:

```python
import hashlib
import math


class CountMinSketch:
    """Fixed-size d x w grid of counters. Increments only ever add, so
    estimate(x) >= true_count(x) always (Lesson 3). Sizing:
        w = ceil(e / epsilon)      columns, e = 2.71828... (Euler's number)
        d = ceil(ln(1 / delta))    rows
    guarantees estimate(x) <= true_count(x) + epsilon * N with probability
    at least 1 - delta, for every key, simultaneously, independent of how
    many distinct keys exist.
    """

    def __init__(self, epsilon: float, delta: float):
        self.epsilon = epsilon
        self.delta = delta
        self.w = math.ceil(math.e / epsilon)
        self.d = math.ceil(math.log(1 / delta))
        self.table = [[0] * self.w for _ in range(self.d)]
        self.total = 0  # N: sum of all counts ever added, across all keys

    def _col(self, item, row: int) -> int:
        data = f"{row}:{item!r}".encode("utf-8")
        h = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(data).digest()[:8], "little")
        return h % self.w

    def add(self, item, c: int = 1) -> None:
        for row in range(self.d):
            self.table[row][self._col(item, row)] += c
        self.total += c

    def estimate(self, item) -> int:
        return min(self.table[row][self._col(item, row)] for row in range(self.d))

    def memory_bytes(self) -> int:
        """w*d fixed-width 8-byte counters -- the number Predict Q2 asks for."""
        return self.w * self.d * 8
```

`hll.py`:

```python
import hashlib
import math

class HyperLogLog:
    """Cardinality (distinct-count) estimator. m = 2**b registers, each
    storing the longest leading-zero run seen in the (bucketed) hash of any
    item routed to it. Standard error ~= 1.04 / sqrt(m), independent of the
    true cardinality and of how many times any item repeats."""

    def __init__(self, b: int = 10):
        self.b = b
        self.m = 1 << b
        self.registers = [0] * self.m
        self.alpha = self._alpha(self.m)

    @staticmethod
    def _alpha(m):
        if m == 16:
            return 0.673
        if m == 32:
            return 0.697
        if m == 64:
            return 0.709
        return 0.7213 / (1 + 1.079 / m)

    def _hash64(self, item):
        data = repr(item).encode("utf-8")
        return int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha1(data).digest()[:8], "big")

    @staticmethod
    def _leading_zero_run(w, width):
        """1-indexed position of the leftmost 1-bit in a `width`-bit value."""
        if w == 0:
            return width + 1
        return width - w.bit_length() + 1

    def add(self, item):
        x = self._hash64(item)
        idx = x & (self.m - 1)            # low b bits pick the register
        w = x >> self.b                   # remaining (64 - b) bits
        rank = self._leading_zero_run(w, 64 - self.b)
        if rank > self.registers[idx]:
            self.registers[idx] = rank

    def cardinality(self) -> float:
        Z = sum(2.0 ** -r for r in self.registers)
        E = self.alpha * (self.m ** 2) / Z
        if E <= 2.5 * self.m:                       # small-range correction
            zeros = self.registers.count(0)
            if zeros:
                E = self.m * math.log(self.m / zeros)
        return E

    def standard_error(self) -> float:
        return 1.04 / math.sqrt(self.m)

    def register_bytes(self) -> int:
        max_rank = 64 - self.b + 1
        bits_per_register = max_rank.bit_length()
        return math.ceil(self.m * bits_per_register / 8)
```

## Three-tier reading — sketches, and who the approximation is wrong about

**Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).**
Reservoir sampling (Algorithm R) produces a provably uniform sample of size k from a stream of unknown length in one pass and O(k) memory — the telescoping proof in Lesson 1 shows every stream position ends at exactly probability k/n, verified empirically in Lab M06-A. A Bloom filter is an m-bit array plus k hash functions; it has **zero false negatives, structurally** (a bit is only ever set, never cleared, by every insertion of every item that touches it) and a false-positive rate `p ≈ (1 - e^(-kn/m))^k` that is tunable at design time and measurable after the fact, confirmed to within tolerance for a 1,000,000-item, 1%-target filter in Lab M06-B. A Count-Min sketch is a `w × d` grid of counters; because every update only adds, never subtracts, `estimate(x) ≥ true_count(x)` **always**, with a magnitude bound `ε·N` from the width and a confidence bound `1-δ` from the depth, demonstrated on a hand-traced six-cell table where the minimum-across-rows recovers two of three exact counts despite two independent collisions, and confirmed at scale in Lab M06-C, where a real sketch's measured overestimate stays inside the `ε·N` bound and an isolated 100,000-count heavy hitter survives 1,000 rare-key collisions with its estimate essentially untouched. HyperLogLog estimates distinct count from the longest observed run of leading zeros in a hashed bit string, bucketed across `m = 2^b` registers to control variance, with standard error `1.04/√m` — a number that does not depend on the true cardinality, only on the memory paid — verified against a known exact count in Lab M06-D, at 768 bytes for `m=1024` versus tens of megabytes for the exact `set()` it replaces at the same scale, and a memory gap that only widens as N grows because the sketch's footprint doesn't move.

**Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.**
"Just use a sketch, approximate is basically free at this scale" is the booster line, and it hides two things this module's formulas make hard to unsee. First: none of these errors are *symmetric noise* the way "measurement error" sounds when the word "approximate" gets used loosely. A Bloom filter's error only ever says yes when the truth is no — never the reverse — which means whatever decision sits downstream of "is this on the list" is *structurally biased toward inclusion*, not toward a coin-flip; if that filter guards a blocklist rather than a dedup check, the bias runs exactly backwards from what a security engineer would want, and "it's only a 1% error rate" is describing the wrong shape of error to be reassuring about. Count-Min's overestimate is the same one-directional story pointed at rate limits and heavy-hitter detection: every estimate creeps upward, never downward, so a system built on it will over-report load, over-throttle, and over-flag — never under —, and "the error averages out" is not a sentence that formula supports. Second, and quieter: "1% false-positive rate" sounds small precisely because it's phrased as a percentage, and percentages hide absolute counts at the scale these structures are built for. A Bloom filter serving a billion queries a day at a 1% false-positive rate produces **ten million** wrong "yes" answers daily — small as a fraction, enormous as a headcount, and the percentage framing is doing real rhetorical work to make that headcount invisible.

**Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you cannot un-ask).**
Every sketch in this module buys its bounded memory by refusing to keep a receipt. A hash set that says "yes, X is a member" can, if asked, also say *why* — X is sitting right there, an entry you could point to. A Bloom filter that says "yes" cannot: the bits that triggered the answer are shared with an unknown number of other items, by design, and there is no operation that recovers which of them actually caused this particular "yes." That is not an implementation gap to be fixed in a future version — it is the entire mechanism by which the structure stays small. So: when a Bloom-filter-backed blocklist falsely flags a legitimate account because that account's hash happens to collide with a bad actor's, the block is not a one-time unlucky event — it is **deterministic and permanent**, the exact same collision, every single time that account is checked, for as long as the filter's parameters don't change. And the system has no way to discover this happened, because discovering it would require keeping the very thing the sketch was built specifically to not keep. The same shape recurs in Count-Min (whichever key's true count gets silently inflated by collision with a heavier key stays inflated, invisibly, and the sketch offers no operation to ask "was my count polluted") and in HyperLogLog (the distinct entity whose hash landed on a register that a *different* entity's hash later overwrote with a longer run is not undercounted in a way anyone can trace back to them specifically — the register just doesn't remember). Ask the question the way this module's labs made you answer it for the exam: not "is there some error," but *who, specifically, and would the system you just built even have the evidence to tell them if they asked?* If the honest answer is "no, and that's not a bug, that's the compression," what obligation does that put on you, the one person in the pipeline who understood the mechanism well enough to have predicted it — before you shipped the sketch that made the receipt impossible to produce?

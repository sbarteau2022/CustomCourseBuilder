## Lessons

Four lessons. The first turns Big-O into money. The second says the money has a hidden exchange rate (the constant). The third pays for a "free" operation you use constantly (amortized append). The fourth hands you the instrument to read any curve you didn't write.

1. **Big-O is a spending rate** — convert a complexity class into wall-clock and bytes at 10^6, 10^9, 10^12.
2. **The constant is the exchange rate** — why the lower Big-O only wins *past a crossover*, and how to find it.
3. **Amortized analysis** — the growable vector's doubling makes append O(1) *amortized*; the aggregate method proves it, and the naive +1 growth costs O(n²).
4. **Measure the curve, predict the fall** — the doubling test classifies a curve you didn't write, and turns "it's O(n)" into "it dies at N ≈ ____."

### Lesson 1 — Big-O is a spending rate, not an exam answer

#### Teaching text

In a data-structures class Big-O is a label you memorize: "lookup in a hash table is O(1), scanning a list is O(n)." That framing is inert — it tells you which is *asymptotically* better and nothing about whether either one fits in the time you have. We are going to use Big-O the way an engineer uses it: as a **spending rate**. You have a budget — some number of nanoseconds per request, some number of bytes of RAM — and the complexity class tells you *how fast your cost grows as the data grows*. The question is never "what is the Big-O"; it is **"at my N, can I afford it?"**

Fix an idealized hardware budget: a modern core does on the order of **10^9 simple operations per second**, so one operation costs about **1 nanosecond**. (Pure Python is 30–100× slower per operation — AIE-102's interpreter tax — so read every "1 ns" below as "~30–100 ns of real Python." We will keep the idealized number for the arithmetic and re-inflate it when it matters.) Now convert each complexity class into wall-clock time at three scales AI systems actually hit:

| Class | N = 10^6 | N = 10^9 | N = 10^12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| O(1) | ~1 ns | ~1 ns | ~1 ns |
| O(log₂ N) | ~20 ns | ~30 ns | ~40 ns |
| O(N) | ~1 ms | ~1 s | ~17 min |
| O(N log N) | ~20 ms | ~30 s | ~11 hours |
| O(N²) | ~17 min | ~32 years | ~30 million years |

Read that table as a **budget sheet**, not a math fact. Three things jump out:

- **O(1) and O(log N) don't move.** A hash lookup and a balanced-tree lookup cost essentially the same at a million items as at a trillion. This is why they are the load-bearing structures under everything at scale — they are the only classes whose cost is *flat* across the whole range that matters.
- **O(N) is the boundary of "one machine, interactively."** A linear scan of a million is a millisecond — fine. A linear scan of a *billion* is a full second — you just blew every interactive latency budget on Earth. Same code, same complexity class, and the difference between "instant" and "unusably slow" is nothing but N crossing from 10^6 to 10^9.
- **O(N²) is a cliff, not a slope.** At a million items an O(N²) algorithm already costs 17 minutes. At a billion it costs longer than humans have farmed. There is no faster computer that saves you: a 1000× faster machine turns 32 years into 12 days. O(N²) doesn't get *slow* at scale — it becomes *physically impossible*, and it does so suddenly.

This is the whole reframe. Big-O is not a grade you assign to an algorithm. It is the **slope of a spending curve**, and your job as an engineer is to know your N, look up the slope, and decide whether the bill at that N is one you can pay. Everything in AIE-110 — hashing, heaps, ANN indexes, sketches — is a technique for buying a *flatter slope* on some operation you can't afford at O(N).

#### Worked example — "will this scale" as a lookup, not a vibe

A colleague proposes: "For each incoming query embedding, scan all stored document embeddings and keep the closest. It's simple and it works." You have 50 million documents and a 200 ms latency budget per query. Does it scale? Don't argue about it — *price* it.

The proposal is O(N) per query: touch all N documents. Per document you do a dot product over, say, 768 dimensions — call it ~768 ns of real work per document (this is NumPy-vectorized, so near the 1 ns/op floor, not the 30–100× Python tax). So:

```
cost per query ≈ N × 768 ns
             = 50_000_000 × 768e-9 s
             ≈ 38 seconds per query
```

38 seconds against a 200 ms budget. It is off by **190×** — not a tuning problem, a *complexity-class* problem. No amount of faster hardware inside the next few years closes 190×. The answer isn't "optimize the loop"; it's "this operation must not be O(N)" — which is exactly why M05 builds an *approximate nearest-neighbor index* that answers in O(log N)-ish time. You just derived the entire motivation for the ANN index from one budget calculation, before writing any code.

Notice what made the argument decisive: you converted a complexity class into a **number with a unit** and compared it to a **budget with the same unit**. "It's O(N), that might be slow" persuades no one. "It's 38 seconds against a 200 ms budget" ends the meeting.

#### Misconceptions to surface (name them out loud)

- **"O(N²) is fine because my N is small."** Sometimes true — and the *only* way to know is to price it at your actual N and check it against a budget. The misconception isn't optimism; it's skipping the calculation. Surface it by making the learner compute the O(N²) cell for *their* N and compare to a stated budget. Small-N O(N²) is a *decision you defend with a number*, not a habit.
- **"Big-O tells me how long my program takes."** No — Big-O tells you the *shape of the growth*, not the wall-clock time. It deliberately throws away the constant. Two O(N) algorithms can differ by 100× in real time (Lesson 2). Big-O answers "how does cost scale with N," never "how many milliseconds."
- **"A faster computer fixes a bad complexity class."** No — it multiplies the whole curve by a constant, which barely dents an exponential gap. Surface it with the O(N²) row: a 1000× faster machine turns 32 years into 12 days, still hopeless. Hardware buys you a constant; the algorithm buys you the slope, and at scale the slope wins every time.

### Lesson 2 — The constant is the exchange rate

#### Teaching text

Big-O throws the constant away on purpose — that is what makes it *asymptotic*, a statement about behavior as N → ∞. But you do not run your code at infinity. You run it at *your* N, and at a finite N the discarded constant is the exchange rate between two currencies: an O(N log N) algorithm with a large constant and an O(N) algorithm with a small one might not cross until N is bigger than any input you will ever see. "Lower Big-O" is a claim about the *limit*; "faster at my N" is a claim about *right now*, and they are not the same claim.

Write the actual cost as `cost(N) ≈ c · f(N)`, where `f` is the complexity class and `c` is the constant hiding everything Big-O dropped: cache behavior, allocation, the interpreter tax, constant factors in the algorithm. Two algorithms cross where their costs are equal:

```
c_A · f_A(N) = c_B · f_B(N)   →   solve for the crossover N*
```

Below N\*, the algorithm with the *worse* asymptotic class can be faster, because its smaller constant hasn't been overwhelmed yet. Above N\*, the asymptotics take over and never give it back. This is not a curiosity — it is why real libraries are *hybrids*. CPython's own sort (Timsort) uses **insertion sort**, an O(N²) algorithm, for runs shorter than 64 elements, and only switches to the O(N log N) merge for larger runs. Insertion sort's constant is tiny; below the crossover it wins, and the "worse" algorithm is the *correct* choice. The engineer who sneers "O(N²), never" would make CPython's sort slower.

The trap runs the other way too. "I'll use the O(N log N) algorithm, it's better" can be wrong when N is always small and the O(N log N) method carries a heavy constant (recursion, allocation, a priority queue). Better *asymptotically* and better *at your N* are independent questions. You answer the first by reading the exponent. You answer the second by finding the crossover and checking which side of it you live on.

#### Worked example — where does linear scan beat the hash set?

Building a `set` from N items and then doing lookups is O(1) per lookup — but building the set costs real time (hashing every element, allocating buckets), and each lookup pays a hash computation. A linear scan of a list is O(N) per lookup but has a *tiny* per-element constant: in CPython, `x in list` is a tight C loop doing pointer comparison and `==`, no hashing. For a *single* lookup against a small list, the scan can win, because you skip building the set entirely.

Model one lookup, cold (you haven't pre-built anything):

- **List scan:** `cost_list(N) ≈ c_L · N`, with `c_L ≈ 10 ns` (C-level compare per element).
- **Build set + one lookup:** `cost_set(N) ≈ c_B · N + c_H`, with `c_B ≈ 60 ns` (hash + insert per element) and `c_H ≈ 50 ns` (one hashed lookup).

For a single lookup, building the set is *strictly worse* — `c_B · N` alone already exceeds `c_L · N` because 60 > 10. The set only pays off when you **amortize the build across many lookups**. With Q lookups:

```
Q · c_L · N     vs     c_B · N + Q · c_H     (build once, look up Q times)
```

The set wins once `Q · c_L · N > c_B · N + Q · c_H`, i.e. once you do enough lookups that the O(N)-per-lookup scan's growing cost overtakes the one-time build. At N = 10^4 and the constants above, the crossover is around **Q ≈ 1**–a handful: past a few lookups the set dominates and never looks back, which is why "if you look things up more than once, build the set" is a real rule. But for a *one-shot membership test on a short list*, the scan is genuinely the right call — the O(1) structure loses because you never amortized its construction. The exchange rate decided it, not the exponent.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"Lower Big-O always wins."** Only in the limit. Below the crossover N\*, the higher-Big-O algorithm with a smaller constant is faster — and for workloads that live entirely below N\* (Timsort's ≤64-element runs), it is the *correct* choice. Surface it by asking the learner to find N\* and then check which side their actual N is on.
- **"The constant doesn't matter, it's just a constant."** The constant is the entire difference between a pure-Python loop and a NumPy call at the *same* O(N) — a 30–100× gap (AIE-102). Big-O dropping the constant is a modeling convenience, not a claim that the constant is small. Surface it with two O(N) sums that differ by 50× in wall-clock.
- **"Timsort using insertion sort is a bug / legacy cruft."** It is a deliberate, measured hybrid: below the crossover the O(N²) method has the smaller constant and wins. Surface it by having the learner reason about why a general-purpose library would *choose* an O(N²) inner loop.

### Lesson 3 — Amortized analysis: paying for the "free" append

#### Teaching text

You call `list.append` constantly and think of it as O(1). Most of the time it is: there's a spare slot in the backing array, you drop the pointer in, done. But sometimes the array is *full*, and append must allocate a bigger array and **copy every existing element** into it — that single append is O(N). So how can we call append "O(1)"? The honest answer is a third kind of complexity, between best-case and worst-case: **amortized** cost — the average cost per operation across a whole *sequence* of operations, guaranteed, not probabilistic.

The trick that makes append cheap is the growth policy AIE-102 showed you: when the array fills, don't grow by one slot — **double the capacity** (CPython actually grows by ~1.125×, but doubling is the clean model and the same asymptotics). Doubling means the expensive copy-everything resizes get *exponentially rarer* as the array grows. Let's prove append is O(1) amortized with the **aggregate method**: add up the total cost of N appends and divide by N.

Suppose we append N items starting from capacity 1, doubling on each overflow. Resizes happen at sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, …, up to N. The copy cost of each resize equals the number of elements copied:

```
total copies = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... + (largest power of 2 ≤ N)
             < 2N                          (geometric series: sum of powers of 2 up to 2^k is 2^(k+1) − 1)
```

So across N appends, the *total* copying work is **less than 2N** — bounded by a constant times N. Divide by N: the amortized cost per append is **< 2 copies = O(1)**. Every append looks O(1) on average, *guaranteed*, even though individual appends occasionally cost O(N). The rare expensive resize is fully paid for by the many cheap appends before it — that is what "amortized" means: not "usually fast and we hope," but "the total is provably bounded, so the per-operation average is a constant."

Now watch what the *wrong* growth policy does. Grow by **one slot** each time the array fills, and every single append triggers a copy of everything:

```
total copies = 0 + 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + (N−1) = N(N−1)/2 ≈ N²/2   →   O(N²) total
```

Amortized cost per append: **N/2 = O(N)**. Same append operation, same interface, and the growth policy alone moves the amortized cost from O(1) to O(N) — turning a loop of N appends from O(N) into O(N²). This is why doubling is not an optimization detail; it is the difference between a growable array that scales and one that quietly becomes quadratic. When you build the hash table in M02 and it resizes, this is the machinery keeping insertion O(1) amortized.

#### Worked example — the receipt for 8 appends

Append 8 items, capacity starts at 1, doubling. Track the per-append cost (1 to store the new element, plus copies on resize):

| append # | array full before? | copies (resize) | store | this append's cost | running total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | yes (cap 1→ full) | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | yes (cap 1→2) | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 3 | yes (cap 2→4) | 2 | 1 | 3 | 6 |
| 4 | no | 0 | 1 | 1 | 7 |
| 5 | yes (cap 4→8) | 4 | 1 | 5 | 12 |
| 6 | no | 0 | 1 | 1 | 13 |
| 7 | no | 0 | 1 | 1 | 14 |
| 8 | no | 0 | 1 | 1 | 15 |

Total cost for 8 appends: **15**, which is < 2·8 = 16. Amortized: 15/8 ≈ 1.9 per append — a constant. Notice the *shape*: the expensive appends (cost 2, 3, 5) get rarer as you go, and long runs of cost-1 appends follow each resize. That is the geometric series working for you. Do the same table with +1 growth and every row has a resize; the total for 8 appends is 0+1+2+3+4+5+6+7 = 28, and it grows as N²/2 — the gap widens without bound.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"Amortized O(1) means every append is fast."** No — it means the *average over the sequence* is O(1); individual appends still occasionally cost O(N) (the resize). This matters for *latency*: if you need a hard per-operation bound (real-time systems), amortized O(1) is not good enough — one append can stall. Surface it by pointing at the cost-5 row: that append really was 5× a normal one.
- **"Amortized is the same as average-case."** No — average-case is *probabilistic* (expected cost over random inputs, could be unlucky). Amortized is a *guarantee* over any sequence: the total is bounded no matter what. Surface it by noting the doubling bound (< 2N) holds for *every* run, not just typical ones.
- **"Growing by one slot is basically the same, just a bit slower."** No — it changes the *class*: O(1) amortized becomes O(N) amortized, turning N appends from O(N) into O(N²). Surface it with the two total-copy formulas (< 2N vs N²/2) side by side and have the learner plug in N = 10^6.

### Lesson 4 — Measure the curve, predict the fall

#### Teaching text

You can read the Big-O off code you wrote. But most of the code you depend on you did *not* write — a library call, a colleague's function, a data structure whose internals you haven't opened. For those you need to recover the complexity class *empirically*, from measurement, and the cleanest instrument is the **doubling test**: measure the operation at N, then at 2N, and look at the **ratio** of the times. The ratio reveals the class, because it cancels the unknown constant:

```
ratio = T(2N) / T(N)
```

| If the ratio is about… | the class is… | because f(2N)/f(N) = … |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | O(1) | 1/1 |
| slightly > 1 (e.g. 1.05) | O(log N) | (log 2N)/(log N) → ~1 for large N |
| ~2 | O(N) | 2N/N |
| ~2.1 (just over 2) | O(N log N) | 2·(log 2N)/(log N) ≈ 2.05–2.2 |
| ~4 | O(N²) | (2N)²/N² |
| ~8 | O(N³) | (2N)³/N³ |

The constant `c` cancels in the ratio: `T(2N)/T(N) = c·f(2N) / c·f(N) = f(2N)/f(N)`. So you don't need to know the constant, the machine, or the language — double N, watch the ratio, read the class off the table. Do it across several doublings (N, 2N, 4N, 8N) and the ratios should *stabilize* on one row; if they drift (small at first, then climbing toward 2), you're watching a crossover — a lower-order term still dominating at small N, exactly Lesson 2's story showing up in the data.

Once you have the class *and* one measured point, you can do the thing this whole course is named for: **predict the fall**. "It's O(N), and at N = 10^6 it took 5 ms" plus the class lets you extrapolate to any N and find where it crosses a budget:

```
T(N) ≈ T(N₀) · f(N) / f(N₀)          (extrapolate using the class)
solve T(N) = budget   for   N        (the N at which it falls over)
```

There are two walls a structure hits. The **time wall**: the N where a single operation (or a batch) exceeds your latency budget. The **memory wall**: the N where the structure no longer fits in RAM and either crashes or starts swapping (which is a 1000×+ slowdown — the disk is that much slower than RAM, an AIE-102 result). A structure "falls over" at the *smaller* of the two Ns. A Python `list` of ints costs ~8 bytes per element just for the pointer array, *plus* ~28 bytes per distinct int object — call it up to ~36 bytes/element. On a 16 GB machine that memory wall is at N ≈ 16e9 / 36 ≈ **4×10^8**. So a list-based approach doesn't reach 10^9, let alone 10^12, no matter how fast the operation is — it runs out of *bytes* first. Predicting *which* wall you hit, and at what N, is the deliverable. "It's O(N)" is a class; "it dies at N ≈ 4×10^8 on the memory wall" is an engineering decision.

#### Worked example — classify and predict a mystery function

You're handed `mystery(data)` and told nothing. You time it:

| N | T(N) | ratio vs previous |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 11 ms | — |
| 20,000 | 44 ms | 4.0 |
| 40,000 | 176 ms | 4.0 |
| 80,000 | 705 ms | 4.0 |

The ratio is a rock-solid **4.0** across every doubling → **O(N²)**. (It doesn't drift, so no crossover is muddying it; we're safely in the asymptotic regime.) Now predict the fall. Suppose your budget is 1 second per call. Using T(N) ≈ T(N₀)·(N/N₀)²:

```
1000 ms = 11 ms · (N / 10_000)²
(N / 10_000)² = 90.9
N / 10_000 = 9.5
N ≈ 95_000
```

So this function falls over the 1-second time wall at **N ≈ 95,000** — you predicted it from four measurements at N ≤ 80,000, without ever running it near 95,000. If someone plans to call `mystery` on 10^6 items, you can tell them *now*, with a number, that it will take about 11 ms · (10^6/10^4)² = 11 ms · 10^4 = **110 seconds** per call, and hand them the doubling-test evidence. That is the instrument this module installs: not "that looks slow," but "it's O(N²), it dies at 95k against your budget, here are the ratios."

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"One benchmark tells me the complexity class."** No — a single (N, T) point tells you *nothing* about the class; the class lives in how T *changes* with N. You need at least two Ns, and really three-plus to see the ratio stabilize. Surface it by showing that a single 11 ms measurement is consistent with O(1), O(N), and O(N²) all at once — only the ratio disambiguates.
- **"A small ratio at small N means it's O(1) or O(log N)."** Not necessarily — it can be a crossover, where a lower-order term dominates until N grows past N\*, after which the ratio climbs to its true value. Surface it by measuring a hybrid and watching the ratio *drift upward* across doublings; a stable ratio is trustworthy, a drifting one is a warning.
- **"If the operation is fast enough, N can be anything."** No — the memory wall is independent of the time wall, and a structure can be O(1) per op yet still die at N ≈ 4×10^8 because it doesn't *fit in RAM*. Surface it by pricing the bytes-per-element and dividing into available memory; the fall is at the *smaller* of the two walls.

### Lab M01-A — The doubling test: classify a curve you didn't write *(core)*

**Goal:** recover a complexity class from measurement alone, using the ratio test, and — the graded part — commit to a class *and its ratio signature* before you see the numbers.

#### Predict (on paper, before running anything)

You are given four "mystery" functions (`mystery_a` … `mystery_d`) whose bodies are hidden from you at predict time. You are told only what each *does* at a high level:

- `mystery_a(data)` — returns the first element.
- `mystery_b(data)` — returns the sum of all elements.
- `mystery_c(data)` — returns a sorted copy.
- `mystery_d(data)` — returns the number of pairs `(i, j)` with `i < j` and `data[i] + data[j] == 0`, computed by checking every pair.

For **each** function, write down: (1) the complexity class you expect, (2) the doubling-test **ratio** it should produce (from the Lesson-4 table), and (3) one sentence of reasoning. Example format: *"`mystery_b`: O(N), ratio ≈ 2, because it touches every element once."*

Then rank the four fastest→slowest at N = 20,000. Seal it: `python -c "from m01harness import sealed_predict; sealed_predict('M01-A')"` and paste your sheet. It is now write-locked.

#### Build

Starter (`classify.py`):

```python
from m01harness import doubling_bench, report

# --- the "mystery" functions (you may read them AFTER sealing your prediction) ---

def mystery_a(data):
    return data[0]

def mystery_b(data):
    total = 0
    for x in data:
        total += x
    return total

def mystery_c(data):
    return sorted(data)

def mystery_d(data):
    n = len(data)
    count = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            if data[i] + data[j] == 0:
                count += 1
    return count

def make_input(n):
    # deterministic, mixed-sign so mystery_d finds some pairs
    return [((-1) ** k) * (k % 97) for k in range(n)]

def ratios(measurements):
    """measurements: [(N, seconds), ...] -> [(N, ratio_vs_previous), ...]"""
    out = []
    for i, (n, t) in enumerate(measurements):
        if i == 0:
            out.append((n, None))
        else:
            prev_t = measurements[i - 1][1]
            out.append((n, round(t / prev_t, 2) if prev_t > 0 else None))
    return out

def classify_all():
    # small Ns for the O(N^2) function so the lab finishes quickly
    ns_fast = [10_000, 20_000, 40_000, 80_000]
    ns_slow = [500, 1_000, 2_000, 4_000]
    results = {}
    for name, fn, ns in [
        ("mystery_a", mystery_a, ns_fast),
        ("mystery_b", mystery_b, ns_fast),
        ("mystery_c", mystery_c, ns_fast),
        ("mystery_d", mystery_d, ns_slow),
    ]:
        meas = doubling_bench(fn, [(n, make_input(n)) for n in ns])
        results[name] = ratios(meas)
    return results

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for name, r in classify_all().items():
        print(name)
        report(r)
```

Reference `m01harness.doubling_bench` (provided; shown so the lab is self-contained):

```python
import timeit

def doubling_bench(fn, cases, repeats=5, number=1):
    """cases: [(N, input_obj), ...] -> [(N, best_seconds), ...]."""
    out = []
    for n, arg in cases:
        t = min(
            timeit.timeit(lambda: fn(arg), number=number, globals=globals())
            for _ in range(repeats)
        ) / number
        out.append((n, t))
    return out
```

```python
class GrowableArray:
    """A minimal growable array that COUNTS element copies, to make the
    amortized argument measurable. `copies` is the total elements moved
    across all resizes."""

    def __init__(self, growth="double"):
        self._cap = 1
        self._n = 0
        self._buf = [None] * self._cap
        self.copies = 0
        self.resizes = 0
        self.growth = growth

    def _new_capacity(self):
        # TODO: return the new capacity when the buffer is full.
        #   "double"    -> at least twice current capacity
        #   "increment" -> current capacity + 1
        raise NotImplementedError

    def _grow(self):
        new_cap = self._new_capacity()
        new_buf = [None] * new_cap
        for i in range(self._n):        # copy every existing element
            new_buf[i] = self._buf[i]
            self.copies += 1            # count each element moved
        self._buf = new_buf
        self._cap = new_cap
        self.resizes += 1

    def append(self, x):
        if self._n == self._cap:
            self._grow()
        self._buf[self._n] = x
        self._n += 1

    def __len__(self):
        return self._n


def total_copies(n, growth):
    arr = GrowableArray(growth=growth)
    for i in range(n):
        arr.append(i)
    return arr.copies
```

```python
import sys
from m01harness import doubling_bench, report

def worst_case_list_lookup(container_and_key):
    container, key = container_and_key
    return key in container   # for a list, `in` is O(N); miss => scans all N

def make_list(n):
    data = list(range(n))
    missing = -1                # guaranteed absent -> worst case (full scan)
    return (data, missing)

def make_set(n):
    data = set(range(n))
    missing = -1
    return (data, missing)

def time_wall(kind, ns):
    factory = make_list if kind == "list" else make_set
    cases = [(n, factory(n)) for n in ns]
    return doubling_bench(worst_case_list_lookup, cases)

def bytes_per_element_list(n=100_000):
    """Empirical bytes/element for a list of distinct ints: pointer array + int objects."""
    data = list(range(n))
    ptr_bytes = sys.getsizeof(data)                       # the pointer array
    int_bytes = sum(sys.getsizeof(x) for x in data)       # the int objects
    return (ptr_bytes + int_bytes) / n

def predict_memory_wall(budget_bytes=16 * 1024**3):
    bpe = bytes_per_element_list()
    return int(budget_bytes / bpe)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("list lookup (worst case) timings:")
    report(time_wall("list", [10_000, 20_000, 40_000, 80_000]))
    print("set lookup timings:")
    report(time_wall("set", [10_000, 20_000, 40_000, 80_000]))
    print("bytes/element (list of distinct ints):", round(bytes_per_element_list(), 1))
    print("predicted memory wall (N):", predict_memory_wall())
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — *complexity as a budget, and who gets left out when the budget is tight* — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).** A complexity class converts to a wall-clock and memory bill, and you can compute the bill on your own laptop. At 1 ns/operation, an O(N²) algorithm costs ~17 minutes at a million items and ~32 years at a billion — a slope, not a constant, and no affordable hardware closes that gap. Append is O(1) *amortized* because doubling the backing array bounds total copies below 2N (a geometric series), while growing by one slot costs N(N−1)/2 copies — O(N²) total — and both bounds are exact, checkable with the copy-counting array in Lab M01-B. The doubling test recovers an unknown function's class from the ratio T(2N)/T(N) because the constant cancels. A Python list of distinct ints costs ~36 bytes/element, so a 16 GB machine hits its memory wall at N ≈ 4×10^8 — it cannot reach 10^9 by *bytes*, independent of speed. All of this is measured with `time`, `timeit`, and `sys.getsizeof`, which ship with the interpreter. It is documented, not opinion.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Premature optimization is the root of all evil" and "just make it work first" are true advice that quietly hides a second truth: **the complexity class is not an optimization, it is a design decision made before the first line runs**, and getting it wrong is not something you "optimize later" — it is something you *rewrite*, often after it has already failed someone in production. The booster culture that says "scale is a good problem to have, worry about it when you get there" cannot admit that O(N²) doesn't degrade gracefully — it works fine in the demo at N=1000 and becomes physically impossible at N=10^9, with no warning in between. The performance-purist counter-culture cannot admit that below the crossover, the "worse" algorithm is genuinely correct and their instinct to reach for the asymptotically-optimal structure wastes real effort. Both are half true. Big-O is neither a premature optimization to defer nor a purity test to pass — it is a *budget you either priced or didn't*, and the field's two loudest camps each have a reason not to say so.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** Every "efficient" system draws a budget line — a latency SLA, a memory ceiling, an index that fits in RAM — and everything on the wrong side of that line is *dropped*, not served slowly. When your retrieval index is fast because it's approximate, the speed is bought by *not finding* the hardest-to-find items: the rare document, the underrepresented name, the query that doesn't cluster near anything. The budget didn't fail — it worked exactly as designed, and the cost landed entirely on whoever sat past the line you couldn't afford to cross. So: when you choose a complexity class, you are choosing an N you will serve and an N at which the thing falls over — and *someone is always at the N you decided not to reach.* Whose retrieval did your budget silently decide wasn't worth the bytes? And will you ever see their name, given that the whole point of the budget was to never scan far enough to find it?

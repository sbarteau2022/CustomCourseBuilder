# AIE-110-M05 — Similarity search: exact to approximate nearest neighbors

**Course:** AIE-110 — Data Structures, Algorithms, and Scale · **Tier:** foundation · **Durability:** durable
**Hours:** 16 · **Sits in the descent:** the differentiator module. Every AI system you will ever build ends in the same operation — "given this query vector, find the k most similar things out of N" — and every RAG pipeline, every recommender, every dedup system, every semantic cache calls it thousands of times a second. We descend beneath `sklearn.neighbors` and beneath every vector-database "just call `.search()`" call to the two structures actually doing the work: the flat vectorized scan (exact, and it dies), and the index built to avoid scanning at all (approximate, and it can silently fail to find someone). You build both by hand, race them, and predict — before measuring — where the exact one dies and where the approximate one's recall falls off a cliff. The index you build here (`NSWIndex`) is the ANN component **AIE-302 (RAG) imports** to select the k nearest chunks against a real corpus.
**Prereq:** AIE-101 (Python + pytest fluent). **Co-req:** AIE-102 (the memory hierarchy). **Builds on AIE-110/M03** (the heap: `topk_heap`/`heapq.nlargest` is the exact top-k selector every brute-force query in this module still calls internally, and the interpreter-tax reversal you found there — asymptotically better, wall-clock slower, until you drop to C — reappears here in a sharper form: a sub-linear graph index, in pure Python, losing to a linear vectorized scan). This module cashes in three prior results: (1) the **interpreter tax** decides whether "fewer operations" beats "operations in C"; (2) **contiguous, cache-friendly layout** is why the brute-force scan is a single BLAS matrix–vector product, not a Python loop; (3) the **memory wall** — and this module adds a precise, sometimes-missed correction to it: an approximate index does **not** shrink the memory wall (it still must store every vector, plus its own index overhead) — it only bends the *time* curve. Confusing those two axes is the module's most consequential misconception.

> **Design note for the runtime.** M03 built the heap: the extreme, cheaply, exact. M05 builds the operation the heap's exactness can't scale to: similarity search over embeddings, where N is measured in millions-to-billions and the vectors themselves are 100s–1000s of dimensions. The module is organized around one arc — **exact dies, approximate survives, and survival has a price** — because that arc is not a curiosity, it is the design decision every retrieval system in production has already made, usually without the engineer who shipped it being able to say *why* it's safe, or *for whom* it might not be. Every worked number below was **measured on the authoring machine** (CPython 3.11, x86-64, 4 cores, 15 GB RAM, NumPy 2.4 with its default BLAS backend) using synthetic random unit vectors — learners **re-measure on their own hardware and, where noted, on real embeddings**; the constants move, the *shapes, crossovers, and cliffs* do not.

---

## Lessons

Four lessons. The first builds the exact baseline — a vectorized brute-force scan — and prices exactly where it dies, on two independent axes (time and memory) that die at different N. The second builds the first approximate structure, locality-sensitive hashing, and turns "approximate" from a vague word into a probability you can compute. The third builds a graph index — the intuition behind HNSW, the structure behind every serious production vector database — and shows why its search is sub-linear *in principle* but not automatically fast *in Python*. The fourth turns "recall vs. speed" from a slogan into a measured curve, and asks you to predict where it breaks — the recall cliff — before you see it break.

1. **Exact nearest neighbor, and why it dies on two different axes** — brute-force cosine similarity as one BLAS call, the curse of dimensionality (why a KD-tree doesn't save you above ~20 dimensions), and pricing the time wall and the memory wall separately because they hit at different N.
2. **Trading correctness for speed: locality-sensitive hashing** — random hyperplane hashing, why "approximate" is a computable probability, and the two knobs (hash width, table count) that trade precision against recall.
3. **Graph search: the HNSW intuition, built as an NSW-lite** — greedy best-first search over a navigable small-world graph, why its query cost is empirically sub-linear but *not* a proven O(log N) the way a balanced tree's is, and the honest reversal: a from-scratch Python graph index losing to a vectorized exact scan.
4. **Recall vs. speed: measuring the trade and predicting the cliff** — QPS and recall@k as the two axes that actually matter, why the trade-off is not smooth, and the correction every booster claim omits: approximation buys you *time*, not *memory*.

### Lesson 1 — Exact nearest neighbor, and why it dies on two different axes

#### Teaching text

Every embedding-based system reduces, eventually, to the same query: you have a query vector **q** (an embedding — a document, an image, a user) and a corpus of **N** vectors, each of dimension **d**, and you want the **k** vectors most similar to **q**. "Similar" for embeddings almost always means **cosine similarity** — the cosine of the angle between two vectors — because embedding models are trained so that *direction*, not magnitude, carries meaning. If every vector is pre-normalized to unit length, cosine similarity collapses to a plain dot product: `cos(a, b) = a·b` when `‖a‖ = ‖b‖ = 1`. That single fact is why the exact algorithm is one line of NumPy and not a loop.

**Brute-force exact search** is: compute the similarity of **q** against all N rows of the corpus matrix **X**, then take the top k. As a matrix operation that's `X @ q` — an (N, d) matrix times a (d,) vector, a single BLAS `gemv` call. This is the *same* move M03 made going from a Python loop to `sorted`/`heapq.nlargest`: push the O(N·d) work into a C-level, cache-friendly, SIMD-vectorized routine instead of an interpreted Python loop over N rows. The top-k *selection* on top of the resulting N similarities is exactly M03's problem again — `np.argpartition` here plays the role `heapq.nlargest` played there, an O(N) partial selection instead of a full O(N log N) sort.

Cost: **O(N·d)** time per query (you touch every vector, every dimension, once) and **O(N·d)** memory to hold the corpus (you cannot compare against what you haven't stored). This is *exact* — it is definitionally correct, the same guarantee `sorted(...)[:k]` gave you in M03 — and it is also the thing that dies, on **two separate axes**, at two separate values of N. Conflating those two axes is the most common error in this module, so name them separately from the start:

- **The time wall.** Query cost grows linearly with N. At small N this is imperceptible (a BLAS `gemv` over a few hundred thousand rows finishes in low milliseconds). At production N — hundreds of millions to billions of embeddings — linear-in-N, even at BLAS speed, blows past any latency budget a live service can hold. There is no clever constant-factor trick that turns O(N) into something that doesn't grow with N; you need a fundamentally different *algorithm*, not a faster implementation of this one.
- **The memory wall.** The corpus matrix costs `N × d × 4 bytes` (float32) to hold — nothing else about the query touches this; it's a static cost paid once, before you run a single query. At production `d` (768 is a common embedding width — think BERT-large or a mid-size sentence-transformer) that number gets large fast, and unlike the time wall, there is no way to "wait longer" past it — you either fit in RAM or you don't run at all, the exact M01/M03 memory-wall shape.

These two walls are hit by *different* N on *any* real machine, and Lesson 4 will show a fact many learners get backwards: the approximate structures this module builds fix the **time** wall and do essentially **nothing** for the **memory** wall, because they still have to store the same N vectors. Hold that; it's coming.

**Why not just index it — doesn't a KD-tree make this O(log N)?** A KD-tree (and its relatives) partitions space by recursively splitting on one coordinate axis at a time, the same idea a balanced BST used on 1-D keys (M03/Lesson 3), generalized to d dimensions. It works beautifully at d = 2 or d = 3. It stops working — not gracefully, but essentially completely — once d exceeds roughly 20, because of the **curse of dimensionality**: in high dimensions, the volume of a space grows so explosively with d that almost every point ends up "on the boundary," and the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbor's distance to a random query converges to 1. Concretely: in high-D space, a KD-tree's pruning rule ("this whole subtree is farther than my current best, skip it") almost never fires, because almost every subtree looks equally close. The tree degenerates to visiting nearly every leaf — you paid O(N) to build a tree that still does O(N) work per query, with extra pointer-chasing on top. Embedding dimensions (256, 768, 1536, 3072) are 10–100× past where exact spatial trees survive. This is *why* the field abandoned exact spatial indexing for embeddings and moved to approximation — not out of laziness, but because the exact structure that "should" work (a tree, O(log N), the M03 instinct) provably doesn't, at this d.

#### Worked example — pricing brute force at lab scale and at production scale

Measured on the authoring machine, `X @ q` for unit-normalized float32 vectors, single query, best-of-5:

| N | d = 128 (lab scale) | d = 768 (production embedding width) |
|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | 1.72 ms | 10.94 ms (matrix: 0.286 GB) |
| 500,000 | 14.49 ms | — |
| 1,000,000 | 23.76 ms | 79.14 ms (matrix: 2.861 GB) |
| 2,000,000 | 67.45 ms | — |

Two things to notice before extrapolating. First, the curve is **not a perfectly straight line** — 500k→1M (2×N) only cost 1.64× the time, while 1M→2M (2×N) cost 2.84× — that's the memory hierarchy (AIE-102's co-req content) leaving fingerprints: BLAS threading efficiency and cache/bandwidth effects bend the constant around the O(N) shape without changing the shape itself. Second, extrapolate the d = 768 column linearly to production N (disclosed as extrapolation, the same move M03/Lesson 4 made pricing the 10^9 top-k): at **N = 10^9**, time ≈ 79.14 ms × 1000 ≈ **79 seconds per query** — useless for anything live — and memory ≈ 2.861 GB × 1000 ≈ **2.86 TB**, past any single machine.

Now price the memory wall precisely, the M01 way — a number with a unit against a budget with the same unit. On this 15 GB machine, at d = 768: bytes/vector = 768 × 4 = 3,072 B. `N_max = 15×1024³ bytes / 3,072 bytes ≈ 5.24 million vectors.` **A production-dimension corpus stops fitting in RAM on this machine before it reaches six million vectors** — nowhere near the billion-scale corpora real systems hold. That number is the whole reason this module exists: exact search isn't merely *slow* at scale, it structurally *cannot run* long before "scale" even starts, on both axes, independently.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"A tree index (like a KD-tree) makes nearest-neighbor search O(log N), same as a balanced BST made ordered queries O(log N) in M03."** No — that generalization holds up to roughly 20 dimensions and then collapses. The curse of dimensionality makes the tree's core move (prune a subtree because it's provably farther) stop firing, because at high d nearly every point is roughly equidistant from the query. The tree still costs O(N) memory and degenerates to O(N)-ish query time, with worse constants than a flat scan. Surface it by asking: "if KD-trees gave O(log N) at any d, why would anyone bother inventing LSH or HNSW?"
- **"Vectorizing with NumPy changes the Big-O."** No — `X @ q` is still O(N·d); vectorization changes the *constant* (BLAS + SIMD vs. an interpreted loop), exactly the M01/M03 exchange-rate lesson, not the exponent. It is why brute force is *usable* at N = 10^5–10^6 and still *dead* at N = 10^9 — the constant bought you three orders of magnitude of headroom, not immunity.
- **"More RAM fixes brute force at scale."** More RAM raises `N_max` linearly; it does not change that time cost is O(N) per query. Even a machine that could somehow hold 10^9 vectors in RAM still pays ~79 s/query (extrapolated above) — useless for a live service with a millisecond-scale latency budget. Memory and time are two separate walls, and fixing one doesn't touch the other. Surface it by asking: "you just bought a machine with infinite RAM — is brute force now fast enough for a 50 ms budget at 10^9?" (No.)

### Lesson 2 — Trading correctness for speed: locality-sensitive hashing

#### Teaching text

The exact scan touches every vector because it has no way to know, without checking, whether a given vector is close to the query. **Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)** buys speed by giving up that certainty on purpose: it hashes vectors so that **similar vectors collide into the same bucket with high probability**, and dissimilar ones collide with low probability. A query then only has to compare against whatever landed in its own bucket — a tiny fraction of N — instead of all of N. The hash is not exact; two truly-nearest vectors *can* land in different buckets and simply never get compared. That is the entire trade, stated plainly: LSH is fast because it stops looking in places where the answer usually isn't — and "usually" is a probability, not a guarantee.

**Random hyperplane hashing** is the concrete construction for cosine similarity, and it's satisfyingly geometric. Draw a random hyperplane through the origin (a random unit normal vector **w**); every vector **x** falls on one side or the other, giving a single hash bit: `sign(w · x)`. Two vectors that are close in angle are very likely to fall on the same side of a random hyperplane — the probability they *disagree* is exactly `θ/π`, where θ is the angle between them (a classical fact: a random hyperplane separates two vectors of angle θ with probability proportional to θ). So `P(same bit) = 1 − θ/π`. One bit alone is a weak, noisy signal (a coin flip biased toward "same" for close vectors, but still noisy). You strengthen it two ways, and they pull in **opposite directions**:

- **Concatenate L random hyperplanes into one hash code** (an L-bit key). Two vectors must agree on *all* L bits to land in the same bucket: `P(same bucket in one table) = (1 − θ/π)^L`. This makes buckets *more selective* — each bucket holds fewer, more-similar vectors, which is good for precision — but it also makes it **exponentially less likely** that even a truly-near neighbor survives all L coin flips. More hyperplanes ⇒ smaller buckets ⇒ *lower* recall.
- **Use T independent hash tables** (T independent sets of L random hyperplanes), and take the **union** of candidates across all T tables at query time. A neighbor only needs to survive *one* of T independent rolls of the L-bit dice: `P(match in ≥1 of T tables) = 1 − (1 − (1 − θ/π)^L)^T`. More tables ⇒ higher recall, but linearly more memory (T full hash tables) and linearly more hashing work per insert and per query.

L and T are a genuine dial, not a free lunch: L trades precision for recall (bigger L = smaller, purer buckets, but real neighbors slip through the cracks more often), and T buys back recall at the cost of memory and time, T-fold. This is the LSH-specific version of a trade-off you've now seen three times in this course (heap size k in M03, hyperplane count and table count here): **the knob that makes a structure cheaper is, without exception, the same knob that makes it wrong more often.**

#### Worked example — from a formula to a measured cliff

**The formula, worked for a concrete pair.** Two vectors with cosine similarity 0.9 have angle `θ = arccos(0.9) ≈ 25.84° ≈ 0.451 rad`. `θ/π ≈ 0.1435`, so a single random hyperplane keeps them together with probability `1 − 0.1435 ≈ 0.8565`. With **L = 8** hyperplanes per table, the probability they survive one whole table is `0.8565^8 ≈ 0.289` — under 30%, for a pair that's extremely similar (cosine 0.9!). With **T = 10** independent tables, the probability at least one table catches them climbs back to `1 − (1 − 0.289)^10 ≈ 0.967`. That's the two knobs in one number: L alone would have missed this near-duplicate pair 71% of the time; T tables recover it.

**The measured cliff, and why it's steeper than the formula predicts.** The formula above is for *one pair* at a *fixed* similarity. Real recall@k needs to catch the *k* true nearest neighbors *simultaneously*, out of thousands of candidates spanning a whole range of similarities — a strictly harder bar. Measured on 20,000 random 64-dimensional unit vectors, 30 queries, `recall@10` against the exact brute-force answer:

| L (hyperplanes/table) | T (tables) | measured recall@10 |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 4 | 0.543 |
| 8 | 4 | 0.097 |
| 8 | 10 | 0.250 |
| 12 | 10 | 0.057 |
| 16 | 16 | 0.013 |

Read the shape: **recall does not degrade gently as L grows** — it collapses (0.543 → 0.097, more than 5×, from doubling L alone), and adding more tables only partially buys it back (0.097 → 0.250 by going from T=4 to T=10 at L=8, still nowhere near the L=4 number). This is why "predict the recall you expect from the formula" is a genuinely useful, genuinely wrong first guess for most learners: the formula says "0.9-similarity pairs survive 97% of the time at L=8,T=10"; the *measured*, realistic recall@10 at L=8,T=10 is 0.250. The gap between those two numbers **is** the lesson — a per-pair collision probability is not a recall number, because recall needs *every one* of k neighbors to survive, and the corpus contains many near-tied candidates competing for the same k slots.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"LSH will eventually find the true nearest neighbor if you just search long enough."** No — LSH doesn't search *more*, it searches a *fixed, pre-committed subset* (whatever landed in the query's bucket(s)). A true nearest neighbor that a random hyperplane happened to place elsewhere is gone for that query, permanently, with zero signal that it was ever missed. There is no "keep looking" lever inside a single LSH query — only "build a differently-parameterized index and re-run," which is a different index, not a longer search.
- **"More hash tables is free — just add more, recall only goes up."** Recall does monotonically increase with T (more independent rolls of the dice), but T tables cost T× the memory (T full copies of the hash structure) and T× the hashing work per insert/query. It's the M03 shape again: a knob that buys correctness back always spends a resource to do it.
- **"The theoretical collision probability tells you the recall you'll get."** It bounds the probability *one specific pair* collides at *one specific similarity*; measured recall@k is lower, sometimes far lower, because k neighbors must all survive simultaneously against a whole distribution of near-ties. Predicting recall from the formula alone, without measuring, is the trap Lesson 4's lab is built to catch.

### Lesson 3 — Graph search: the HNSW intuition, built as an NSW-lite

#### Teaching text

LSH's weakness is that its bucket structure is *fixed at hash time* — it has no way to "look a little further" when the bucket comes up empty or thin. The structure that dominates production vector search today, **HNSW** (Hierarchical Navigable Small World), replaces buckets with a **graph**: every vector is a node, connected to a handful of its approximate near neighbors, and a query is answered by **greedily walking the graph toward the query** — "am I closer to the query than my neighbors? follow whichever neighbor gets me closer; repeat." This module builds the load-bearing half of that idea by hand — a single-layer graph, an **NSW** (Navigable Small World), which is exactly what sits at the *bottom* layer of a full HNSW. The "H" — a hierarchy of sparser and sparser layers stacked on top, giving fast long-range jumps before the fine-grained walk — is described here in text, not built; the payoff of building the base layer alone is real, and the multi-layer version is a direct, describable extension once you understand this one.

**Construction.** Insert vectors one at a time. For the very first vector, there's nothing to connect to — it becomes the graph's entry point. For every vector after that: run a graph search (described next) from the current entry point to find the **M** existing vectors most similar to the new one, then add a bidirectional edge to each. Crucially, **cap every node's degree at M** — after adding an edge to some existing node `nb`, if `nb` now has more than M neighbors, prune `nb`'s neighbor list back down to its M closest, dropping the weakest edge. Without this cap, a densely-clustered region of the graph would grow unboundedly high-degree nodes, ballooning both memory (O(N·M) is the whole point) and search cost (each hop visits every neighbor of the current node).

**Search — greedy best-first with a beam.** Start at the entry point. Maintain two collections: a min-heap of **candidates to explore** (ordered so the *most promising* unexplored node comes out first) and a bounded set of the **best `ef` nodes found so far**. Repeatedly: pop the most promising candidate; if it's already worse than the *worst* node currently in your best-`ef` set, and you already have `ef` results, **stop** — nothing left in the candidate queue can possibly improve your answer. Otherwise, look at all of that node's graph-neighbors; for each unvisited neighbor, if it's good enough to enter the best-`ef` set (or the set isn't full yet), add it to both the candidate queue and the best-set, evicting the current worst member if the set overflowed. This is literally beam search over a graph, and `ef` is the beam width — the *query-time* recall/speed knob, distinct from `M`, the *graph-density* recall/speed knob fixed at build time.

**Why this is sub-linear in principle but not automatically fast.** A well-connected navigable small-world graph has the property that greedy hops shrink the distance to the target roughly geometrically — empirically, the number of hops to converge grows something like O(log N), the same shape as a balanced tree's height. But — and this is the honest caveat M03's balanced-tree lesson didn't need — **this is an empirical, typical-case property, not a proven worst-case bound.** A balanced BST's O(log N) height is a mathematical guarantee enforced by an explicit invariant (AIE-110/M03/Lesson 3). An NSW/HNSW graph's search cost is a *measured regularity* of how these graphs tend to behave on real data; there is no theorem that forbids a pathological graph or query from taking O(N) hops. You ship it because it *measures* well, repeatedly, on real embedding distributions — not because a proof forbids the bad case. Treat that distinction as load-bearing, not pedantic: it is *why* this lesson's lab measures instead of trusting the shape.

**And it costs something real per hop.** Each hop touches a Python `set` of neighbor ids, computes a similarity (a dot product), and does heap bookkeeping — all in the interpreted layer, unless you've compiled it away. That per-hop constant is where Lesson 4's reversal lives.

#### Worked example — one greedy walk by hand

A tiny 6-node graph (M = 2), similarities to query **q** shown at each node, entry point is node A:

```
A(0.31) — B(0.55) — D(0.71)
  |          |
  C(0.42)   E(0.88)
             |
            F(0.63)
```
(edges: A–B, A–C, B–D, B–E, E–F)

Greedy walk, `ef = 1` (track only the single best node found, the simplest case): start at **A** (sim 0.31). Look at A's neighbors: B (0.55) and C (0.42) — both beat A, best neighbor is B, move there. At **B** (0.55), look at B's neighbors: D (0.71) and E (0.88) — both beat B, best is E, move there. At **E** (0.88), look at E's neighbor: F (0.63) — worse than E. No neighbor improves on the current best, so **stop**. Result: **E, similarity 0.88**, found in **3 hops** without ever visiting D (0.71) or C (0.42) — the walk never needed them, because at every step it followed the single most-promising direction. Compare to brute force: 6 comparisons, one per node. The graph did it in 3 similarity checks (A, B, E) plus a look at 2 dead-end neighbors (C, D via degree)) — genuinely fewer full evaluations, and the saving compounds as N grows, *provided* the graph stays well-connected and `ef` is wide enough not to get trapped in a local pocket that looks good but isn't the true global best.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"It's a graph, so it must find the true nearest neighbor, unlike random hashing."** No — greedy best-first search can converge to a **local optimum**: a node that beats all its immediate neighbors but isn't the global best, especially with a narrow beam (`ef` too small). This is exactly the failure LSH has, arrived at by a different mechanism — a graph index is still approximate, and the beam width `ef` is the knob that trades how often that happens against how much work you do.
- **"HNSW's search is O(log N), a guarantee, the same as a balanced BST."** No — a balanced BST's O(log N) height is enforced by a provable invariant; an NSW/HNSW graph's roughly-logarithmic hop count is an empirically observed regularity on typical embedding distributions, with no worst-case proof forbidding a bad query or a poorly-connected region from costing far more. Ship it on measurement, not on a theorem — and re-measure when the data distribution changes.
- **"A bigger M (more neighbors per node) is free — it can only help recall."** M controls graph density directly: memory is O(N·M), and construction cost grows with M too (every insert searches a denser graph and prunes a longer neighbor list). Bigger M buys recall at a real, linear cost in both memory and build time — not a free upgrade.

### Lesson 4 — Recall vs. speed: measuring the trade, and the memory correction

#### Teaching text

Two axes actually matter in production similarity search: **recall@k** (what fraction of the true top-k did the index actually return?) and **speed** — usually reported as query latency or, inverted, queries-per-second (QPS). Every knob this module has introduced — L and T for LSH, M and `ef` for the graph index — moves you along a **recall/speed frontier**: spend more time per query, get more recall; spend less, get less. The mistake this lesson exists to correct is treating that frontier as smooth. It usually isn't.

**The recall cliff.** Measured on a from-scratch NSW-lite graph (N = 5,000, d = 64, M = 16, `ef_construction` = 100), recall@10 against the exact brute-force answer, sweeping the query-time beam width `ef_search`:

| ef_search | recall@10 | time/query |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.147 | 0.14 ms |
| 10 | 0.267 | 0.26 ms |
| 20 | 0.420 | 0.51 ms |
| 40 | 0.660 | 0.89 ms |
| 80 | 0.797 | 1.33 ms |
| 160 | 0.933 | 2.31 ms |
| 320 | 0.980 | 3.84 ms |

This particular curve is closer to a *smooth diminishing-returns curve* than a sheer cliff — but look at the **steep region below ef≈40**: recall more than quadruples (0.147 → 0.660) over that stretch, while the "safe-feeling" region above ef≈160 buys only a few more points of recall for a doubling of latency. **The cliff, when one exists, sits on the low-`ef`/low-latency end** — exactly the end a latency-constrained production system is pressured toward. A team that tunes purely for p99 latency, watching only the average recall number, can walk itself down this curve without ever seeing a dashboard alarm — average recall degrades *gradually* even where the *worst-served queries* degrade sharply, a distinction Tier 3 returns to.

**The correction that reverses a common assumption: approximation does not shrink the memory wall.** Both the LSH index and the NSW graph index built in this module still hold a full copy of every vector — `self._vectors` — because a candidate, once found by hashing or graph-walking, still needs a real similarity computed against the real vector. LSH adds `T` hash tables of integer bucket keys (small, O(N) each) on top of that; NSW adds an adjacency structure of O(N·M) edges on top of that. **Both are O(N·d) at the base, plus a smaller O(N) or O(N·M) index overhead** — meaning the corpus-storage memory wall from Lesson 1 (≈5.24 million vectors at d=768 on this 15 GB machine) applies to the exact scan **and** to both approximate structures, essentially unchanged. What LSH and the NSW graph buy you is **query time** — turning an O(N) scan into something that touches a small fraction of N — not a smaller footprint for the corpus itself. Production systems that genuinely need to hold billions of vectors combine an ANN index (for time) with **vector quantization** — compressing each vector to a few bytes instead of `d × 4` — which is a *separate* technique this module does not build (AIE-302 picks it up as a RAG-specific concern). Naming this correction explicitly matters because the marketing shorthand — "vector databases scale to billions" — quietly bundles a time fix and a memory fix together, and only one of them is what HNSW actually is.

**QPS, and why the interpreter tax reappears here at full strength.** A from-scratch NSW-lite graph, in pure Python, pays a real per-hop cost: a `set` lookup, a dot product, heap pushes and pops — all through the interpreter. Compare, at N = 5,000, d = 64: brute-force `X @ q` (BLAS, C) measured **≈0.05 ms/query**; the pure-Python NSW-lite graph at `ef_search = 5` (its *cheapest, least-accurate* setting) measured **0.14 ms/query — nearly 3× slower**, and its most accurate setting (`ef_search = 320`, recall 0.980) measured **3.84 ms — nearly 80× slower**. At this N, in this language, the theoretically sub-linear structure loses to the theoretically linear one on *every single point on its own recall/speed curve* — a sharper version of M03's reversal, because here the "winner" doesn't even have the excuse of being exact. The lever that unreverses it is the same lever M03 found: a C-compiled graph-search implementation (production libraries — FAISS, `hnswlib` — compile exactly this algorithm), which is why every real vector database ships this structure in C or C++/Rust, never as a Python loop over sets and dicts. **The algorithm being asymptotically better and the implementation being fast are still two separate decisions** — the deepest repeat in this course, now landing on the exact component the rest of your AI-engineering career will import instead of hand-roll.

#### Worked example — predicting the crossover N

The measured brute-force curve at d = 64: N=20,000 → 0.09 ms/query; N=200,000 → 1.60 ms/query; N=1,000,000 → 16.14 ms/query — growing with N, though not on a perfectly clean line (the same memory-hierarchy jitter Lesson 1 flagged). The NSW-lite graph's query time, measured directly at N = 20,000 with `ef_search = 80`, was **1.87 ms** — and a graph's query cost grows only slowly with N (more hops, not more per-hop work, so roughly logarithmic rather than linear). Setting the two curves equal: brute force reaches ≈1.9 ms/query somewhere around **N ≈ 2×10^5** at this d and `ef_search`, interpolating between the 200,000-row and 1,000,000-row measurements above. Below that N, brute force wins on wall-clock, full stop, at *every* `ef_search` setting tested above — the approximate structure is strictly dominated, buying neither speed nor certainty. Above it, the linear scan's cost keeps growing while the graph's stays roughly flat, and the crossover — the N past which "approximate but sub-linear" finally starts paying for its own inexactness — arrives. **The lesson is not "graphs are better" or "graphs are worse" — it's that the crossover exists at a specific, measurable N, and shipping an ANN index below that N is pure cost with no benefit.** Lab M05-B asks you to predict this crossover before you have this paragraph in front of you.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"An approximate index (LSH or HNSW) uses less memory than exact search, because it doesn't store everything."** No — both still store every vector (a candidate must be compared for real once found), plus their own index overhead on top. Approximation buys **query time**, not corpus memory; the memory wall from Lesson 1 applies to all three structures at essentially the same N. Compression that shrinks the *corpus* itself is a different technique (quantization), not what LSH or HNSW do.
- **"Recall vs. speed is a smooth, linear trade — pick any point you like."** The measured curve above is closer to smooth than a sheer wall in this configuration, but the steepest degradation clusters at the low-latency end — exactly where a latency-pressured team is pulled toward — and other parameter regimes (small `ef_construction`, too-small `M`, adversarial data distributions) produce genuinely sharp cliffs, not gradual slopes. Never ship a recall/speed setting without measuring recall at that exact setting; extrapolating from a nearby point is how a team discovers the cliff in production instead of in a lab.
- **"An approximate structure being sub-linear means it's automatically faster than brute force."** Only past its crossover N, and only if its constant (compiled vs. interpreted) doesn't swamp the operation-count savings — exactly the interpreter-tax reversal, now with the added twist that the "winning" structure isn't even exact. Predicting the crossover, not assuming it's at N=0, is the graded skill.

```python
def brute_force_knn(X, q, k):
    sims = X @ q
    n = len(sims)
    if k >= n:
        idx = np.argsort(-sims)
        return idx, sims[idx]
    part = np.argpartition(-sims, k)[:k]     # O(N): the k largest, unordered
    order = np.argsort(-sims[part])          # O(k log k): order just the k
    idx = part[order]
    return idx, sims[idx]
```

```python
import numpy as np

def make_embeddings(n, d, seed=0):
    """n unit-normalized random float32 vectors of dimension d.
    A synthetic, reproducible stand-in for real embeddings."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = rng.standard_normal((n, d)).astype(np.float32)
    norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    norms[norms == 0] = 1.0
    return X / norms

def recall_at_k(approx_idx, exact_idx):
    """Fraction of the true top-k (exact_idx) also present in approx_idx.
    Order-independent -- recall asks WHO was found, not in what order."""
    if len(exact_idx) == 0:
        return 1.0
    return len(set(approx_idx) & set(exact_idx)) / len(exact_idx)
```

```python
import heapq
import numpy as np

class NSWIndex:
    """A single-layer Navigable Small World graph -- the base layer HNSW
    stacks additional sparse layers on top of. Approximate cosine-similarity
    nearest-neighbor search via greedy best-first graph walk.

    M               = max neighbors kept per node (graph density / memory)
    ef_construction = beam width used while INSERTING (a build-time recall knob)
    """
    def __init__(self, dim, M=16, ef_construction=100):
        self.dim = dim
        self.M = M
        self.ef_construction = ef_construction
        self.vectors = []      # list of (d,) unit vectors; index = node id
        self.neighbors = []    # list of sets; adjacency list
        self.entry_point = None

    def _sim(self, node_id, x):
        return float(self.vectors[node_id] @ x)

    def _search_layer(self, q, entry, ef):
        """Greedy best-first search from `entry`, keeping a beam of size ef.
        Returns up to ef (similarity, node_id) pairs, most-similar first.

        TODO -- implement the standard navigable-small-world search:
          - `candidates`: a min-heap on (-sim, node) so the CLOSEST unexplored
            node pops first (negate similarity to turn a max-pop into heapq's
            min-pop).
          - `found`: a min-heap on (sim, node) of the best `ef` results seen;
            its root (found[0]) is the WORST of the current best-ef -- the
            eviction threshold, same role heap[0] played in M03's bounded
            top-k heap.
          - loop: pop the most promising candidate `c`. If c is already worse
            than found's worst AND found already has ef members, stop -- no
            remaining candidate can improve on what you have.
          - otherwise, visit c's unvisited neighbors: for each, if `found`
            has room OR the neighbor beats found's current worst, push it
            onto both `candidates` and `found` (evicting found's worst if
            `found` overflowed past ef).
          - return `found`, sorted best-first.
        """
        raise NotImplementedError

    def add(self, x):
        """Insert unit vector x. Returns its new node id."""
        i = len(self.vectors)
        self.vectors.append(x)
        self.neighbors.append(set())
        if i == 0:
            self.entry_point = i
            return i
        candidates = self._search_layer(x, self.entry_point, self.ef_construction)
        neighbor_ids = [node for _, node in candidates[:self.M]]
        for nb in neighbor_ids:
            self.neighbors[i].add(nb)
            self.neighbors[nb].add(i)
            if len(self.neighbors[nb]) > self.M:      # keep degree bounded
                nb_vec = self.vectors[nb]
                sims = [(float(self.vectors[j] @ nb_vec), j) for j in self.neighbors[nb]]
                sims.sort(key=lambda p: -p[0])
                self.neighbors[nb] = {j for _, j in sims[:self.M]}
        return i

    def search(self, q, k, ef_search=None):
        """Approximate top-k by cosine similarity. Returns (indices, sims)."""
        if not self.vectors:
            return np.array([], dtype=int), np.array([], dtype=np.float32)
        ef = ef_search if ef_search is not None else max(k, self.ef_construction)
        found = self._search_layer(q, self.entry_point, ef)[:k]
        idx = np.array([node for _, node in found], dtype=int)
        sims = np.array([sim for sim, node in found], dtype=np.float32)
        return idx, sims
```

Reference solution (`_search_layer`):

```python
    def _search_layer(self, q, entry, ef):
        visited = {entry}
        entry_sim = self._sim(entry, q)
        candidates = [(-entry_sim, entry)]     # min-heap on -sim -> closest pops first
        heapq.heapify(candidates)
        found = [(entry_sim, entry)]           # min-heap on sim -> found[0] is the worst kept
        heapq.heapify(found)

        while candidates:
            neg_c_sim, c = heapq.heappop(candidates)
            c_sim = -neg_c_sim
            worst_found = found[0][0]
            if c_sim < worst_found and len(found) >= ef:
                break   # nothing left in the queue can beat our current worst kept result
            for e in self.neighbors[c]:
                if e in visited:
                    continue
                visited.add(e)
                e_sim = self._sim(e, q)
                worst_found = found[0][0]
                if len(found) < ef or e_sim > worst_found:
                    heapq.heappush(candidates, (-e_sim, e))
                    heapq.heappush(found, (e_sim, e))
                    if len(found) > ef:
                        heapq.heappop(found)     # evict the current worst
        return sorted(found, key=lambda p: -p[0])
```

Reference solution:

```python
    def _hash(self, x, t):
        signs = self.planes[t] @ x >= 0
        code = 0
        for b in signs:
            code = (code << 1) | int(b)
        return code

    def query(self, q, k):
        candidates = set()
        for t in range(self.T):
            candidates.update(self.tables[t].get(self._hash(q, t), []))
        if not candidates:
            return np.array([], dtype=int), np.array([], dtype=np.float32)
        cand_idx = np.fromiter(candidates, dtype=int)
        sims = self._vectors[cand_idx] @ q
        kk = min(k, len(sims))
        if kk == len(sims):
            order = np.argsort(-sims)
        else:
            part = np.argpartition(-sims, kk)[:kk]
            order = part[np.argsort(-sims[part])]
        return cand_idx[order][:kk], sims[order][:kk]
```

import numpy as np
from annlib import make_embeddings, recall_at_k
from bruteforce import brute_force_knn
from lsh import LSHIndex
from nsw import NSWIndex
from m05harness import bench

if __name__ == "__main__":
    N, d, k = 20_000, 64, 10
    X = make_embeddings(N, d, seed=1)
    queries = make_embeddings(30, d, seed=42)

    print("--- LSH ---")
    for L, T in [(4, 4), (8, 4), (8, 10), (12, 10), (16, 16)]:
        idx = LSHIndex(dim=d, L=L, T=T, seed=0)
        idx.build(X)
        recalls = []
        for q in queries:
            e, _ = brute_force_knn(X, q, k)
            a, _ = idx.query(q, k)
            recalls.append(recall_at_k(a, e))
        t = bench(lambda idx=idx: [idx.query(q, k) for q in queries[:5]], repeats=3, number=1)
        print(f"L={L:>2} T={T:>2}  recall@{k}={np.mean(recalls):.3f}  {t/5*1000:.4f} ms/query")

    print("--- NSW ---")
    nsw = NSWIndex(dim=d, M=16, ef_construction=100)
    for i in range(N):
        nsw.add(X[i])
    for ef in [20, 40, 80, 160]:
        recalls = []
        for q in queries:
            e, _ = brute_force_knn(X, q, k)
            a, _ = nsw.search(q, k, ef_search=ef)
            recalls.append(recall_at_k(a, e))
        t = bench(lambda ef=ef: [nsw.search(q, k, ef_search=ef) for q in queries[:5]], repeats=3, number=1)
        print(f"ef_search={ef:>4}  recall@{k}={np.mean(recalls):.3f}  {t/5*1000:.4f} ms/query")

```python
import numpy as np
from lsh import LSHIndex
from bruteforce import brute_force_knn
from annlib import make_embeddings, recall_at_k

def test_hash_is_deterministic_for_same_table():
    idx = LSHIndex(dim=16, L=6, T=1, seed=0)
    x = make_embeddings(1, 16, seed=1)[0]
    assert idx._hash(x, 0) == idx._hash(x, 0)

def test_hash_code_in_valid_range():
    idx = LSHIndex(dim=16, L=6, T=1, seed=0)
    x = make_embeddings(1, 16, seed=1)[0]
    code = idx._hash(x, 0)
    assert 0 <= code < 2**6

def test_identical_vector_is_own_top_result():
    X = make_embeddings(500, 32, seed=2)
    idx = LSHIndex(dim=32, L=6, T=8, seed=0)
    idx.build(X)
    approx_idx, approx_sims = idx.query(X[10], 1)
    assert len(approx_idx) >= 1
    assert approx_idx[0] == 10          # a vector always hashes with itself

def test_empty_bucket_returns_empty():
    idx = LSHIndex(dim=8, L=20, T=1, seed=0)   # huge L on tiny T: buckets nearly empty
    X = make_embeddings(20, 8, seed=3)
    idx.build(X)
    far_q = -X[0]                               # near-opposite of everything indexed
    approx_idx, approx_sims = idx.query(far_q, 5)
    assert len(approx_idx) == len(approx_sims)   # shapes agree even if empty

def test_more_hyperplanes_reduces_or_holds_recall():
    X = make_embeddings(3000, 32, seed=4)
    queries = make_embeddings(15, 32, seed=5)
    def mean_recall(L, T):
        idx = LSHIndex(dim=32, L=L, T=T, seed=0)
        idx.build(X)
        rs = []
        for q in queries:
            e, _ = brute_force_knn(X, q, 10)
            a, _ = idx.query(q, 10)
            rs.append(recall_at_k(a, e))
        return np.mean(rs)
    assert mean_recall(4, 4) >= mean_recall(16, 4) - 1e-9   # more L, same T -> recall doesn't improve
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — approximate similarity search, and who a fast index quietly stops looking for — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).** Exact cosine-similarity search is one BLAS call, `X @ q`, O(N·d) time and O(N·d) memory — measured at 79 ms/query and 2.86 GB at N=1,000,000, d=768 on the authoring machine, and extrapolable (disclosed as extrapolation) to ~79 s/query and ~2.86 TB at N=10^9, past what a single machine can serve or hold. A 15–16 GB machine stops fitting a production-dimension (d=768) corpus around 5–6 million vectors — arithmetic reproducible with `bytes/vector = d × 4` against any machine's real RAM. Locality-sensitive hashing turns "approximate" into a computable probability — `P(collision) = (1 − θ/π)^L` per table, `1 − (1 − P)^T` across T tables — and Lab M05-C measures that the naive formula (0.967 at a realistic L,T for a 0.9-similarity pair) overstates real recall@10 (0.250, measured) because k neighbors must survive simultaneously, not just one pair. A from-scratch navigable-small-world graph (`NSWIndex`, Lab M05-B) achieves recall@10 from 0.147 to 0.980 by sweeping its beam width `ef_search`, and — measured, not asserted — loses to exact brute-force search on wall-clock at every tested setting at N=5,000, by roughly 3× to nearly 80×, because the interpreter tax on each Python-level graph hop dwarfs the operation-count savings at this N; the crossover is estimated (extrapolated from measured curves) around N≈2×10^5 at d=64. Both LSH and NSW store the *entire* corpus (`self._vectors`/`self.vectors`) plus a smaller index overhead — verified directly with `tracemalloc` — so neither reduces the memory wall the exact scan hits; they reduce query time only.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Vector databases scale to billions" is the field's dominant claim, and it is technically achieved — but by *bundling two separate fixes* (a sub-linear index for time, plus quantization/compression for memory) and marketing them as one, so that an engineer who reaches for "just add an ANN index" to a memory-constrained system gets a working demo at 10^5 rows and a production incident at 10^8, because the index they added never touched the axis that was actually going to break. This module's Tier 1 gives you the receipt: `NSWIndex` at N and d costs essentially the same corpus memory as brute force, full stop, a fact any vector-database vendor's marketing page omits by simply never stating what "scale to billions" is scaling *along*. Second, and this one implicates the from-scratch builder as much as the vendor: **recall@k, reported as a single averaged number, is precisely the statistic that hides who the index fails.** A system that reports "97% recall" sounds nearly exact. But recall@k averaged across a benchmark query set says nothing about the *distribution* of that failure — whether the missing 3% is spread thinly and randomly across every query, or concentrated entirely on a specific subset of queries that score near-zero every time. Both produce the identical headline number. The field ships the headline number because it's the one number that fits in a comparison table; it does not, by construction, ship the distribution, and a distribution is exactly what you'd need to know whether "97% recall" means "everyone is a little bit worse off" or "everyone is fine except this group, always."

- **Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** You built `NSWIndex` by hand. You know, because you measured it, that it is not exact — that its beam search can converge on a local optimum and never reach the true nearest neighbor, and that this happens more, not less, for query vectors sitting in a sparse or unusual region of the embedding space, where fewer nearby graph edges exist to route a greedy walk toward the right answer. LSH has the same shape from a different mechanism: a vector unlucky enough to land, by the accident of a few random hyperplanes, in a bucket with nothing similar to it, is simply never compared to its true neighbors — no error, no signal, just a query that returns confidently and is quietly wrong. Both failure modes concentrate on the same kind of point: the outlier, the vector unlike its neighbors, the query that sits far from every cluster your index's structure was built around — which, translated out of the geometry and into what these vectors usually *represent* in a real system, is the document unlike the corpus, the phrasing unlike the common phrasings, the name or the context that embeds far from where most embeddings cluster because it belongs to whoever is least like the majority the model was trained on. Recall@k, averaged across a benchmark, will never single this out — it reports one number, and one number cannot distinguish "everyone loses 3%" from "this person loses 100% of the time, every time, and nobody else loses at all." So: you now know how to measure recall. You do not, from that measurement alone, know *whose* queries live inside the 3% you didn't catch — and the entire economic case for building this index instead of the exact one was that you would stop checking every candidate. What would it take to find out who's inside that 3% — and if finding out requires doing the exact, expensive, exhaustive check you built this index specifically to avoid doing, was the index ever really answering the question "who is nearest," or only the cheaper question "who is nearest, among those I decided in advance to look at"?

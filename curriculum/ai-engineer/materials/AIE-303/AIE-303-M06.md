## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — The incident lifecycle: detect, triage, mitigate, assess impact, resolve, document

**Teaching text.** An incident has a shape, and the shape has an order that is easy to violate under pressure and expensive to violate anyway. **Detect** — a threshold-crossing signal fires (an SLO alert, a user report, a canary rollback per M04). **Triage** — scope and severity: how much of the traffic, how bad, is it getting worse. **Mitigate** — restore the user-facing SLO by whatever safe, fast means available, *without* requiring you to already understand why it broke. **Assess impact** — now that new requests are no longer affected, account for the ones that already were: how many, over what window, and did any of them get a *wrong* answer or just a *slow* one — the step M04's Lesson 3 named and explicitly left for this module ("identifying affected users, re-processing, notifying someone"). **Resolve** — the durable, root-cause fix ships, off the clock, without the SLO breach pressuring the engineering. **Document** — the postmortem.

The load-bearing claim of this lesson is that **mitigate and resolve are different jobs with different clocks**, and collapsing them is the single most common failure mode this module sees. Mitigation's clock is "every minute this stays broken costs users and money" — it rewards the fastest safe lever, even an admittedly non-durable one (a restart, a feature flag flip), because the goal is stopping active harm, not understanding it. Resolution's clock is "does this fix survive review, testing, and a calm re-read tomorrow" — it rewards correctness and completeness, and it should almost never be rushed by the same pressure that made mitigation urgent, because that pressure is gone the moment mitigation lands. A postmortem that shows the root-cause fix landing in production nine minutes after the alert fired is not usually evidence of a great engineer — mitigation doesn't require a root-cause fix, and shipping one that fast under live pressure is exactly how a rushed second bug gets introduced while fixing the first one.

**Worked example.** This module's own scenario, as a real timeline (every timestamp below is a real timestamp `run_drill` in Lab M06-B will produce for you, not narrated):

```
09:00:00  DETECT     PagerAlert fires: p95=181.5ms vs slo=120.0ms, breached=True, all traffic
09:00:45  TRIAGE     scope = 100% of /chat traffic, no error responses, latency only,
                      getting worse (cache still growing every request)
09:02:10  MITIGATE   diagnose.py run -> cache_lookup dominant (70.9% of request time)
                      disable_cache applied (feature flag, instant)
09:02:15  VERIFY     check_slo re-run -> p95=50.3ms, SLO restored
09:02:15  ASSESS     impact window = 09:00:00-09:02:15 (2m 15s), ~100% of traffic slow,
                      0% incorrect (a slow answer, never a wrong one -- confirmed by
                      checking the cache's role: it only ever SKIPPED regeneration,
                      never altered an answer's content)
09:04:00+ RESOLVE     FastCacheStore written, tested, reviewed -- off the clock,
                      no user impact, no time pressure
09:20:00  DOCUMENT   postmortem.md written, linted, filed
```

Mitigation took **2 minutes 15 seconds**. Resolution took longer and happened entirely *after* users stopped being affected — which is the point: the fix is better for having been written calmly. Notice also the Assess step's specific finding: because the defect here degrades a *stage's latency* and never touches an answer's *content*, impact assessment concludes no re-processing is needed. That's a real finding, not a formality — a defect in a different stage (say, one that occasionally cached the *wrong* answer against a *similar* query) would make Assess Impact the step that decides whether anyone needs a corrected answer resent, and skipping it would be the mistake, not writing it up.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The on-call engineer must find and fix the root cause during the incident."* This conflates mitigate with resolve. The incident is over, from a user's perspective, the moment the SLO is restored — even though the buggy line of code is still sitting in the repository. Lab M06-B is timed on mitigation, not on shipping `FastCacheStore`.
- *"Restarting the process isn't a real fix, so it's not a legitimate move during an incident."* Backwards for the *mitigate* phase specifically: `restart_process` in this module's mitigation menu restores the SLO (it empties the unbounded cache) even though it is explicitly not durable — the mistake is treating a restart as *resolution*, reporting the incident closed, and not noticing the cache will regrow toward the same breach point at the same rate. A mitigation that isn't durable is still a legitimate mitigation.
- *"Triage is bureaucratic overhead when you can already tell it's bad."* Triage is what the Assess Impact step depends on later — "100% of traffic, latency only, no wrong answers" versus "12% of traffic, and some of those requests may have gotten stale account data" are different incidents requiring different follow-through, and you cannot reconstruct that distinction after the fact if no one wrote down the scope while it was live.

### Lesson 2 — Diagnosis under a clock: cheap signals before expensive ones

**Teaching text.** M05 built the instinct: read a stage breakdown before you read source code. This lesson is about what changes when the clock from Lesson 1 is running. The temptation under time pressure is to skip straight to the most familiar move — usually "open the file and start reading" — because it *feels* like progress. It is almost always slower than following a cheap-to-expensive ladder in order: **(1) what does the alert itself already tell you** — which metric, how far over threshold, what scope (M05's subgroup-dilution lesson is exactly relevant here: "all traffic" and "one slice" are different diagnoses before you've run anything); **(2) the stage/span breakdown** — which single stage of the request pipeline is spending the time, a tool that already exists and costs one function call to run; **(3) only then, source code** — and now you're reading one function instead of guessing which of several files might be the culprit.

Skipping step 2 doesn't just risk being slower — it risks being *wrong under pressure in a way that looks like progress*. An engineer who opens `oncall_service.py` cold, under an SLO-breach clock, and reads top to bottom will eventually find the linear scan — the file is short enough — but "eventually" is exactly the word Lesson 1 is measuring, and a wrong guess read as far as line 40 before finding nothing is a wrong guess that cost real minutes an already-built tool would have saved.

**Worked example.** The actual, measured stage breakdown from this module's incident cache (200,000 accumulated entries, ten sample incident-window requests, real `diagnose.py` output):

```
cache_lookup   mean=122.69ms  share=70.9%
generation     mean= 50.22ms  share=29.0%
cache_store    mean=  0.02ms  share= 0.0%
```

One function call — `stage_breakdown(cache, generate_fn, queries)` — turns "the request was slow" into "70.9% of every request's time is in `cache_lookup`, specifically," before a single line of `oncall_service.py` has been opened. That's the entire value of building the instrument *before* the incident, the way M05 did: an on-call engineer under a real clock is not the moment to be writing a profiler from scratch.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"The alert's metric name tells you the cause."* An SLO alert on `p95_ms` tells you *what* broke (latency) — it says nothing about *why*, exactly as M05 warned that a drift alert tells you *that* a distribution shifted, never *why*. `cache_lookup` being the dominant stage is new information the alert never contained.
- *"Under time pressure, the Predict step is a nice-to-have you can skip."* It's the opposite: predicting which stage will dominate *before* running the diagnostic, and being wrong, is cheap and fast to notice — reading source with no prediction to check yourself against means you can convince yourself a plausible-looking line is the bug without ever confirming it against a real number. The moment it's most tempting to skip Predict is the moment skipping it costs the most.
- *"More logging during a live incident helps find it faster."* Improvised logging added mid-incident competes for the same limited attention the incident already needs, and it wasn't there when the alert fired, so it captures nothing about the moments before you added it. The already-built stage breakdown tool captures the *next* request completely, immediately, with zero code changes to a live system under load.

### Lesson 3 — The mitigation menu: every lever has a time cost and a correctness cost

**Teaching text.** A **mitigation menu** is a short, pre-agreed table of viable responses to a class of incident, reasoned through *calmly, before* any specific incident, each with a stated time-to-apply and a stated correctness cost. The entire value of a menu is that the tradeoffs were already worked out when nobody was under pressure — inventing a mitigation for the first time mid-incident is slower to reason about and more likely to be wrong than picking the right pre-vetted row from a table, which is exactly M04 Lesson 4's rehearsal claim applied one level up: a lever you haven't rehearsed and reasoned through in advance is a lever you don't actually know works, whether that lever is a version-pointer flip or a feature flag.

This module's menu, for the cache-latency incident shape, has four rows, each measured in `mitigate.py`:

| Lever | Time to apply | Restores SLO? | Correctness cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| `disable_cache` | ~0 ms (flag flip) | Yes | None — answers are freshly generated, identical in kind to what the cache would have served |
| `restart_process` | ~50 ms (simulated reload) | Yes, temporarily | None immediately, but not durable — cache regrows toward the same breach point at the same rate |
| `rollback_to_last_known_good` | ~200 ms (simulated pipeline run) | Yes | Reverts *every* change in the rolled-back release, not just this defect — M04 Lesson 3's exact warning, that "rollback" is shorthand for a pointer flip that should touch only the state that's actually broken |
| `rate_limit` | ~20 ms | **No** | Admitted requests still pay the full scan; shed users get no answer at all |

`disable_cache` is the fastest AND the cheapest lever here — not a coincidence this module planted, but a real property worth noticing: it is scoped to *exactly* the feature that's broken, the same discipline M04 Lesson 3 taught about a version-pointer flip touching only the state it needs to. `rate_limit` is deliberately the wrong answer for this specific failure shape: the cost here is **per admitted request**, not a function of total concurrent load, so shedding traffic does nothing for the users still being served — and neither would adding more server replicas behind a load balancer, for the identical reason. A second machine running the same `O(n)` scan against the same accumulated cache still scans the same list. You cannot buy your way out of an algorithmic cost with hardware, a fact AIE-102 and AIE-301 already put in your hands; this lesson is where it costs you a wrong 3am decision if you forget it.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"Rollback is always the safest choice."* M04 Lesson 3, again: rollback here is slower than a flag flip and it reverts unrelated changes shipped in the same release. "Safest" is not the same property as "cheapest to reason about" — a flag scoped to exactly the broken feature is frequently both safer and faster than reverting an entire release to get at one bad line inside it.
- *"Rate limiting is a reasonable first response to any latency incident."* False for this failure shape specifically, and the mechanism generalizes: rate limiting helps when the cost scales with *concurrent load* (too many requests competing for a fixed resource); it does nothing when the cost scales with *per-request work* that doesn't shrink no matter how few requests get through.
- *"A mitigation menu should be built during the incident, tailored to what's actually wrong."* The entire value of a menu is that it was reasoned through without a clock running. `mitigate.py`'s four levers, their timings, and their correctness costs were all decided and tested *before* this module's Lab B ever starts the drill — that's what makes picking one during the drill fast instead of another improvised guess.

### Lesson 4 — The blameless postmortem: root cause as a system fact, never a person-shaped one

**Teaching text.** A postmortem has a fixed shape — Timeline, Impact, Root Cause, Contributing Factors, Mitigation, Action Items — and one binding rule: **the Root Cause section must terminate at something a system or process could have caught, never at "a person made an error."** This is not about being polite. A person-shaped root cause is a dead end for prevention: you cannot patch a future person's judgment, and every person, including the best engineer on the team, will eventually write a bug. What you *can* patch is a missing test, a missing alert, a review checklist that didn't ask the right question, a CI gate that didn't check the right property. "Blameless" means the causal chain is required to keep going until it reaches one of those.

The discipline for getting there is **five whys**, with one added rule: if a "why" bottoms out at something a person did or didn't think of, that's a sign you're not done — ask one more why: *why did the system let that happen undetected.*

```
BAD (stops at a person):
1. Why did the SLO breach?           Cache lookup got slow.
2. Why did cache lookup get slow?    It's a list scan, not a dict lookup.
3. Why is it a list?                 It was written that way.
4. Why was it written that way?      Nobody thought about scale at review time.
   -> stops here. Action item: "be more careful." Untestable. Unenforceable.
   Will recur with the next feature nobody thinks about scale for either.

BLAMELESS (keeps going to a testable fact):
1. Why did the SLO breach?           Cache lookup is O(n); at 200,000 entries
                                      that's ~121ms added to every miss.
2. Why did that ship?                CI's test suite never exercised the
                                      cache above a few dozen entries.
3. Why didn't CI catch it?           No load-test stage seeds
                                      production-representative data volume
                                      before merge.
4. Why is there no such stage?       The CI/CD gate (AIE-303-M04) currently
                                      checks eval correctness, not
                                      data-scale performance.
   -> Action item: "add a production-scale load test to the CI gate."
   Testable. Assignable to a team. Prevents the NEXT unscaled data
   structure too, not just this one.
```

Both chains are honest about what happened. Only the second one produces an action item anyone could verify was actually done, and only the second one would have caught a *different* engineer making the *same* kind of scale-blind mistake next quarter — which is the actual measure of whether a postmortem did its job.

**Worked example.** This module's own two versions of the same incident's Root Cause section, side by side, are exactly the pair Lab M06-C's linter is built to tell apart:

```
BAD:   "Alex forgot to think about scale when writing the cache. It was
        basically human error -- Alex should have known better."

GOOD:  "CacheStore.lookup in oncall_service.py performs a linear scan
        over every cached entry (O(n) in cache size). At 200,000
        accumulated entries this adds ~121ms to every cache-miss
        request. No test exercised the cache at production-representative
        size before the feature shipped."
```

The BAD version is not merely unkind — it is *less informative*. It names a person and asserts a mental state ("forgot," "should have known") that is unfalsifiable and gives the next reader nothing to check or fix. The GOOD version names a function, a file, a measured number, and a testing gap — every one of those is something Lab M06-C's automated linter can verify actually got named, which is exactly why this module's rubric can enforce blamelessness *mechanically* instead of trusting tone.

**Named misconceptions**

- *"Blameless means no one is accountable."* Every action item in a passing postmortem still has an owner — a team or a process, e.g. `Owner: CI/CD Pipeline`, never left blank. Blameless changes *what* gets held accountable for prevention (a system, which can be changed) from *who* gets held accountable for the mistake (a person, who cannot be un-fallible) — it does not remove accountability, it relocates it somewhere a fix can actually attach.
- *"'Human error' is a valid root cause if that's really what happened."* "Human error" names a symptom — someone made a mistake, which is true of every incident ever, by every team, always — not a cause. The actual question a postmortem exists to answer is why the system let that ordinary, inevitable human mistake reach users undetected. A system that assumes zero human error is itself the design defect.
- *"A near-miss caught before user impact isn't worth writing up."* A near-miss is the cheapest data a postmortem process ever gets — no user-impact pressure biasing the write-up, and very often the exact same systemic gap that will cause a real incident the next time timing doesn't go your way. Skipping it because "nothing actually broke" throws away a free warning.

```python
# oncall_service.py (provided in full -- this is the system, not your exercise)
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class CacheEntry:
    query: str
    tokens: frozenset
    answer: str


class CacheStore:
    """A semantic response cache: a repeat (or near-repeat) question
    returns a stored answer instead of paying for a fresh generation.
    THE DEFECT: lookup() is a linear scan over a Python list, comparing
    the incoming query against every entry ever stored -- O(n) in the
    number of entries. It shipped this way because at development-time
    cache sizes (tens of entries) the scan is unmeasurably fast; nothing
    in code review or CI caught that its cost grows with accumulated
    production traffic, because nothing tested it at production-scale n."""

    def __init__(self, threshold: float = 0.9):
        self._entries: list[CacheEntry] = []
        self.threshold = threshold

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._entries)

    @staticmethod
    def _tokenize(text: str) -> frozenset:
        return frozenset(text.lower().split())

    def lookup(self, query: str) -> str | None:
        q_tokens = self._tokenize(query)
        for entry in self._entries:                     # <-- THE DEFECT
            inter = len(q_tokens & entry.tokens)
            union = len(q_tokens | entry.tokens)
            sim = inter / union if union else 0.0
            if sim >= self.threshold:
                return entry.answer
        return None

    def store(self, query: str, answer: str) -> None:
        self._entries.append(CacheEntry(query, self._tokenize(query), answer))


SETTINGS = {"cache_enabled": True}


def handle_chat(query: str, cache: CacheStore, generate_fn, stages: list | None = None) -> dict:
    """One request through the service: cache lookup, then (on a miss)
    generation and cache store. If `stages` is passed, append
    (stage_name, duration_ms) for each stage that ran -- this module's
    lightweight, compact stand-in for the M05 tracer: same discipline
    (structured, per-stage, timed), rebuilt small because this lab's
    point is diagnosis speed under a clock, not tracing infrastructure."""

    def record(name, t0):
        if stages is not None:
            stages.append((name, (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000.0))

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    cached = cache.lookup(query) if SETTINGS["cache_enabled"] else None
    record("cache_lookup", t0)

    if cached is not None:
        return {"query": query, "answer": cached, "cache_hit": True}

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    answer = generate_fn(query)
    record("generation", t0)

    if SETTINGS["cache_enabled"]:
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        cache.store(query, answer)
        record("cache_store", t0)

    return {"query": query, "answer": answer, "cache_hit": False}


def generate_answer(query: str) -> str:
    """Real generation path -- distilgpt2, CPU, greedy decode. Imported
    lazily so tests can run against handle_chat with a stub generate_fn
    and never need the model on disk."""
    import torch
    from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

    global _TOK, _MODEL
    try:
        _TOK
    except NameError:
        _TOK = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilgpt2")
        _MODEL = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("distilgpt2").eval()

    ids = _TOK(query, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = _MODEL.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=16, do_sample=False,
                               pad_token_id=_TOK.eos_token_id)
    return _TOK.decode(out[0][ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True)


# A minimal FastAPI wrapper -- the same handle_chat logic, reachable over
# HTTP, matching this course's serving toolchain (AIE-303-M01).
from fastapi import FastAPI
from pydantic import BaseModel

app = FastAPI()
_global_cache = CacheStore()

class ChatRequest(BaseModel):
    query: str

@app.post("/chat")
def chat(req: ChatRequest):
    return handle_chat(req.query, _global_cache, generate_answer)
```

```python
# solution: monitor.py (completed)
import time
import statistics
from dataclasses import dataclass
from oncall_service import handle_chat


@dataclass
class PagerAlert:
    p95_ms: float
    slo_ms: float
    breached: bool
    n_requests: int


def measure_p95(cache, generate_fn, queries: list[str]) -> float:
    latencies = []
    for q in queries:
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        handle_chat(q, cache, generate_fn)
        latencies.append((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000.0)
    latencies.sort()
    idx = max(0, int(round(0.95 * (len(latencies) - 1))))
    return latencies[idx]


def check_slo(cache, generate_fn, queries: list[str], slo_ms: float) -> PagerAlert:
    p95 = measure_p95(cache, generate_fn, queries)
    return PagerAlert(p95_ms=p95, slo_ms=slo_ms, breached=p95 > slo_ms,
                       n_requests=len(queries))


if __name__ == "__main__":
```

```python
import statistics
from oncall_service import handle_chat


def stage_breakdown(cache, generate_fn, queries: list[str]) -> dict[str, dict]:
    per_stage: dict[str, list[float]] = {}
    for q in queries:
        stages = []
        handle_chat(q, cache, generate_fn, stages=stages)
        for name, ms in stages:
            per_stage.setdefault(name, []).append(ms)

    grand_total = sum(sum(v) for v in per_stage.values())
    result = {}
    for name, values in per_stage.items():
        total = sum(values)
        result[name] = {
            "mean_ms": statistics.mean(values),
            "total_ms": total,
            "share_of_total": total / grand_total if grand_total else 0.0,
        }
    return result


def dominant_stage(breakdown: dict[str, dict]) -> str:
    return max(breakdown, key=lambda name: breakdown[name]["share_of_total"])
```

```python
class FastCacheStore:
    def __init__(self):
        self._index: dict[str, str] = {}

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._index)

    @staticmethod
    def _normalize(query: str) -> str:
        return " ".join(query.lower().split())

    def lookup(self, query: str) -> str | None:
        return self._index.get(self._normalize(query))

    def store(self, query: str, answer: str) -> None:
        self._index[self._normalize(query)] = answer
```

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

REQUIRED_SECTIONS = [
    "## Timeline", "## Impact", "## Root Cause",
    "## Contributing Factors", "## Mitigation", "## Action Items",
]

# Phrases that locate the cause in a PERSON's judgment/competence rather
# than a system or process. Deliberately blunt -- this is a rubric a
# learner can read and satisfy before submitting, not a subtle
# classifier to reverse-engineer around.
BLAME_PHRASES = [
    "forgot to", "didn't think", "should have known", "should have thought",
    "was careless", "human error", "developer error", "engineer error",
    "engineer's mistake", "engineers mistake", "made a mistake",
    "wasn't paying attention", "his fault", "her fault", "their fault",
    "was responsible for the bug", "failed to notice", "should have caught",
    "not experienced enough", "junior mistake", "rushed the change",
]

# Catches "Alex forgot to..." even when the exact phrase above isn't used.
NAME_BLAME_PATTERN = re.compile(
    r"\b([A-Z][a-z]+)\s+(wrote|forgot|missed|didn't|did not|failed to|"
    r"was responsible|made a mistake|should have|overlooked)\b"
)

COMMON_SENTENCE_STARTS = {
    "The", "This", "That", "A", "An", "It", "We", "Our", "During",
    "After", "Before", "Once", "When", "While", "Because", "Since",
}

ALLOWED_OWNERS = {
    "Serving Team", "SRE", "CI/CD Pipeline", "On-Call Rotation",
    "Platform Team", "Code Review Process", "Load Testing Process",
}

ACTION_ITEM_PATTERN = re.compile(r"^- \[ \] .+ — Owner: (.+)$", re.MULTILINE)


@dataclass
class LintResult:
    passed: bool
    missing_sections: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    blame_violations: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    ungrounded_root_cause: bool = False
    bad_owners: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    no_action_items: bool = False


def find_blame_violations(text: str) -> list[str]:
    violations = []
    lower = text.lower()
    for phrase in BLAME_PHRASES:
        if phrase in lower:
            violations.append(f'banned phrase: "{phrase}"')
    for match in NAME_BLAME_PATTERN.finditer(text):
        name = match.group(1)
        if name not in COMMON_SENTENCE_STARTS:
            violations.append(f'name-blame pattern: "{match.group(0)}"')
    return violations


def _section_text(text: str, header: str) -> str:
    pattern = re.compile(rf"{re.escape(header)}\s*\n(.*?)(?=\n## |\Z)", re.DOTALL)
    m = pattern.search(text)
    return m.group(1) if m else ""


def lint(text: str, code_symbols: set[str]) -> LintResult:
    result = LintResult(passed=True)

    for section in REQUIRED_SECTIONS:
        if section not in text:
            result.missing_sections.append(section)

    result.blame_violations = find_blame_violations(text)

    root_cause = _section_text(text, "## Root Cause")
    result.ungrounded_root_cause = not any(sym in root_cause for sym in code_symbols)

    owners = ACTION_ITEM_PATTERN.findall(text)
    result.no_action_items = len(owners) == 0
    result.bad_owners = [o for o in owners if o not in ALLOWED_OWNERS]

    result.passed = (
        not result.missing_sections
        and not result.blame_violations
        and not result.ungrounded_root_cause
        and not result.no_action_items
        and not result.bad_owners
    )
    return result
```

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — on-call response and the blameless postmortem, not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground.** Blameless postmortem culture is a documented, named practice in the SRE field, not a values statement invented for this course — Etsy popularized it publicly, and Google's *Site Reliability Engineering* codifies it as a formal chapter, precisely because incident-response cultures that assigned individual blame measurably produced worse outcomes: engineers learned to hide near-misses, delay reporting, and under-instrument systems to avoid leaving evidence against themselves, because a name-shaped root cause makes every future honest report a personal risk. This is organizational history that can be checked, not an opinion this module is asking you to adopt on faith — and it is the specific, concrete case of this course's own Tier 1 claim that most ML failure in the wild is systems failure: the SLO breach in this module traces to an untested data structure, not a person's competence, and the record of the field bears that pattern out at scale.
- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "Blameless" gets treated by plenty of organizations as a *tone* requirement — strip the names, soften the language, keep the exact same template — while the mechanism that actually matters (whether the CI pipeline gets a real new gate, whether the on-call rotation gets the new alert, whether "add a load test" competes successfully against the next feature sprint for engineering time) is a resourcing and prioritization question the tone of the document does nothing to settle. A blameless-sounding postmortem whose action items never get funded is functionally identical to a blaming one: the incident recurs either way. This module's linter can mechanically verify tone — no name, a real owner, a grounded cause — and it structurally *cannot* verify that "Owner: CI/CD Pipeline" ever received the engineering time the action item assumed. That gap, between a well-formed document and an actually-changed system, is exactly the space where "reliability" gets measured (the linter passes, the postmortem is filed, the metric looks good) while the thing the metric was supposed to protect against — the recurrence — goes unmeasured until it happens again.
- **Tier 3 — sit with this.** This module's rubric can mechanically enforce that a postmortem never *names* a person as the cause. It cannot enforce, and no rubric can, that the person who wrote the buggy line, the person who happened to be on-call when it broke, and the person who has to write the document describing it are frequently the same human being — who will read every carefully depersonalized sentence about "the system" and privately know exactly which decisions were theirs. Structural blamelessness protects the organization's incentive to report honestly next time; it does not touch, and was never built to touch, what that person feels reading their own mistake rendered in the passive voice. Every measurement this module hands you — p95 latency, time-to-mitigate, lint pass/fail — is a measurement of the system. None of them is a measurement of what it costs the person who wrote `CacheStore.lookup` to sit in the room while it's discussed, however carefully the document avoids their name. What would it take to build an incident practice that is honest about that gap — that a postmortem can be correctly, rigorously blameless and a person can still carry it personally — instead of a practice that quietly treats removing the name as having removed the weight?

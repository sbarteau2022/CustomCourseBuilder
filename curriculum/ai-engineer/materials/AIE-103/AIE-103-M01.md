## Lessons

Four instruments, built from the substrate up:

1. **The vector and the dot product** — a 1-D array is data; the dot product is the first operation. `(n,)·(n,) → scalar`.
2. **The matrix–vector product** — a matrix is a stack of dot products, i.e. a function from vectors to vectors. `(m,n)@(n,) → (m,)`.
3. **The matrix–matrix product and the shape contract** — `(m,n)@(n,p) → (m,p)`; the inner dims must meet. The `(n,)` vs `(n,1)` trap, and the shape error before it happens.
4. **Broadcasting** — the rule that lets `(m,n)` and `(n,)` combine, aligned from the right — and the silent expansion that gives you `(m,n)` of garbage with no error at all.

### Lesson 1 — The vector and the dot product

#### Teaching text

A **vector** is the simplest object in this course: an ordered list of numbers. In symbols we write $\mathbf{a} = [a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n]$; in NumPy it is a 1-D array with **shape** `(n,)`. That trailing comma is not a typo — `(n,)` is a one-element tuple, and it is telling you something exact: *this array has one axis, of length n.* Not a row, not a column — a flat run of `n` numbers. Half of all shape confusion in your career comes from a vector secretly being `(n,)` when you pictured it as a `(1, n)` row or an `(n, 1)` column. We will make that distinction load-bearing in Lesson 3; for now, burn in the fact that a plain vector has **one** axis.

The first *operation* on vectors is the **dot product**. In symbols:

$$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i$$

That is the entire definition: multiply the two vectors element by element, then add up the results. It eats two vectors of the same length and returns **one number** — a scalar. Everything grand that the notation later implies (similarity, projection, a neuron's pre-activation, a single cell of a matrix product) is *this sum and nothing more.* Descend to it once by hand so the symbol never intimidates you again.

By hand, for $\mathbf{a} = [1, 2, 3]$ and $\mathbf{b} = [4, 5, 6]$:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = (1)(4) + (2)(5) + (3)(6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32$$

In NumPy, three ways that all compute exactly that sum:

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([1, 2, 3])
b = np.array([4, 5, 6])

a @ b            # 32   — the @ operator IS the dot product for 1-D arrays
np.dot(a, b)     # 32
(a * b).sum()    # 32   — a * b is [4, 10, 18] elementwise, then sum -> 32
```

Look hard at the third form, because it exposes the machinery. `a * b` is **not** the dot product — it is the elementwise product `[4, 10, 18]`, still a vector of shape `(3,)`. The dot product is that vector *summed*. Two different operations, and the `*` operator gives you the elementwise one, never the dot. Confusing `*` with `@` is the single most common NumPy error in this course; the fix is to always ask "am I multiplying, or am I multiplying-then-summing?"

The shape contract for the dot product is strict and worth stating as a rule you will predict against:

> **Dot-product contract:** `(n,) · (n,) → ()`. Both operands must have the *same* length `n`. The result is a scalar (shape `()`, the empty tuple). Mismatched lengths raise `ValueError`.

```python
np.array([1, 2, 3]) @ np.array([4, 5])   # ValueError: shapes (3,) and (2,) not aligned
```

That error is not bad luck — it is *predictable from the shapes alone.* You could have said "3 ≠ 2, this cannot dot" before running it. That prediction is the skill.

#### Worked example — what the dot product *measures*

The dot product is the substrate under the word "similarity," and you can feel it with three unit-ish vectors. Take a fixed vector $\mathbf{a} = [1, 0]$ (pointing along the x-axis) and dot it with three others:

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
np.dot(a, [1.0, 0.0])   #  1.0  — same direction
np.dot(a, [0.0, 1.0])   #  0.0  — perpendicular
np.dot(a, [-1.0, 0.0])  # -1.0  — opposite direction
```

Same-direction gives the largest value, perpendicular gives exactly zero, opposite gives the most negative. The dot product is measuring *alignment*: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$. You do not need that formula yet — you need to have *watched* the sum-of-products go from +1 to 0 to −1 as the second vector swung around, and to know that the "cosine similarity" a paper cites in AIE-202 is this exact sum, divided by the two lengths. The symbol buys you a similarity score; the substrate is $\sum a_i b_i$.

#### Misconceptions to surface (name them out loud)

- **"`a * b` is the dot product."** No — `*` is *elementwise* multiplication and returns a vector the same shape as its inputs; the dot product is that vector summed. Surface it by printing both: `a * b` is `[4, 10, 18]` (shape `(3,)`), `a @ b` is `32` (a scalar). The learner who predicts `a * b == 32` just met the most expensive habit in NumPy.
- **"A vector is a row (or a column)."** No — a plain 1-D array has *one* axis, shape `(n,)`; it is neither a `(1, n)` row nor an `(n, 1)` column until you deliberately reshape it. This looks pedantic now and becomes the whole ballgame in Lesson 3. Surface it with `np.array([1,2,3]).shape` → `(3,)`, one number in the tuple.
- **"The dot product returns a vector."** No — it collapses two vectors to a single scalar; that collapse (the `sum`) is the entire point. Surface it by checking `type(a @ b)` and `(a @ b).shape` → `()`.

### Lesson 2 — The matrix–vector product

#### Teaching text

A **matrix** is a rectangle of numbers with two axes: `m` rows and `n` columns, shape `(m, n)`. In NumPy, row-major, the *first* axis indexes rows and the *second* indexes columns — `A[i, j]` is row `i`, column `j`. Say the shape out loud as "m by n" and always in that order; the entire module's shape discipline rests on never flipping those two.

But a matrix is not just storage. The instrument this lesson builds is the **matrix–vector product** $A\mathbf{x}$, and the cleanest way to understand it is: *a matrix is a stack of row-vectors, and $A\mathbf{x}$ dots every row with $\mathbf{x}$.* The result's $i$-th entry is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{x}$:

$$(A\mathbf{x})_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}\, x_j$$

So $A\mathbf{x}$ is just Lesson 1 done `m` times — one dot product per row. That immediately tells you two things: the length of $\mathbf{x}$ must equal the number of *columns* of $A$ (so each row-dot is legal), and the result has one entry per *row*, so its length is `m`.

> **Matrix–vector contract:** `(m, n) @ (n,) → (m,)`. The vector's length must match `A`'s **second** axis (columns). The output length is `A`'s **first** axis (rows). The shared `n` is *consumed* — it appears in both inputs and vanishes from the output.

By hand, with

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{shape } (2,3)), \qquad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{shape } (3,))$$

we compute one dot product per row:

- Row 0: $(1)(1) + (2)(0) + (3)(-1) = 1 + 0 - 3 = -2$
- Row 1: $(4)(1) + (5)(0) + (6)(-1) = 4 + 0 - 6 = -2$

So $A\mathbf{x} = [-2, -2]$, shape `(2,)`. Note the `n = 3` got consumed: two `(3,)` things went in per row, a scalar came out, and we have `m = 2` of them.

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3],
              [4, 5, 6]])       # shape (2, 3)
x = np.array([1, 0, -1])        # shape (3,)

A @ x                           # array([-2, -2])   shape (2,)
(A @ x).shape                   # (2,)

# proof that it's row-wise dot products:
np.array([A[0] @ x, A[1] @ x])  # array([-2, -2])  — identical
```

Here is the mental move that pays off forever: **read `A @ x` as "consume the shared axis."** `(2,3) @ (3,)` — the two 3's touch and cancel, leaving `(2,)`. If they don't match, there is no legal product and NumPy raises before doing any arithmetic. You can see that failure coming purely from `(2,3)` and `(2,)`: the `3` and the `2` don't meet, so it cannot run.

#### Worked example — the matrix as a function that reshapes space

The reason $A\mathbf{x}$ is *the* operation of this course is that it is a **function**: feed it a vector of length `n`, get back a vector of length `m`. A `(3, 3)` matrix maps 3-vectors to 3-vectors; a `(2, 3)` matrix takes 3-vectors *down* to 2-vectors (a projection — the seed of AIE-103-M02); a `(4, 3)` matrix lifts 3-vectors *up* to 4-vectors (what a neural network layer does — the seed of AIE-201). Watch a single matrix act on three different inputs:

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, 0, 0],
              [0, 1, 0]])        # (2,3): "keep x and y, drop z"

A @ np.array([5, 7, 9])          # array([5, 7])   — z=9 discarded
A @ np.array([5, 7, 999])        # array([5, 7])   — z ignored entirely
A @ np.array([1, 2, 3])          # array([1, 2])
```

This `A` is a projection: it takes any 3-D point and returns its shadow on the xy-plane, throwing the z-coordinate away. You just watched a `(2,3)` matrix *be* a function from 3-D to 2-D — and you could have predicted the output shape `(2,)` from `(2,3) @ (3,)` before running a line. When a paper writes $\mathbf{h} = W\mathbf{x}$ for a layer, `W`'s shape *is* the layer's input-and-output sizes; reading the shape reads the architecture.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"`x`'s length must match the number of rows of `A`."** No — it must match the number of **columns** (the second axis), because each *row* is dotted with `x`. Surface it with `(2,3) @ (3,)` working and `(2,3) @ (2,)` failing: the vector meets the columns, not the rows.
- **"`A @ x` and `x @ A` are the same."** No — order matters and so do shapes. `(2,3) @ (3,)` gives `(2,)`; `(3,) @ (2,3)` fails because `3 ≠ 2` on the touching axes. Surface it by predicting both, then running both.
- **"The output has the same length as the input."** Only when `A` is square. A `(2,3)` matrix takes length-3 in and gives length-2 out — the shape *changes*, which is the entire reason matrices are interesting. Surface it by reading the output length straight off `A`'s first axis.

### Lesson 3 — The matrix–matrix product and the shape contract

#### Teaching text

The matrix–matrix product $AB$ generalizes Lesson 2 by one step: instead of one vector on the right, put several vectors side by side as the columns of a matrix `B`. Then $AB$ is "$A$ times each column of $B$," laid out as columns of the result. Equivalently, and this is the formula to hold in your head:

$$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik} B_{kj}$$

The `(i, j)` cell of the product is **the dot product of row `i` of `A` with column `j` of `B`.** Every cell is a Lesson-1 dot product. Nothing new happens — you just do `m × p` of them.

That immediately forces the shape contract, the single most useful rule in this entire module:

> **Matrix–matrix contract:** `(m, n) @ (n, p) → (m, p)`. The **inner** dimensions must match (`A`'s columns = `B`'s rows), because you dot a row of `A` (length `n`) with a column of `B` (length `n`). The inner `n` is *consumed*; the **outer** dimensions `m` and `p` survive into the result. Inner mismatch → `ValueError`.

Say it as a ritual: **"inner must match, outer survive."** Write the two shapes next to each other, `(m, n)(n, p)`; the two middle numbers must be equal and they disappear; the two outer numbers are your answer's shape. This is how you predict a shape error before it happens.

By hand, with

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \ (2,3), \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \ (3,2)$$

`(2,3) @ (3,2)`: inner `3 = 3` ✓, so the product is `(2, 2)`. Each cell is a row·column dot:

- $(AB)_{00} = \text{row}_0 \cdot \text{col}_0 = (1)(1)+(2)(3)+(3)(5) = 1+6+15 = 22$
- $(AB)_{01} = \text{row}_0 \cdot \text{col}_1 = (1)(2)+(2)(4)+(3)(6) = 2+8+18 = 28$
- $(AB)_{10} = \text{row}_1 \cdot \text{col}_0 = (4)(1)+(5)(3)+(6)(5) = 4+15+30 = 49$
- $(AB)_{11} = \text{row}_1 \cdot \text{col}_1 = (4)(2)+(5)(4)+(6)(6) = 8+20+36 = 64$

$$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 22 & 28 \\ 49 & 64 \end{bmatrix}$$

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])     # (2,3)
B = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4], [5, 6]])   # (3,2)

A @ B            # array([[22, 28], [49, 64]])
(A @ B).shape    # (2, 2)

B @ A            # array([[ 9, 12, 15], [19, 26, 33], [29, 40, 51]]) — (3,3)!
(B @ A).shape    # (3, 3)   — DIFFERENT SHAPE, different meaning
```

`A @ B` is `(2,2)` and `B @ A` is `(3,3)`: matrix multiplication is **not commutative**, and the shapes alone prove it — `(2,3)(3,2)→(2,2)` but `(3,2)(2,3)→(3,3)`. The order is part of the meaning.

**Now the trap that generates half of all real shape bugs: `(n,)` vs `(n,1)`.** A 1-D vector, a row, and a column *look* the same when you print them, but they are three different shapes and they behave differently under `@` and under broadcasting (Lesson 4). Transpose, `.T`, does **nothing** to a 1-D array — this astonishes people:

```python
import numpy as np
v = np.array([1, 2, 3])
v.shape        # (3,)
v.T.shape      # (3,)   — transpose of a 1-D array is a NO-OP. It is NOT a column.

col = v.reshape(3, 1)    # (3,1)  — an explicit column vector
row = v.reshape(1, 3)    # (1,3)  — an explicit row vector
col.T.shape              # (1,3)  — NOW transpose does something
```

If you expected `v.T` to give you a column vector, you carried a math-class picture (where every vector is secretly a column) into NumPy, where a 1-D array is genuinely axis-less-until-reshaped. The instrument you need is: **when a shape matters, make it 2-D explicitly** with `.reshape(-1, 1)` for a column or `.reshape(1, -1)` for a row, and never rely on `.T` to promote a 1-D array. This one habit prevents a category of bug that otherwise recurs for years.

#### Worked example — reading a shape error before it happens

Here is a chain like the ones you will actually write, stacking three transforms. Predict the shape at each step *before* looking, using only "inner match, outer survive":

```python
import numpy as np
X = np.random.randn(100, 4)   # 100 samples, 4 features   -> (100, 4)
W1 = np.random.randn(4, 8)    # layer 1: 4 -> 8           -> (4, 8)
W2 = np.random.randn(8, 3)    # layer 2: 8 -> 3           -> (8, 3)

H = X @ W1        # (100,4)@(4,8): inner 4=4 ✓  -> (100, 8)
Y = H @ W2        # (100,8)@(8,3): inner 8=8 ✓  -> (100, 3)
Y.shape           # (100, 3)
```

Every arrow was predictable. Now break it deliberately and *predict the failure*:

```python
Bad = X @ W2      # (100,4)@(8,3): inner 4 ≠ 8  -> ValueError, and you knew it
# ValueError: matmul: Input operand ... has a mismatch in its core dimension ...
#             (size 8 is different from 4)
```

You did not need to run `X @ W2` to know it dies — the inner dimensions `4` and `8` don't meet. A learner who reads that line and says "that's `(100,4)@(8,3)`, inner 4 vs 8, it can't run" *before* pressing enter has acquired the outcome of this entire module. The shape error stopped being a surprise and became a prediction.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"`AB = BA`."** No — matrix multiplication is not commutative; often `BA` isn't even a legal shape. Surface it with `A @ B` → `(2,2)` vs `B @ A` → `(3,3)`: different shapes, so they can't possibly be equal.
- **"`v.T` turns a vector into a column."** No — `.T` is a no-op on a 1-D array; `(3,).T` is still `(3,)`. Surface it with `v.T.shape == v.shape`, then show `v.reshape(3,1).T.shape == (1,3)` to prove `.T` only *acts* on 2-D. This is the sneakiest bug in the module.
- **"The outer dimensions must match."** Backwards — the **inner** dimensions must match; the **outer** ones become the result and are free to differ. Surface it by contrasting `(m,n)@(n,p)` (legal, inner `n` shared) with `(m,n)@(m,p)` (illegal unless `n==m`).
- **"`*` multiplies matrices."** No — `*` is elementwise and requires equal (or broadcastable) shapes; `@` is the matrix product. `A * B` on two `(2,3)`s multiplies cell-by-cell; `A @ B` needs `(2,3)@(3,?)`. Surface it by showing `*` fails or does something different from `@`.

### Lesson 4 — Broadcasting

#### Teaching text

You will constantly need to combine arrays of *different* shapes — subtract a length-`n` mean from an `(m, n)` data matrix, scale every row of a matrix by a per-row factor, add a bias vector to a batch of activations. **Broadcasting** is the rule NumPy uses to stretch a smaller array across a larger one without copying data. It is enormously convenient and it is the source of the module's most dangerous bug: the operation that produces *the wrong shape with no error at all.*

The rule has exactly two steps. Line the two shapes up **from the right** (trailing axes aligned), then for each axis position:

1. The lengths are **compatible** if they are **equal**, or if **one of them is 1** (the length-1 axis is stretched to match the other).
2. If a shape is shorter, it is treated as having leading 1's until the ranks match.

If any aligned pair is neither equal nor has a 1, the shapes are incompatible → `ValueError`.

> **Broadcasting contract:** align shapes on the **right**; each axis must be equal or contain a 1; a 1 stretches to the other length; missing leading axes count as 1.

Work three cases by hand. Data matrix `X` is `(4, 3)` — 4 samples, 3 features.

**Case A — subtract the column mean (the correct, everyday operation).** The per-feature mean has shape `(3,)`:

```
   X:   (4, 3)
  mu:      (3,)     <- align right; mu gets a leading 1 -> (1, 3)
result:  (4, 3)     <- axis0: 4 vs 1 -> stretch to 4; axis1: 3 vs 3 -> ok
```

```python
import numpy as np
X = np.arange(12).reshape(4, 3).astype(float)   # (4,3)
mu = X.mean(axis=0)                              # (3,) — mean of each column/feature
mu.shape                                         # (3,)
(X - mu).shape                                   # (4, 3)  ✓ each row had mu subtracted
```

**Case B — the operation that *raises* (a genuine mismatch).** The per-*sample* mean has shape `(4,)`, one number per row. Subtract it naively:

```
   X:   (4, 3)
  mu:      (4,)     <- align right; mu becomes (1, 4)
result:   ERROR     <- axis1: 3 vs 4, neither equal nor 1 -> ValueError
```

```python
mu_row = X.mean(axis=1)      # (4,) — mean of each ROW
X - mu_row                   # ValueError: operands could not be broadcast
                             #             together with shapes (4,3) (4,)
```

This error is a *gift*: it tells you your shapes disagree before you compute garbage. To subtract a per-row value you must make it an explicit column, `(4, 1)`, so it broadcasts across the 3 feature columns:

```python
(X - mu_row.reshape(4, 1)).shape    # (4, 3) ✓  — or mu_row[:, None]
```

**Case C — the SILENT bug (the one this module exists to inoculate against).** Now the same intent, but with a `(4, 1)` column and a `(4,)` vector — and broadcasting *helpfully* does something you never wanted, with **no error**:

```python
scores = np.array([10., 20., 30., 40.])   # (4,)
col    = scores.reshape(4, 1)             # (4,1)
out    = scores - col                     # NO error. What shape?
```

Align right: `(4,)` becomes `(1, 4)`; against `(4, 1)`:

```
 col:  (4, 1)
 sco:  (1, 4)
 out:  (4, 4)     <- axis0: 4 vs 1 -> 4;  axis1: 1 vs 4 -> 4
```

You get a `(4, 4)` matrix — every pairwise difference `scores[j] - scores[i]` — when you almost certainly wanted a `(4,)` vector. **NumPy did not complain.** Your downstream code keeps running on a `(4, 4)` where it expected `(4,)`, and the bug surfaces a hundred lines later as a nonsensical result or a *different* shape error whose true cause is here. This is why "it ran" is never evidence of correctness in numerical code, and why the discipline is to **predict the output shape and compare it to the shape you intended** — not to wait for an exception that broadcasting is specifically designed not to throw.

The instrument that kills Case C: whenever you broadcast, write the two shapes down, align them right, and predict the output shape. If the predicted shape isn't the shape your *intent* needs, you have found the bug on paper, before it ran.

#### Worked example — the outer product falls out of the rule

Case C's "surprise" is actually a named, useful operation — the **outer product** — which shows that broadcasting is coherent, not capricious. `a[:, None] * b[None, :]` gives every product `a_i b_j`:

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([1, 2, 3])       # (3,)
b = np.array([10, 20])        # (2,)

a[:, None]                    # (3,1)  — a as a column
b[None, :]                    # (1,2)  — b as a row
a[:, None] * b[None, :]       # (3,2):
# array([[10, 20],
#        [20, 40],
#        [30, 60]])
```

Align `(3,1)` and `(1,2)`: axis0 is `3 vs 1 → 3`, axis1 is `1 vs 2 → 2`, output `(3, 2)`, and cell `(i,j)` is `a_i * b_j`. The same rule that *bit* you in Case C *builds* the outer product here — the difference is entirely whether the `(3,2)` was your intent. Broadcasting is never doing something arbitrary; it is doing exactly what the aligned shapes dictate. Your job is to know what they dictate before you run.

#### Misconceptions to surface

- **"If it doesn't raise an error, the shapes were right."** The most dangerous belief in the module. Broadcasting is *designed* to combine unequal shapes silently; a run with no exception can still be producing a `(4,4)` where you needed `(4,)`. Surface it with Case C — no error, wrong shape — then have them check `out.shape` against their stated intent.
- **"Broadcasting aligns from the left."** No — it aligns trailing axes, i.e. **from the right**, padding missing *leading* axes with 1. Surface it with `(4,3)` vs `(3,)`: the `3`s line up on the right and it works; `(4,3)` vs `(4,)` lines `3` against `4` and fails.
- **"A `(n,)` vector and a `(1,n)` row are interchangeable in broadcasting."** Usually yes by luck, but a `(n,)` and an `(n,1)` are *not* — they broadcast to `(n,n)`. Surface it with `scores - scores.reshape(-1,1)` giving `(4,4)`. The trailing-comma shape you ignored in Lesson 1 is what decides.
- **"Subtracting the mean always works."** Only if the mean's shape aligns on the right with the data. `X.mean(axis=0)` is `(n,)` and subtracts cleanly; `X.mean(axis=1)` is `(m,)` and *raises* — you must reshape it to `(m,1)`. Surface it with Cases A and B side by side.

```python
import numpy as np

def matmul(A, B):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
    B = np.asarray(B, dtype=float)
    m, n = A.shape
    n2, p = B.shape
    if n != n2:
        raise ValueError(
            f"inner dimensions must match: A is (m,n)=({m},{n}), "
            f"B is (n,p)=({n2},{p}); {n} != {n2}"
        )
    C = np.zeros((m, p))
    for i in range(m):          # each output row
        for j in range(p):      # each output column
            s = 0.0
            for k in range(n):  # the consumed inner axis: dot row i of A with col j of B
                s += A[i, k] * B[k, j]
            C[i, j] = s
    return C
```

Starter (`broadcasting.py`) — one function is subtly wrong; the tests will pin down where:

```python
import numpy as np

def center_features(X):
    """Subtract each FEATURE's mean (per column). Result shape == X.shape."""
    mu = X.mean(axis=0)          # (n_features,)
    return X - mu                # broadcasts (m,n)-(n,) -> (m,n)  ✓

def center_samples_BUGGY(X):
    """INTENDED: subtract each SAMPLE's mean (per row). Result shape == X.shape.
    This implementation is wrong on purpose — find it with a shape prediction."""
    mu = X.mean(axis=1)          # (m,)  <-- per-row mean
    return X - mu                # (m,n)-(m,) : does this do what we want?

def center_samples(X):
    """Correct version: subtract each sample's mean, keeping shape (m,n)."""
    mu = X.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)   # (m,1) — stays 2-D so it broadcasts across columns
    return X - mu
```

Reference solution: `center_features` and `center_samples` above are correct; `center_samples_BUGGY` raises `ValueError` for non-square `X` (e.g. `(5,3)`: `(5,3)-(5,)` → `3 vs 5`), and — the insidious part — for *square* `X` it does **not** raise but subtracts the wrong thing (a `(n,)` broadcast down the columns, i.e. per-*column* values, not per-row). The fix is `keepdims=True` (or `.reshape(-1, 1)`), which makes the mean `(m, 1)` so it broadcasts across features.

## Three-tier reading

Specific to this module's subject — *the shape as a contract, and the operation that fails silently* — not boilerplate.

- **Tier 1 — material ground (verifiable, reproducible today).** A shape is a contract you can check on paper before running. `(m,n) @ (n,p)` is legal iff the inner dimensions match, and the result is `(m,p)` — verify with any `A`, `B` and `.shape`. `A @ B` and `B @ A` generally have *different shapes*, so matrix multiplication is provably non-commutative. A 1-D array has one axis, shape `(n,)`; `v.T` does nothing to it. Broadcasting aligns trailing axes and stretches length-1 axes, so `X - X.mean(axis=0)` centers features cleanly, while `scores - scores.reshape(-1,1)` silently returns an `(n,n)` matrix with **no error at all**. Every one of these is reproducible with NumPy and `.shape` in under a minute; it is documented behavior, not opinion.

- **Tier 2 — what the field suppresses.** "NumPy is intuitive, broadcasting just does the right thing" and "if it runs, the shapes were fine" hide the same thing: the most dangerous shape bug throws *no exception*. The convenience that lets you subtract a `(n,)` mean from an `(m,n)` matrix in one clean line is the *same mechanism* that turns a `(n,)`-vs-`(n,1)` mixup into an `(n,n)` matrix of garbage your code keeps running on. The booster can't admit the ergonomics and the silent-failure surface are one feature, not two; the "just be careful" critic can't admit that carefulness without a *predict-the-shape discipline* is unfalsifiable advice. Both are half true. Shape checking is not the tax you pay for using NumPy — it *is* the skill NumPy's convenience quietly relocates onto you, and the ability to predict a shape (and a shape-error) before running is exactly the competence the "it's intuitive" framing tells you that you don't need.

- **Tier 3 — sit with this (a question you can't unknow).** When a model's output shape happens to line up and the pipeline runs green — no exception, a plausible number at the end — how would anyone on your team ever discover that a `(n,)` was broadcast into an `(n,n)` fifty lines up, silently averaging the wrong axis, if no one predicted the shape it *should* have been? Which number in your model's report is the product of a broadcast nobody drew on paper — and if an auditor asked you to derive its shape by hand, from the inputs, could you?

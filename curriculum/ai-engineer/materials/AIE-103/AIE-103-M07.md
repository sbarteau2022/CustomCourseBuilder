# AIE-103-M07 — Information theory: entropy, cross-entropy, KL as distance

**Foundation tier · 16 learner-hours · Prereq: AIE-103-M04 (probability, distributions, expectation), AIE-103-M03 (chain rule, gradients) · Co-req: AIE-101 (Python + NumPy fluent); recommended alongside AIE-103-M06 (loss landscapes)**

> **The descent, stated plainly.** M04 gave you a distribution and taught you to compute its expectation — a weighted average, one dot product. M06 gave you a loss landscape and a rule for walking downhill on it. This module goes below the word that sits at the exact intersection of those two ideas and that you will read in every model card, every training log, and every paper's Table 1 without exception — *loss* — specifically the loss spelled **cross-entropy**, and the two words that explain what it is secretly measuring: **entropy** and **KL divergence**. Each is a small, concrete arithmetic, and each is an expectation, so M04's dot-product intuition carries straight through. **Entropy** is the expected surprise of a distribution — one number, in bits or nats, that says how hard a source is to predict, and (Shannon proved this, not just suggested it) the fewest bits per message you could possibly compress it to on average. **Cross-entropy** is what happens when you measure surprise using the *wrong* distribution — your model's guess `q` — while the messages actually come from the true distribution `p`. **KL divergence** is the extra cost of that mismatch: cross-entropy minus entropy, the exact number of extra bits your wrong guess is costing you, and — because it is *directed*, not symmetric — the single fact that keeps generative-model training and classifier training from meaning the same thing even though both minimize "a divergence." You will compute all three by hand on distributions small enough to hold in your head, in NumPy on distributions that don't overflow when you're careless, and you will predict — before computing — which of two distributions has higher entropy, and which of two mismatched guesses costs more nats. When AIE-201 hands you `nn.CrossEntropyLoss()` and it prints a number after every batch, you will know exactly what that number is an expectation *of*, why it collapses to `-ln(q_true)` for a one-hot label, and why the softmax-plus-cross-entropy gradient your framework computes for you is the single cleanest expression you will see all year: `q − y`.

> **The loop under every lab is AI-PRIMM.** Predict (on paper, before code) → Prompt → Read → Verify → Integrate. In information theory the prediction is almost always *a ranking, a number, and a sign or bound*: "distribution A has higher entropy than B, because A is closer to uniform," "the cross-entropy loss for this one-hot label is exactly `−ln(q_2)`, so it's about `0.408` nats," "`D_KL(p‖q)` and `D_KL(q‖p)` will *not* match, and I expect the reverse one to be larger because `q` is more spread out than `p`," "this KL divergence cannot come out negative, no matter what numbers I plug in." The evidence is: **your predicted ranking, magnitude, or sign matched the measured one, and where it didn't, your written revision says why.** That trail is what the assessment grades.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — Entropy: the average surprise, measured in bits

**The teaching.** Start from one idea: an outcome that was unlikely is *surprising* when it happens, and an outcome that was certain is not surprising at all. Shannon made "surprise" precise as a formula, not a feeling:

```
surprisal(x) = −log₂ p(x)          (bits)
```

Read the formula, don't just memorize it. If `p(x) = 1` (certain), `surprisal = −log₂ 1 = 0` — a certain event tells you nothing new when it happens, zero bits of surprise. If `p(x) = 0.5` (a coin flip), `surprisal = −log₂ 0.5 = 1` — exactly one bit, the amount of surprise in "which of two equally likely things happened." If `p(x) = 0.001` (rare), `surprisal ≈ 9.97` bits — a rare event is *very* surprising when it lands. The base-2 log is not decoration: it is calibrated so that "one fair coin flip" comes out to exactly `1`, which is the whole reason information is measured in **bits** — literally, binary yes/no questions.

**Entropy** is the *expected* surprisal of a distribution — the M04 move, a weighted average, applied to the surprisal of every outcome:

```
H(p) = E_{x~p}[ −log₂ p(x) ]  =  − Σᵢ pᵢ · log₂ pᵢ
```

Read `H(p)` as: *if you keep drawing from `p` and averaging how surprised you are each time, this is the number you converge to.* It has an operational meaning that is not metaphorical — Shannon's source coding theorem says `H(p)` (in bits) is the fewest bits **per symbol, on average**, that *any* lossless code can use to communicate outcomes drawn from `p`. Entropy is not a vibe about "disorder." It is a hard lower bound on compression, provable, and you can watch real compressors approach it.

Two extremes anchor the intuition. A distribution that puts all its mass on one outcome (`p = [1, 0, 0, …]`) has `H = 0` — no surprise ever, because there is nothing to learn from the outcome. A distribution spread as evenly as possible over `n` outcomes (`p = [1/n, …, 1/n]`) has the *maximum possible entropy for that many outcomes*: `H = log₂ n`. Everything else sits between `0` and `log₂ n`, and where a distribution sits on that scale is exactly what "high entropy" and "low entropy" mean — not more outcomes, not more complexity, but more *evenness of mass*.

**Worked example — predicting a ranking, then computing it.**

Four distributions, ranked by eye before any arithmetic:

```
A: fair coin        [0.5, 0.5]
B: biased coin       [0.9, 0.1]
C: uniform over 4    [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]
D: skewed over 4     [0.97, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01]
```

Predict the order from lowest entropy to highest using only "more spread-out mass = more surprise on average": `D` should be lowest (almost certain which outcome), then `B` (still lopsided), then `A` (maximally even over 2 outcomes), then `C` (maximally even over 4 outcomes, the largest space here). Now compute, in bits, using `log₂(0.9) ≈ −0.15200`, `log₂(0.1) ≈ −3.32193`, `log₂(0.97) ≈ −0.04394`, `log₂(0.01) ≈ −6.64386`:

```
H(A) = −(0.5·log₂0.5 + 0.5·log₂0.5)              = −log₂0.5           = 1.0000 bits
H(B) = −(0.9·(−0.15200) + 0.1·(−3.32193))        = 0.13680 + 0.33219  = 0.4690 bits
H(C) = −(4 × 0.25·log₂0.25)                       = log₂4              = 2.0000 bits
H(D) = −(0.97·(−0.04394) + 3×0.01·(−6.64386))    = 0.04263 + 0.19932  = 0.2419 bits
```

Ranked: `D (0.242) < B (0.469) < A (1.000) < C (2.000)` — the predicted order, exactly. Notice what this proves about the trap inside it: `D` has **four** outcomes (twice as many as the fair coin `A`) and *less than a quarter* of `A`'s entropy. More outcomes is not more entropy. More *evenness* is more entropy, and `D` is almost all mass on one outcome — the number of categories is irrelevant next to how the probability is distributed across them.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Entropy is a physics idea about disorder, borrowed loosely for AI."** The word is borrowed from thermodynamics, but the AI meaning is not loose — it is Shannon's precise, provable minimum-average-code-length quantity. When you read "the model's output distribution has high entropy," the honest claim is "the model is, on average, this many bits unsure about its answer," not a vague gesture at messiness.
- **"More possible outcomes always means higher entropy."** False, and `D` above is the counterexample: 4 outcomes, entropy `0.242` bits — less than the *2-outcome* fair coin's `1.0` bit. Entropy tracks how *evenly* probability is spread, not how many bins exist. The maximum for `n` outcomes is `log₂n`, but nothing forces a distribution to be anywhere near its maximum.
- **"Entropy depends on which outcome is labeled which."** `H(p)` is a function of the *multiset of probabilities*, not of the labels attached to them. `[0.9, 0.1]` and `[0.1, 0.9]` have identical entropy — permuting which outcome carries which probability changes nothing about how surprised you are on average. If your computed entropy changes when you relabel classes, the bug is in your code, not in the concept.
- **"The log base is a minor implementation detail."** The base sets the *unit* — bits (`log₂`), nats (`ln`), or "hartleys/dits" (`log₁₀`) — the way meters versus feet set a unit for length. It does not change *which* distribution has more entropy (that ranking is base-invariant, since all logs differ by a positive constant factor), but it absolutely changes the *number*, and mixing bases inside one formula produces numbers that are wrong, not just differently scaled. This is the exact mechanism behind capstone defect 1.

### Lesson 2 — Cross-entropy: the cost of the wrong code, and why it's THE classification loss

**The teaching.** Entropy asked "how surprised am I, on average, using the *true* distribution `p`'s own probabilities to weigh the surprise?" **Cross-entropy** asks the practically important question: what if I weigh the *true* outcomes' surprise using a different distribution `q` — my model's guess?

```
H(p, q) = E_{x~p}[ −log qᵢ ]  =  − Σᵢ pᵢ · log qᵢ
```

Read it as: *data really comes from `p`, but you are scoring/encoding it as if it came from `q`.* If `q = p`, cross-entropy collapses to plain entropy — `H(p, p) = H(p)`, no penalty, you guessed the truth. Whenever `q ≠ p`, `H(p, q) > H(p)` **always** (you will prove this is not optional in Lesson 3) — guessing wrong never helps, it only costs extra nats on average. That gap is exactly what Lesson 3 names.

Here is why this specific formula became **the** loss function for classification, not merely *a* loss function. In supervised classification the true label is a one-hot distribution: `p = [0, …, 1, …, 0]`, all mass on the correct class `c`. Plug a one-hot `p` into the cross-entropy sum and every term except the correct class vanishes (`0 · log qᵢ = 0`):

```
H(p, q) = − Σᵢ pᵢ log qᵢ  =  −1 · log q_c  =  −log q_c
```

**Cross-entropy loss for a classification example is just the negative log of the probability your model assigned to the correct answer.** No averaging over wrong classes, no extra machinery — one term. That single-term collapse is *why* this loss and not some other measure of "how wrong was the prediction": it directly, only, and monotonically rewards putting more probability mass on the true class, and it does so with a gradient (below) that turns out to be the simplest one in this entire course.

**Worked example — softmax, cross-entropy, and the gradient that makes it THE loss.**

Three-class logits `z = [1, 2, 3]`, true label class index `2` (one-hot `y = [0, 0, 1]`). Softmax first — you built the shape discipline for this in M01, the exponentials in M04:

```
softmax(z)ᵢ = exp(zᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ)

exp(1) = 2.71828,  exp(2) = 7.38906,  exp(3) = 20.08554,  sum = 30.19288
q = [0.09003, 0.24473, 0.66524]        (sums to 1.00000)
```

Cross-entropy loss, using the one-hot collapse:

```
L = −ln(q₂) = −ln(0.66524) = 0.40761 nats
```

Now the gradient, and this is where M03's chain rule pays off directly. `L` is a composition — logits → softmax → negative-log-of-the-true-entry — and the chain rule says multiply local sensitivities along the path and sum across the paths where `zⱼ` influences `q` (every `qᵢ` depends on every `zⱼ`, because softmax's denominator sums over all of them). Carrying that composition through — you will do it explicitly, term by term, in Lab 2 — the local derivatives of softmax and of `−log(·)` **cancel almost entirely**, leaving:

```
∂L/∂zⱼ = qⱼ − yⱼ
```

For this example: `∂L/∂z = [0.09003, 0.24473, 0.66524] − [0, 0, 1] = [0.09003, 0.24473, −0.33476]`. Read the signs: the gradient is *positive* for the two wrong classes (raising their logits raises the loss — descent will *push them down*) and *negative* for the true class (descent will *push it up*). And notice: `Σⱼ (qⱼ − yⱼ) = Σqⱼ − Σyⱼ = 1 − 1 = 0` always, because both `q` and one-hot `y` are probability distributions summing to `1` — a free correctness check on every gradient you compute in Lab 2, no reference answer required.

That `q − y` result is the entire practical reason cross-entropy paired with softmax is the default: the composition of "squash to a distribution" and "penalize by negative-log-probability-of-truth" was chosen *because* their gradients telescope down to a subtraction, not because someone liked the shape of the curve.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Cross-entropy and entropy are the same formula with a different name."** Entropy is a property of *one* distribution; cross-entropy needs *two* — the true `p` and the guess `q`. Entropy is the special case `H(p, p)`. Confusing them is why "the loss is low" gets misread as "the data has low entropy" — the loss being low says your `q` matches `p` well, it says nothing about how inherently unpredictable `p` itself is.
- **"A lower cross-entropy loss always means a better model, full stop."** It means `q` is closer to whatever `p` you trained against. If `p` — the labels — is noisy, mislabeled, or reflects a biased sampling process, driving `H(p, q)` toward its floor `H(p)` means the model is learning to reproduce that noise faithfully. The loss has no way to tell you your labels were wrong; it only measures agreement with the labels you gave it.
- **"You compute cross-entropy by calling `softmax()` then `log()` as two separate steps, always."** That is mathematically correct and numerically dangerous — `log(softmax(z))` computes an intermediate `q` that can quietly underflow to exactly `0.0` in floating point for confident-and-wrong predictions, and `log(0.0)` is `−inf`. Lesson 4 gives you the fused, stable way (`log-sum-exp`) that never materializes a `q` that could be exactly zero.
- **"Cross-entropy is a symmetric measure of how far apart `p` and `q` are, like a distance."** `H(p, q) ≠ H(q, p)` in general — scoring the truth with the wrong code (`H(p,q)`) is not the same number as scoring the wrong distribution with the true code (`H(q,p)`). This directedness is not a footnote; it is the entire subject of Lesson 3, and it is why "distance between distributions" is a phrase to use carefully, if at all.

### Lesson 3 — KL divergence: the directed distance between distributions

**The teaching.** Cross-entropy told you the average cost, in nats, of coding `p`-data with a `q`-shaped code. Entropy told you the *unavoidable* floor of that cost — what you'd pay even with the best possible code, the one built from `p` itself. The gap between them is pure waste, caused only by `q` being wrong, and it has a name:

```
D_KL(p ‖ q) = H(p, q) − H(p) = Σᵢ pᵢ · ln(pᵢ / qᵢ)
```

Read `D_KL(p‖q)` as **the extra nats you pay, on average, for coding `p`-distributed data as if it were `q`-distributed** — exactly the "wasted bits" reading, and it is why KL divergence shows up everywhere information-theoretic waste can be measured: how far a model's belief is from the truth, how far a compressed representation drifts from the data it's compressing, how far a policy has moved from a reference policy.

Two structural facts, both provable and both graded in Lab 3:

```
D_KL(p‖q) ≥ 0 always,  and  D_KL(p‖q) = 0  if and only if  p = q
```

This is **Gibbs' inequality** (a direct consequence of `−log` being convex and Jensen's inequality) — it is not an empirical tendency, it is a theorem, and "my computed KL divergence came out negative" is *always* a bug, never a valid measurement, on genuine probability distributions. The second fact, `D_KL(p‖p) = 0`, is the cleanest possible correctness check you can run on any KL implementation: feed it the same distribution twice and it must return exactly zero, no tolerance needed beyond floating-point noise.

The fact that makes KL a **divergence** and explicitly *not* a distance: it is not symmetric.

```
D_KL(p‖q)  ≠  D_KL(q‖p)     in general
```

Both are always `≥ 0`, both are `0` only at `p = q`, but the two numbers differ, and the difference is not a rounding artifact — it reflects two different penalties. `D_KL(p‖q)` — the **forward KL**, the one hiding inside every classification loss — averages using `p`'s weighting, so it punishes hardest wherever `p` has real mass and `q` assigns little: `q` is forced to *cover* every place `p` puts probability, or pay dearly. `D_KL(q‖p)` — the **reverse KL**, the one used in variational inference — averages using `q`'s weighting instead, so it punishes hardest wherever `q` puts mass and `p` has little: `q` is instead penalized for spending probability anywhere `p` wouldn't, pushing `q` to hug just one region of `p`'s support and ignore the rest ("mode-seeking" behavior). Same two ingredients, genuinely different objectives — this is why swapping the argument order in a KL call is not a cosmetic choice.

**Worked example — forward vs. reverse KL, and the entropy-plus-KL identity.**

`p = [0.9, 0.1]` (true, confident), `q = [0.5, 0.5]` (a model that hedges to uniform). Using `ln(1.8) ≈ 0.58779` and `ln(0.2) ≈ −1.60944`:

```
D_KL(p‖q) = 0.9·ln(0.9/0.5) + 0.1·ln(0.1/0.5)
          = 0.9·ln(1.8) + 0.1·ln(0.2)
          = 0.9(0.58779) + 0.1(−1.60944)
          = 0.52901 − 0.16094 = 0.36806 nats
```

Now the reverse, using `ln(5/9) ≈ −0.58779` and `ln(5) ≈ 1.60944`:

```
D_KL(q‖p) = 0.5·ln(0.5/0.9) + 0.5·ln(0.5/0.1)
          = 0.5(−0.58779) + 0.5(1.60944)
          = −0.29389 + 0.80472 = 0.51083 nats
```

`0.36806 ≠ 0.51083` — asymmetric, as predicted, and the reverse direction costs more here because `q` (uniform) has to be measured against how surprising `p`'s confidence looks. Now check the identity from the top of this lesson using `H(p) = 0.32508` nats (Lesson 1's biased-coin entropy, converted to nats: `H(p) = −0.9 ln0.9 − 0.1 ln0.1 = 0.32508`) and `H(p,q) = −ln(0.5) = 0.69315` (since every `qᵢ = 0.5`, every term collapses to the same log):

```
H(p) + D_KL(p‖q) = 0.32508 + 0.36806 = 0.69314 ≈ H(p,q) = 0.69315   ✓ (rounding only)
```

The identity holds to the last decimal you'd expect from hand-rounding. **This is also the deepest justification for why minimizing cross-entropy loss during training is the same act as minimizing KL divergence to the true data distribution:** for a fixed dataset, `H(p)` — the entropy of the true label distribution — is a constant with respect to your model's parameters; it doesn't move no matter how you train. So `H(p, q_θ) = H(p) + D_KL(p‖q_θ)`, and minimizing the left side over `θ` is *exactly* minimizing `D_KL(p‖q_θ)` over `θ`, because the constant `H(p)` cannot be reduced any further. Every time you watch a training loss go down, you are watching `D_KL(p‖q_θ)` shrink toward its floor of zero — the cross-entropy loss is not *analogous* to KL divergence, it *is* KL divergence to the true distribution, plus an unmovable constant.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"KL divergence is a distance metric between distributions."** A true metric is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality; KL is neither in general (Lesson 3's own worked example shows the asymmetry with real numbers). Call it a *divergence* — a directed measure of mismatch — and reserve "distance" for things like the (symmetric, but rarely used in this stack) Jensen–Shannon divergence.
- **"Forward KL and reverse KL measure the same kind of mismatch, just computed in opposite order."** They optimize for structurally different behavior: forward KL (`D_KL(p‖q)`, what training against labeled data does) forces `q` to cover every place `p` has mass, at the cost of spreading `q` thin; reverse KL (`D_KL(q‖p)`, common in variational methods) lets `q` abandon regions of `p` entirely as long as wherever `q` does put mass, `p` agrees. A model that "confidently commits to one plausible answer while ignoring other valid ones" is showing reverse-KL-flavored behavior even when it was trained with a forward-KL loss — worth noticing when it happens.
- **"A correctly implemented KL divergence can still come out negative for a hard-enough example."** No — Gibbs' inequality guarantees `D_KL(p‖q) ≥ 0` for *any* valid probability distributions `p, q` with matching support, full stop, no exceptions for "the distributions are very different." A negative output is a certificate that the implementation is wrong, not that the distributions are unusual — and Lab 3 makes you check this against two hundred random distribution pairs, not just the one worked example.
- **"Cross-entropy training and KL-divergence minimization are two different techniques that happen to produce similar results."** They are the identical objective. `H(p, q_θ) = H(p) + D_KL(p‖q_θ)` with `H(p)` constant in `θ` means the two are the same optimization problem written two ways, not two problems with a coincidental resemblance.

### Lesson 4 — Numerical stability and maximum likelihood: what "the loss" is actually buying you

**The teaching.** Two threads left loose from Lessons 2 and 3, both load-bearing for anything you run at real scale.

**Thread one: why does minimizing average cross-entropy over a dataset equal maximum likelihood estimation?** For `N` labeled examples with one-hot true labels, the *average* cross-entropy loss is:

```
(1/N) Σₙ H(pₙ, qₙ) = (1/N) Σₙ [−ln q_{n, cₙ}] = −(1/N) ln ( Πₙ q_{n, cₙ} )
```

`Πₙ q_{n, cₙ}` is the **likelihood** of the whole dataset under your model — the probability the model assigns, jointly, to every true label actually observed (M04's likelihood, read backward: parameters free, data fixed). So average cross-entropy is `−(1/N) × log-likelihood`. **Minimizing average cross-entropy is, term for term, maximizing the log-likelihood of the data.** This is not an analogy the field reaches for after the fact — cross-entropy was the *right* loss to standardize on precisely because it is negative log-likelihood in disguise, and maximum likelihood has a century of statistical theory (consistency, efficiency, asymptotic normality) backing why it's a sound thing to optimize at all. When someone says "the model was trained to maximize likelihood" and someone else says "the model was trained to minimize cross-entropy," they mean the identical training run.

**Thread two: the overflow that ships to production.** Softmax computes `exp(zᵢ) / Σ exp(zⱼ)`, and real logits are not always small. Try `z = [1000, 1001, 1002]` the naive way:

```
>>> np.exp(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.]))
RuntimeWarning: overflow encountered in exp
array([inf, inf, inf])
>>> inf / inf
nan
```

Every probability comes back `nan`. The fix is one identity, called **log-sum-exp**, and it relies on softmax being *shift-invariant*: adding the same constant `k` to every logit leaves the softmax output completely unchanged, because the constant factors out of both numerator and denominator (`exp(zᵢ+k) = exp(zᵢ)·exp(k)`, and `exp(k)` cancels). So subtract the largest logit before exponentiating — the biggest term becomes `exp(0) = 1`, every other term is `exp(negative number) ≤ 1`, and nothing overflows:

```
logsumexp(z) = max(z) + ln( Σⱼ exp(zⱼ − max(z)) )
cross_entropy_from_logits(y, z) = logsumexp(z) − Σᵢ yᵢ zᵢ
```

Run it on `z = [1000, 1001, 1002]` (which is exactly `[1, 2, 3] + 999`, so by shift-invariance the *answer must match Lesson 2's worked example exactly*):

```
max(z) = 1002
shifted = [-2, -1, 0],  exp(shifted) = [0.13534, 0.36788, 1.0],  sum = 1.50321
logsumexp(z) = 1002 + ln(1.50321) = 1002 + 0.40761 = 1002.40761
loss = logsumexp(z) − z_true = 1002.40761 − 1002 = 0.40761 nats
```

Identical to `0.40761` from Lesson 2, because the two logit vectors differ only by the constant `999`, which log-sum-exp is built to cancel — and it never once computed an intermediate `exp(1000)`. This is exactly why `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` in every real framework takes **raw logits**, not a pre-softmaxed probability vector, and fuses softmax and the log internally: fusing is not a style preference, it is the only version of the computation that cannot silently produce `inf` or `nan` on realistic logit magnitudes.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Softmax outputs are always strictly positive, so `log(softmax(z))` never sees a zero."** True in exact real-number math (`exp` of anything finite is `> 0`), false in floating point: for a very confidently-wrong prediction, `exp(zᵢ − max)` for the losing classes can underflow all the way to `0.0`, and `ln(0.0) = −inf`. This is a real, reachable production bug, not a hypothetical — it's why the fused log-sum-exp path exists instead of "just be careful."
- **"Lower cross-entropy loss and higher accuracy are the same signal."** They correlate but are not the same number. A model can lower its average loss by becoming more confident on examples it already got right, with zero change in accuracy — and, more concerning, a model minimizing *average* loss can become *more* wrong, with higher confidence, on a rare tail case, because one bad tail term barely moves an average over a large `N`. Watching only the scalar loss can hide exactly the failures worth catching; this is the thread that Tier 2 below pulls hardest on.
- **"MLE and cross-entropy minimization are two frameworks that happen to agree, useful to know as a fun fact."** They are algebraically the same objective, shown term-by-term above — not a coincidence, not an approximation. # solution.py
```python
import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float)
    shifted = z - np.max(z)
    ez = np.exp(shifted)
    return ez / ez.sum()

def logsumexp(z):
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float)
    m = np.max(z)
    return float(m + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(z - m))))

def cross_entropy(y, q, eps=1e-12):
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    q = np.asarray(q, dtype=float)
    q = np.clip(q, eps, 1.0)
    return float(-np.sum(y * np.log(q)))

def cross_entropy_from_logits(y, z):
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float)
    return float(logsumexp(z) - np.sum(y * z))

def grad_cross_entropy_logits(y, z):
    return softmax(z) - np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
```

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

def kl_divergence(p, q, eps=1e-12):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    q = np.asarray(q, dtype=float)
    mask = p > 0
    q_safe = np.clip(q, eps, None)
    return float(np.sum(p[mask] * np.log(p[mask] / q_safe[mask])))

def entropy_nats(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    mask = p > 0
    return float(-np.sum(p[mask] * np.log(p[mask])))

def cross_entropy_nats(p, q, eps=1e-12):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    q = np.asarray(q, dtype=float)
    mask = p > 0
    q_safe = np.clip(q, eps, None)
    return float(-np.sum(p[mask] * np.log(q_safe[mask])))

def kl_from_entropy_identity(p, q):
    return cross_entropy_nats(p, q) - entropy_nats(p)
```

## Three-tier reading

**Tier 1 — the material ground (verifiable facts).** Entropy `H(p) = −Σ pᵢ log pᵢ` is the expected surprisal of a distribution and, by Shannon's source coding theorem, the provable minimum average number of bits (or nats) needed to encode outcomes drawn from it — not a metaphor, a bound you can watch real compressors approach. Cross-entropy `H(p,q) = −Σ pᵢ log qᵢ` is the average cost of encoding true data `p` with a code shaped for guess `q`, and for a one-hot label it collapses to `−log q_true` — exactly the loss `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` reports. KL divergence `D_KL(p‖q) = H(p,q) − H(p)` is the extra cost that mismatch causes, is provably `≥ 0` (Gibbs' inequality) with equality only at `p=q`, and is provably asymmetric — `D_KL(p‖q) ≠ D_KL(q‖p)` in general, a fact you verified with two different numbers on the same pair of distributions. Because `H(p)` is fixed by the data and does not depend on model parameters, minimizing cross-entropy loss during training is algebraically identical to minimizing KL divergence to the true data distribution, which is algebraically identical to maximizing likelihood. These are chained, provable facts, not a stack of loosely related tools — you derived each link in the chain by hand, and every one of them you verified numerically against a finite difference or a random-sampling property test, the least contestable layer of the whole AI stack.

**Tier 2 — what the mathematical dressing suppresses.** Cross-entropy is a single scalar, and that is exactly its danger: `−(1/N) Σ log q_{true}` is an *average*, and an average is a number that has already decided which errors to erase. A model can drive its training loss beautifully low while being catastrophically, confidently wrong on a thin slice of the distribution — a rare disease, a minority dialect, an edge-case fraud pattern — because that slice contributes a handful of large terms to a sum over millions, and the sum does not preserve the shape of where the badness lives. The loss curve going down is real evidence the model is fitting *something*; it is not evidence about *what* it might still be getting wrong, or for whom. Worse: cross-entropy is minimized against whatever `p` you handed it — the labels — and the formula has no mechanism to notice if `p` itself encodes a biased sampling process or an unrepresentative labeling standard. A model trained to convergence on biased labels achieves a genuinely, mathematically low cross-entropy loss; the arithmetic is flawless and says nothing about whether the labels were fair. Boosters point at "the loss converged" as evidence of learning; critics point at "it's just curve-fitting to whatever data you fed it" as evidence of nothing. Both dodge the same fact: **the loss faithfully measures agreement with the distribution you chose to call ground truth, and the choice of that distribution is not inside the mathematics — it is a decision you made before the first gradient step, and the loss cannot audit it for you.**

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your model's training log reports a cross-entropy loss of `0.31` — low, respectable, the kind of number that gets a model shipped. That single scalar is an *average* over every example in your dataset, weighted equally, and averages are indifferent to where the remaining error lives: it could be spread thin and harmless across every example, or it could be almost entirely concentrated on the 2% of cases that look least like your training distribution — the cases, often, that matter most because they are rare, unusual, or belong to people your data underrepresented. Nothing in the number `0.31` distinguishes these two worlds. An auditor asks you a question this module has given you the exact tools to answer honestly: not "what was your average loss," but "show me the cross-entropy, per example, sorted from worst to best — and tell me who is in the tail." You can build that histogram in four lines of the code you wrote this module. **The question you cannot unknow: you now know precisely how to compute the distribution of your model's failures, not just its average — so the next time you report a single loss number as if it were the whole truth, is that a limitation of the mathematics, or a choice you made about which number was comfortable to show?**

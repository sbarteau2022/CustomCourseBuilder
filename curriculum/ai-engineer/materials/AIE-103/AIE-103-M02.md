### Lesson 1 — The dot product is a similarity score; the norm is a length; cosine divides one by the other

**The teaching.** You met the dot product in M01 as the atom of matrix multiplication: `a · b = Σ aᵢbᵢ`. That definition is correct and it hides the geometry. The geometric identity is the one you carry forward:

```
a · b = |a| |b| cos θ
```

where `|a|` is the **length** (Euclidean norm) of `a` and `θ` is the angle between the two vectors. Read it in both directions. Left to right: a dot product is a length-times-length-times-alignment. Right to left: if you divide the dot product by the two lengths, what remains is `cos θ` — a pure alignment score in `[-1, 1]`, blind to how long either vector is. That quotient is **cosine similarity**, and it is the single most common similarity measure in every embedding system you will ever touch.

The **norm** is its own instrument. The L2 (Euclidean) norm `|a|₂ = √(Σ aᵢ²)` is the length you draw with a ruler. It is not the only norm: L1 `|a|₁ = Σ|aᵢ|` (taxicab distance, the thing L1 regularization shrinks) and L∞ `|a|∞ = maxᵢ|aᵢ|` (the largest single coordinate) measure different notions of size and will reappear in M06's regularizers. When someone writes `‖·‖` with no subscript, they almost always mean L2 — but "almost always" is exactly the kind of unstated assumption Tier 2 of this course is about.

**Why the raw dot product lies about similarity.** Because `a · b` scales with `|a|` and `|b|`, a long vector looks "similar" to everything simply by being long. In a document search, a 5,000-word document has a large vector; it will out-dot a perfectly on-topic 8-word document. Cosine removes the length so that only *direction* — the topic mix — is compared. This is not a detail; it is the reason retrieval systems normalize.

**Worked example — the same three documents, two rankings, by hand.**

Query `q = [1, 0, 1]` (the user cares about topic A and topic C, not B). Three document vectors (counts of A, B, C):

```
d0 = [10, 10, 0]    a long doc about A and B
d1 = [ 1,  0, 1]    a short doc about exactly A and C
d2 = [ 3,  0, 2]    a medium doc about A and C
```

Raw dot products:

```
q · d0 = 1·10 + 0·10 + 1·0  = 10
q · d1 = 1·1  + 0·0  + 1·1  =  2
q · d2 = 1·3  + 0·0  + 1·2  =  5
```

Dot-product ranking: **d0 > d2 > d1.** The off-topic long document wins.

Now the lengths: `|q| = √2 ≈ 1.414`, `|d0| = √200 ≈ 14.14`, `|d1| = √2 ≈ 1.414`, `|d2| = √13 ≈ 3.606`. Cosine:

```
cos(q,d0) = 10 / (1.414 · 14.14) = 10/20   = 0.500
cos(q,d1) =  2 / (1.414 · 1.414) =  2/2    = 1.000
cos(q,d2) =  5 / (1.414 · 3.606) =  5/5.10 = 0.981
```

Cosine ranking: **d1 > d2 > d0.** A complete reversal of the top pick. `d1` scores a perfect `1.000` because it points in exactly the query's direction — it *is* "A and C, equal parts." The only thing that changed is that we divided out length. This single example is the whole lesson; Lab 1 makes you predict both rankings before you run them.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Dot product and cosine similarity are basically the same, cosine is just normalized."** They agree on *direction* but disagree on *ranking* the moment vector lengths differ, which is always in real data. The worked example flips its own top result. "Normalized" is not cosmetic — it changes which document you return to the user.
- **"A bigger dot product means more similar."** Only among vectors of equal length. Otherwise a bigger dot product can just mean a longer vector. This is the trap Lab 1 is built to spring.
- **"The norm of a vector is `np.sum(v**2)`."** That is the *squared* norm. The norm is `np.sqrt(np.sum(v**2))` = `np.linalg.norm(v)`. Forgetting the square root is the most common cosine bug, and it silently produces plausible-looking wrong numbers rather than crashing.
- **"`cos θ` can be any value, it's a similarity."** For real vectors it is bounded to `[-1, 1]`. If your cosine code ever returns `1.7`, you divided by the wrong thing (probably the squared norm), and the out-of-range value is your free bug detector — Lab 1 asserts on the bound.

### Lesson 2 — Projection: the shadow of one vector on another, and why the residual is perpendicular

**The teaching.** *Projecting* `b` onto `a` answers: "how much of `b` points along `a`, expressed as a vector in `a`'s direction?" Geometrically it is the shadow `b` casts on the line through `a` when light falls perpendicular to that line. The formula is one dot product over another, times `a`:

```
proj_a(b) = ( (a · b) / (a · a) ) a
```

Read the pieces. `a · b` is how much `b` aligns with `a` (unnormalized). Dividing by `a · a = |a|²` rescales so the result is measured in units of `a` and is independent of how long `a` happens to be. Multiplying by `a` puts the answer back as a vector pointing along `a`. The scalar `(a · b)/(a · a)` is the **coordinate** of `b` in the `a` direction — the single number you would store if you were compressing `b` down to "its `a`-ness."

The **residual** is what projection throws away: `r = b − proj_a(b)`. The defining property — the reason projection is *the* least-squares operation and not just one of many ways to approximate — is:

```
r · a = 0     (the residual is orthogonal to a)
```

The shadow captures *all* of `b`'s `a`-component, so what remains has *none*: it is perpendicular. This is not an approximation that happens to be good; it is the exact, provable optimum. `proj_a(b)` is the unique closest point to `b` on the whole line through `a`, and "closest" and "residual is perpendicular" are the same statement.

**Scaling up to a subspace — least squares is projection.** Projecting onto a single line generalizes to projecting onto the column space of a matrix `A` (the span of several vectors at once). The formula becomes the **normal equations**:

```
Aᵀ A x = Aᵀ b       →       x = (Aᵀ A)⁻¹ Aᵀ b
```

and `A x` is the projection of `b` onto the columns of `A` — the closest point in that subspace, with residual `b − A x` orthogonal to *every* column (`Aᵀ (b − A x) = 0`). This is exactly ordinary least squares: fitting a line or plane to data is projecting the observations onto the space of things your model can express. You will meet the same identity as "the gradient of squared loss is zero at the optimum" in M03, and as "the fitted values are the projection of `y`" in M05. Same instrument, three names.

**Worked example — projection onto a line, by hand.**

`a = [2, 0]`, `b = [3, 4]`.

```
a · b = 2·3 + 0·4 = 6
a · a = 2·2 + 0·0 = 4
proj_a(b) = (6/4) · [2, 0] = 1.5 · [2, 0] = [3, 0]
residual r = b − proj = [3, 4] − [3, 0] = [0, 4]
check:  r · a = 0·2 + 4·0 = 0   ✓ perpendicular
```

`b`'s shadow on the x-axis is `[3, 0]` — its x-part, exactly — and the leftover `[0, 4]` points straight up, perpendicular to the axis. Notice the projection's scale did *not* depend on `a`'s length: `a = [2,0]` and `a = [1,0]` give the identical shadow `[3,0]`, because `a·a` in the denominator cancels the scaling. That cancellation is the coordinate story from Lesson 1 reappearing.

**Worked example — fitting a line is projecting, by hand.**

Fit `y = c₀ + c₁x` to points `(1,1), (2,2), (3,2)`. Design matrix and target:

```
A = [[1,1],      b = [1,
     [1,2],           2,
     [1,3]]           2]
```

```
AᵀA = [[3, 6],     Aᵀb = [5,
        [6,14]]           11]
```

Solve `[[3,6],[6,14]] x = [5,11]`; determinant `= 42−36 = 6`:

```
x = (1/6) [[14,−6],[−6,3]] [5,11]
  = (1/6) [14·5 − 6·11,  −6·5 + 3·11]
  = (1/6) [4, 3]
  = [0.6667, 0.5]      →  y = 2/3 + (1/2)x
```

Fitted values `A x = [1.167, 1.667, 2.167]`, residual `r = b − Ax = [−0.167, 0.333, −0.167]`. Check orthogonality to both columns: `Σ rᵢ = 0` (residual ⟂ the all-ones column) and `Σ rᵢ xᵢ = −0.167 + 0.667 − 0.5 = 0` (residual ⟂ the `x` column). The fit is the projection of the data onto the two-dimensional space of straight lines; nothing about the data that a line *could* have captured is left in the residual. Lab 2 has you predict these residual dot products are zero, then measure them landing at `1e-16`.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Projection needs `a` to be a unit vector."** No — the `(a · a)` in the denominator normalizes for you. The unit-vector form `proj = (a·b) a` is a *special case* when `|a| = 1`. Using it on a non-unit `a` silently scales your answer by `|a|²`; nothing crashes.
- **"The residual being perpendicular is a coincidence / an approximation."** It is the exact defining property and it is what makes the projection the closest point. If your computed residual is *not* orthogonal to `a` (beyond floating-point noise), your projection is wrong — orthogonality is a free correctness check, which is precisely how Lab 2 grades it.
- **"Least squares and projection are different topics."** They are the same operation. `x = (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀb` *is* the projection coefficients; `Ax` *is* the projected vector. Treating them as unrelated is why so many people can run `np.linalg.lstsq` but cannot say what it computed.
- **"`(AᵀA)⁻¹` is how you should compute it in code."** Mathematically yes, numerically no — forming and inverting `AᵀA` squares the conditioning and loses precision. Real code uses `np.linalg.lstsq` (which uses a QR/SVD path). Lab 2 makes you compute *both* and compare, so you feel the difference rather than being told.

### Lesson 3 — Eigenvectors and the SVD: finding the axes a matrix actually cares about

**The teaching.** A matrix is a transformation: `x ↦ Ax` stretches, rotates, and shears space. Most directions get rotated. A few special directions get *only stretched, not rotated* — those are the **eigenvectors**, and the stretch factor along each is its **eigenvalue** `λ`:

```
A v = λ v        (v is an eigenvector, λ its eigenvalue)
```

For a symmetric matrix these directions are perpendicular and the eigenvalues are real — the matrix is, in the right coordinate frame, just independent stretches along orthogonal axes. Example: `A = [[2,1],[1,2]]` has `A[1,1]ᵀ = [3,3]ᵀ = 3·[1,1]ᵀ`, so `[1,1]` is an eigenvector with `λ=3`, and `[1,−1]` is one with `λ=1`. The matrix stretches the `[1,1]` diagonal by 3× and the `[1,−1]` diagonal by 1×. That is the *entire* content of the matrix, re-expressed as "which axes, how much."

Eigen-decomposition only applies to square matrices. Real data matrices are rectangular (rows = examples, columns = features). The **Singular Value Decomposition** is the generalization that works for *any* `m×n` matrix:

```
A = U Σ Vᵀ
```

- `V` (n×n): orthonormal **right singular vectors** — the input axes the matrix cares about, in the row/feature space.
- `U` (m×m): orthonormal **left singular vectors** — the output axes, in the column/example space.
- `Σ` (m×n, diagonal): the **singular values** `σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0` — how much the matrix stretches along each paired axis, sorted biggest first.

Read the geometry: SVD says *any* linear map is "rotate the input (Vᵀ), scale each axis (Σ), rotate into the output (U)." The singular values are the scale factors, and their descending order is a ranking of *importance* — `σ₁` is the dominant pattern in the data, `σ₂` the next, and the tail is often noise. This ranking is the hinge of Lesson 4.

The connection to eigenvalues you can compute by hand: `AᵀA` is symmetric, its eigenvectors are the columns of `V`, and its eigenvalues are the **squared** singular values `σᵢ²`. So `σᵢ = √λᵢ(AᵀA)`.

**Worked example — SVD of a 3×2 matrix, entirely by hand.**

```
A = [[1, 1],
     [1, 0],
     [0, 1]]
```

Step 1 — form `AᵀA` (2×2, symmetric):

```
AᵀA = [[1·1+1·1+0·0,  1·1+1·0+0·1],      = [[2, 1],
        [1·1+0·1+1·0,  1·1+0·0+1·1]]         [1, 2]]
```

That is the same `[[2,1],[1,2]]` from above — eigenvalues `λ = 3, 1`. So the singular values are:

```
σ₁ = √3 ≈ 1.732,   σ₂ = √1 = 1
```

Step 2 — right singular vectors are `AᵀA`'s eigenvectors, normalized:

```
v₁ = [1, 1]/√2   (for λ=3),    v₂ = [1, −1]/√2   (for λ=1)
```

Step 3 — left singular vectors from `uᵢ = A vᵢ / σᵢ`:

```
A v₁ = A·[1,1]/√2 = [2, 1, 1]/√2   →  u₁ = [2,1,1]/√2 / √3 = [2, 1, 1]/√6
A v₂ = A·[1,−1]/√2 = [0, 1, −1]/√2 →  u₂ = [0, 1, −1]/√2 / 1 = [0, 1, −1]/√2
```

Check `|u₁|² = (4+1+1)/6 = 1` ✓ and `|u₂|² = (0+1+1)/2 = 1` ✓ — both unit length, as left singular vectors must be. You have now computed, by hand, the same decomposition `np.linalg.svd` returns (up to the sign convention, which is arbitrary per column). `‖A‖_F = √(σ₁² + σ₂²) = √(3+1) = 2` — the Frobenius norm is just the length of the vector of singular values, a fact Lesson 4 turns into an error budget.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Eigenvectors and singular vectors are the same thing."** Only for symmetric positive-semidefinite matrices do they coincide. In general, eigen-decomposition needs a square matrix and can produce complex or non-orthogonal eigenvectors; SVD works for any shape and always produces real, non-negative singular values and orthonormal `U`, `V`. Data matrices are rectangular — you need SVD.
- **"Singular values can be negative."** Never — they are lengths (`σ = √λ ≥ 0`). Eigenvalues can be negative; singular values cannot. If you see a negative `σ`, you conflated the two.
- **"`np.linalg.svd` returns `V`."** It returns `Vt` = `Vᵀ`. Reconstructing with `U @ np.diag(s) @ V` instead of `U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt` is a silent, extremely common bug that produces a wrong-but-plausible matrix. Lab 3 plants exactly this.
- **"The singular values' order is arbitrary."** NumPy guarantees descending order, and Lesson 4's entire low-rank story depends on it — "keep the top `k`" only means anything because they are sorted by importance.

### Lesson 4 — Low-rank approximation and why an embedding is a vector in a learned space

**The teaching.** The SVD writes `A` as a sum of rank-1 pieces, each weighted by a singular value:

```
A = σ₁ u₁ v₁ᵀ + σ₂ u₂ v₂ᵀ + … + σᵣ uᵣ vᵣᵀ
```

Because `σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ …`, the first terms carry the most of the matrix and the last terms carry the least. **Truncating** the sum after `k` terms gives the best rank-`k` approximation `A_k`. "Best" is not hand-waving — it is the **Eckart–Young theorem**: among *all* matrices of rank ≤ `k`, `A_k` minimizes the Frobenius error, and that minimum error is exactly the tail of dropped singular values:

```
‖A − A_k‖_F = √( σ²ₖ₊₁ + σ²ₖ₊₂ + … + σ²ᵣ )
```

This is an *exact error budget you can predict before you compute the approximation.* Drop the smallest singular value from the 3×2 example (`σ₂ = 1`) and you know, in advance, the rank-1 approximation will be off by exactly `1.0` in Frobenius norm, out of a total `‖A‖_F = 2.0` — you keep `σ₁² / (σ₁²+σ₂²) = 3/4 = 75%` of the matrix's "energy" while halving its rank. Lab 3 has you predict this number and watch it land.

**From compression to embeddings.** Now the payoff the whole module was aimed at. Suppose `M` is a matrix whose rows are *things* (words, users, products) and whose columns are *contexts* (which documents, which movies, which co-occurring words). `M` is huge and mostly noise. Take its SVD and keep `k` singular values. The rows of `U_k Σ_k` (equivalently, each thing's coordinates along the top-`k` singular directions) are `k`-dimensional vectors — one per thing — that place similar things near each other, because the top singular directions *are* the dominant patterns of co-occurrence. **Those vectors are an embedding.** The space is "learned" in the honest, literal sense that the axes came from the data's own SVD, not from a human naming features.

This is Latent Semantic Analysis, and it is the direct ancestor of everything AIE-201 and AIE-301 will call "embeddings." Modern neural embeddings (word2vec, transformer encoders) learn the space with a fancier, nonlinear procedure and far more data — but the object they produce is the same object you build in Lab 3: **a vector per item, in a space where cosine similarity means "similar," recovered by factoring a co-occurrence matrix.** When AIE-301 asks you to debug why a retrieval system returns garbage, "the vectors aren't normalized so it's ranking by length" (Lesson 1) and "the low-rank truncation threw away the signal, not the noise" (this lesson) will be the first two hypotheses you reach for, and you will know how to check both.

**Worked example — rank-1 approximation and its exact error, by hand.**

Continue the 3×2 `A` with `σ₁=√3, σ₂=1`, `u₁=[2,1,1]/√6`, `v₁=[1,1]/√2`. The rank-1 approximation:

```
A₁ = σ₁ u₁ v₁ᵀ = √3 · ([2,1,1]/√6) · ([1,1]/√2)ᵀ
```

`σ₁ u₁ = √3·[2,1,1]/√6 = [2,1,1]/√2`, and the outer product with `v₁=[1,1]/√2`:

```
A₁ = (1/2) [[2,2],      = [[1.0, 1.0],
            [1,1],          [0.5, 0.5],
            [1,1]]          [0.5, 0.5]]
```

Error `A − A₁ = [[0,0],[0.5,−0.5],[−0.5,0.5]]`, whose Frobenius norm is `√(4·0.25) = √1 = 1.0` — *exactly* `σ₂`, as Eckart–Young promised. You predicted the error to three-figure precision from the singular values alone, before forming the approximation. That predict-then-measure gap closing to `1e-15` is the deliverable of Lab 3.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Low-rank approximation loses a bit of everything uniformly."** No — it discards the *smallest* singular directions first, which in structured data are usually noise. That selectivity ("keep signal, drop noise") is why it works as denoising and compression, and it is a claim you should always check rather than assume: if your `k` is too small you throw away signal, and the Frobenius budget tells you exactly how much.
- **"An embedding is a mysterious neural-network thing."** The simplest useful embedding is `U_k Σ_k` from an SVD of a co-occurrence matrix — arithmetic you can do in four lines of NumPy. Neural embeddings are more powerful, not more magical. Not knowing the SVD version is why "embedding" feels like a black box.
- **"More dimensions in the embedding is always better."** Past the point where singular values become noise, extra dimensions add noise, not signal — and cost memory and compute. The singular-value spectrum (the "elbow") tells you where signal ends. Choosing `k` is reading that spectrum, not maximizing it.
- **"Reconstructing `A_k` needs the full `U`, `s`, `Vt`."** It needs only the top `k` columns/rows: `U[:, :k] @ diag(s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]`. Slicing wrong (e.g. `Vt[:, :k]` instead of `Vt[:k, :]`) produces a shape error or a silently wrong matrix — a shape-discipline check straight from M01.

**Solution.**

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

def l2_norm(v):
    v = np.asarray(v, dtype=float)
    return np.sqrt(np.sum(v * v))

def cosine_sim(a, b):
    a = np.asarray(a, dtype=float); b = np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    na, nb = l2_norm(a), l2_norm(b)
    if na == 0.0 or nb == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return float(np.dot(a, b) / (na * nb))

def rank_by_dot(query, docs):
    scores = docs @ query
    return list(np.argsort(scores)[::-1])

def rank_by_cosine(query, docs):
    scores = np.array([cosine_sim(d, query) for d in docs])
    return list(np.argsort(scores)[::-1])
```

# solution.py
import numpy as np

def project_onto(b, a):
    a = np.asarray(a, dtype=float); b = np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    return (np.dot(a, b) / np.dot(a, a)) * a

def residual(b, a):
    b = np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    return b - project_onto(b, a)

def least_squares_normal(A, b):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float); b = np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    # Solve (AᵀA) x = Aᵀb without forming an inverse.
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

WORDS = ["cat", "dog", "car", "truck"]
M = np.array([[5., 4., 0., 0.],
              [5., 5., 1., 0.],
              [0., 0., 5., 4.],
              [0., 1., 4., 5.]])

def low_rank_approx(A, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    return U[:, :k] @ np.diag(s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

def frobenius_error(A, Ak):
    return float(np.linalg.norm(A - Ak))

def predicted_error(A, k):
    s = np.linalg.svd(A, compute_uv=False)
    return float(np.sqrt(np.sum(s[k:] ** 2)))

def embed(A, k=2):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    return U[:, :k] * s[:k]     # broadcasts columns by singular values

def cosine_sim(a, b):
    na, nb = np.linalg.norm(a), np.linalg.norm(b)
    if na == 0 or nb == 0:
        return 0.0
    return float(np.dot(a, b) / (na * nb))

def nearest(word, A, k=2):
    E = embed(A, k)
    i = WORDS.index(word)
    best_j, best = None, -np.inf
    for j in range(len(WORDS)):
        if j == i:
            continue
        c = cosine_sim(E[i], E[j])
        if c > best:
            best, best_j = c, j
    return WORDS[best_j]
```

## Three-tier reading

**Tier 1 — the material ground (verifiable facts).** Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by both norms, bounded in `[−1, 1]`; it and raw dot product agree on direction but can rank differently the instant vector lengths differ. Projection has a closed form with a provably orthogonal residual, and least squares *is* projection onto a column space. The SVD factors any matrix into orthonormal rotations and non-negative singular values, and Eckart–Young says the truncated SVD is the *provably optimal* low-rank approximation with error equal to the tail of dropped singular values. An embedding, in its simplest honest form, is the top-`k` left-singular coordinates of a co-occurrence matrix. None of this is contestable; you derived all of it by hand this module. These are among the least arguable facts in the entire AI stack.

**Tier 2 — what the mathematical dressing suppresses.** Every one of those clean results sits on top of choices that the notation makes invisible. *Cosine similarity assumes direction is what matters and magnitude is noise* — true for topic mix, false when magnitude carries signal (frequency, confidence, recency), and the formula never tells you which regime you are in. *An embedding is only as meaningful as the co-occurrence matrix it factors* — feed it a corpus that encodes society's biases and the SVD will faithfully, provably-optimally, reproduce those biases as geometry, and the word "learned" will lend them the authority of mathematics. *Eckart–Young is optimal in Frobenius norm* — which weights all entries equally; if the entries you most care about are the rare ones, the "provably best" approximation is provably best at the wrong thing. The equation does not lie. It answers *exactly* the question you encoded, which is rarely exactly the question you meant. Boosters say "the embedding learned the semantics"; critics say "it just did a matrix factorization" — both are dodging that the factorization is *load-bearing and value-laden*, and the math cannot referee that.

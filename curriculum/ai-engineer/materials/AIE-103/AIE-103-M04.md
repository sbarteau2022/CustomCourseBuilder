## Lessons

### Lesson 1 — A distribution is a weighted list; expectation is its weighted average

**The teaching.** A (discrete) random variable `X` is nothing more mysterious than a **list of outcomes paired with weights that sum to 1**: outcomes `x₁, x₂, …` with probabilities `p₁, p₂, …`, where every `pᵢ ≥ 0` and `Σ pᵢ = 1`. That is the whole object. The two constraints — non-negative, sums to one — are the *only* thing that makes a list of numbers a distribution; a "probability" of `1.3` or a set of weights summing to `0.9` is not a distribution, it is a bug, and it is the first thing to check when a probabilistic pipeline returns nonsense.

The **expectation** (expected value, mean) is the weighted average of the outcomes:

```
E[X] = Σ xᵢ pᵢ
```

Read it as a dot product: `E[X] = x · p`, the outcomes dotted with their weights. It is the "center of mass" of the distribution — the balance point if you placed weight `pᵢ` at position `xᵢ` on a ruler. Crucially, **the expectation need not be an attainable outcome.** A fair die has `E[X] = 3.5`; you will never roll a 3.5. The expectation is a summary of the whole distribution, not a prediction of any single draw — confusing those two is the source of more bad reasoning about "averages" than any other single error.

Expectation is **linear**, and this is the property you will use constantly:

```
E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b            E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]   (always, even if X, Y are dependent)
```

The second identity holding *without any independence assumption* is a small miracle that makes expectation the friendliest object in probability — you can push it through sums freely. Variance (Lesson 2) will not be so generous, and knowing exactly where the generosity stops is the lesson.

The bridge to computation — and the theme of the entire module — is the **Law of Large Numbers**: if you draw `x⁽¹⁾, x⁽²⁾, …, x⁽ⁿ⁾` independently from `X`, the sample mean converges to the expectation,

```
x̄ₙ = (1/n) Σ x⁽ⁱ⁾  →  E[X]   as n → ∞.
```

This is the license to *check every analytic claim by simulation*. `E[X]` is a formula; `x̄ₙ` is a measurement; the LLN says they meet. But "converge" hides a rate, and the rate is the whole story of Lesson 2 — the sample mean at `n=100` is still visibly wrong, and *how wrong* is predictable.

**Worked example — a bet with positive expectation, by hand.** A lottery ticket: with probability `0.1` you win `+10`; with probability `0.9` you lose `−1`.

```
outcomes x = [ 10,  −1 ]
weights  p = [0.1, 0.9]     (≥0, sums to 1 ✓)

E[X] = 10·0.1 + (−1)·0.9 = 1.0 − 0.9 = 0.1
```

The expected profit is `+0.1` per ticket — a *favorable* bet. Now read what the expectation does **not** tell you: `0.1` is not an outcome. Every single ticket returns either `+10` or `−1`; the `+0.1` only appears as the balance point of thousands of tickets. A player who buys ten tickets will most likely be *down* (nine losses, maybe one win), and only across a large `n` does the favorable expectation assert itself. That gap between "positive expectation" and "probably losing right now" is the seam Lesson 2 pries open with variance. Lab 1 makes you predict `E[X]=0.1`, then sample `100` tickets and watch the mean land somewhere like `0.4` or `−0.2` — *not* `0.1` — and decide whether that is a bug (it is not) or noise (it is).

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"The expectation is the most likely outcome."** No — it is the weighted average, which is often an *impossible* outcome (die: `3.5`) and often *not even the mode*. In the bet, the most likely outcome is `−1` (probability `0.9`), while the expectation is `+0.1`. "Expected value" is a translation trap: in English "expected" means "anticipated," but the math means "long-run average." Lab 1 asserts on a distribution where the mean and the mode have opposite signs.
- **"Probabilities can be any non-negative weights."** They must also *sum to 1*. Un-normalized weights are the single most common distribution bug — a classifier that returns `[0.6, 0.6]` has not returned probabilities. Checking `np.isclose(p.sum(), 1.0)` is a free correctness gate, and Lab 1 asserts it.
- **"`E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y]` needs `X` and `Y` independent."** It does not — linearity of expectation is unconditional. (It is *variance* that needs independence for its sum rule.) Believing expectation needs independence makes people afraid to use its most powerful property.
- **"If the expectation is positive, I'll come out ahead."** Only in the long run, and "long" is set by the variance. A positive-expectation bet with huge variance can bankrupt you before the LLN arrives. This is the exact gap — mean says go, variance says maybe you're broke first — that Lesson 2 and Tier 3 are built on.

### Lesson 2 — Variance is expected squared distance; the sample mean has variance σ²/n

**The teaching.** Expectation tells you the center; **variance** tells you the spread. It is the expected squared distance from the mean:

```
Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]     where μ = E[X]
```

Squared distance, not distance — so that deviations above and below do not cancel, and so the algebra stays clean. Expanding the square gives the identity you will actually compute with:

```
Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²        ("mean of the square minus square of the mean")
```

The **standard deviation** `σ = √Var(X)` puts the spread back in the same units as `X` (dollars, not dollars-squared), which is why you report `σ`, not variance, to a human.

Variance's sum rule is where the friendliness ends. For **independent** `X, Y`:

```
Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)      (independence required — unlike expectation)
Var(aX)    = a² Var(X)            (constants come out squared)
```

Now assemble the single most important consequence in applied probability. Take `n` independent draws from `X` (mean `μ`, variance `σ²`) and average them: `x̄ₙ = (1/n) Σ x⁽ⁱ⁾`. Using the two rules above — the sum of `n` independent variances is `nσ²`, and the `1/n` factor comes out as `(1/n)²`:

```
Var(x̄ₙ) = (1/n)² · (n σ²) = σ² / n         ⟹     SE(x̄ₙ) = σ / √n
```

The **standard error** of the mean — the standard deviation of the sample mean itself — shrinks like `1/√n`, *not* `1/n`. This is the rate the LLN hid in Lesson 1. It is why:

- To halve your error you must **quadruple** your data. `n → 4n` turns `σ/√n` into `σ/√(4n) = (σ/√n)/2`.
- A benchmark reported on `100` examples has a standard error of `σ/10` — often large enough to make a "2% improvement" indistinguishable from noise.
- The bet from Lesson 1, with `σ = √10.89 ≈ 3.30`, has `SE = 3.30/√100 = 0.33` at `n=100`. So the sample mean of `100` tickets, whose *true* mean is `0.1`, routinely lands anywhere in `0.1 ± 0.33` — a range that includes negative numbers. **The favorable bet looks like a losing one at `n=100`, and the standard error tells you exactly how long you must play before the truth shows.**

The `√n` law is the quantitative core of this module. Every "predict then measure" gap you will see is either sampling noise of size `≈ σ/√n` (shrinks predictably when you add data) or a bug (does not). Distinguishing them *is* the skill.

**Worked example — variance of the bet, and the standard error of 100 plays, by hand.** Same bet: `x = [10, −1]`, `p = [0.1, 0.9]`, `μ = 0.1`.

```
E[X²] = 10²·0.1 + (−1)²·0.9 = 100·0.1 + 1·0.9 = 10 + 0.9 = 10.9
Var(X) = E[X²] − μ² = 10.9 − (0.1)² = 10.9 − 0.01 = 10.89
σ = √10.89 = 3.30
```

The standard deviation `3.30` is **thirty-three times** the mean `0.1`. That ratio — enormous spread around a tiny positive center — is the whole danger of the bet in one number. Now the mean of `n=100` plays:

```
Var(x̄₁₀₀) = σ²/n = 10.89 / 100 = 0.1089
SE(x̄₁₀₀) = √0.1089 = 0.33         (equivalently σ/√n = 3.30/10 = 0.33)
```

So `x̄₁₀₀ ≈ 0.1 ± 0.33`. To pin the mean to `±0.033` (one decimal of confidence) you would need `SE = 0.033`, i.e. `σ/√n = 0.033`, i.e. `√n = 100`, i.e. **`n = 10,000` plays.** You predicted, from `σ` alone, that a hundred tickets is nowhere near enough to see the edge and ten thousand is. Lab 1 samples exactly these `n` and measures the shrinkage: quadruple `n`, halve the spread.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"Variance is `E[X²] − E[X]`."** It is `E[X²] − (E[X])²` — the mean is *squared* before subtracting. Dropping the square is the most common variance bug and, like the missing `sqrt` in M02's norm, it does not crash — it silently returns a plausible wrong number. In the bet it gives `10.9 − 0.1 = 10.8` instead of `10.89`; close enough to pass a careless eye, wrong enough to corrupt every downstream standard error.
- **"The standard error shrinks like `1/n`."** It shrinks like `1/√n`. This is the single most consequential rate error in the module: believe `1/n` and you will think `100` samples buys you `10×` less error than it does, and you will call noise "significant." The `√n`, not `n`, is why data is expensive.
- **"Variance adds for any two variables."** Only for *independent* ones. `Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X,Y)`, and the covariance term is zero exactly when they are uncorrelated. Assuming independence you do not have is how variance gets *underestimated* — and an underestimated variance is an *overstated* confidence, the most dangerous direction to be wrong in.
- **"Population variance (`/n`) and sample variance (`/(n−1)`) are interchangeable."** They differ by the `ddof` argument in `np.var`, and the difference matters at small `n`: NumPy's default `np.var(x)` uses `ddof=0` (divide by `n`, the MLE), while `ddof=1` divides by `n−1` (Bessel's correction, unbiased). Lesson 4 shows *why* the MLE uses `/n`; using the wrong one at `n=8` shifts your variance by 14%. Lab 3 makes you predict both.

### Lesson 3 — Conditional probability and Bayes: restricting to a sub-population, then re-normalizing

**The teaching.** A **conditional probability** `P(A | B)` — "the probability of `A` given `B`" — is a two-step operation with no mystery in it: **restrict to the world where `B` happened, then ask what fraction of *that* world also has `A`.**

```
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
```

The denominator `P(B)` is the re-normalization: having thrown away every outcome where `B` is false, the surviving probabilities no longer sum to 1, so you divide by their total `P(B)` to make them a proper distribution again. That is *all* a conditional is — a restriction followed by a re-normalization. The famous asymmetry falls straight out: `P(A|B)` and `P(B|A)` divide the same joint `P(A and B)` by *different* denominators, so they are almost never equal. **`P(positive test | disease)` and `P(disease | positive test)` are different numbers**, and mistaking one for the other is not a subtle error — it is the error that runs medicine, law, and machine-learning classifiers off the road.

Rearranging the definition (write it once with `A,B` and once with `B,A`, both equal to `P(A and B)`) gives **Bayes' rule**:

```
              P(B | A) · P(A)
P(A | B) = ─────────────────────
                  P(B)
```

and the denominator, expanded by the **law of total probability** over `A` and its complement `¬A`, is:

```
P(B) = P(B | A) · P(A) + P(B | ¬A) · P(¬A)
```

Name the pieces, because you will meet them again as *prior*, *likelihood*, *posterior*, *evidence* every time a paper says "Bayesian":

- `P(A)` — the **prior**: what you believed about `A` before seeing the evidence. Also the **base rate**.
- `P(B | A)` — the **likelihood**: how probable the evidence `B` is *if* `A` is true.
- `P(A | B)` — the **posterior**: your updated belief about `A` after seeing `B`.
- `P(B)` — the **evidence** / marginal: how probable `B` is overall, the normalizer.

The load-bearing insight — the one the whole lesson exists to install — is that **the posterior depends on the base rate `P(A)`, and ignoring the base rate is the most common quantitative error educated people make.** A test can be 99% accurate and a positive result still mean you *probably do not have the disease*, if the disease is rare enough. The math is not counterintuitive; it is arithmetic. It only *feels* counterintuitive because people substitute the likelihood `P(B|A)` (the 99%) for the posterior `P(A|B)` (the thing they actually want).

**Worked example — the 99%-accurate test that means 17%, by hand.** A disease with **base rate 1%** (`P(D) = 0.01`). A test with **sensitivity 99%** (`P(+ | D) = 0.99`, catches the sick) and **specificity 95%** (`P(− | ¬D) = 0.95`, so the false-positive rate `P(+ | ¬D) = 0.05`). You test positive. What is `P(D | +)`?

Do it with a concrete population of `10,000` first — Bayes is easier to *see* than to symbol-push:

```
10,000 people, 1% sick:
   100 have the disease        →  99 test positive   (99% of 100)
 9,900 are healthy             → 495 test positive   (5% of 9,900, the false positives)
                                 ───
 Total positives = 99 + 495   = 594
 Of those, truly sick         =  99
 P(D | +) = 99 / 594 = 0.1667
```

Now the same thing as Bayes' rule, symbol for symbol:

```
              P(+|D) P(D)                 0.99 · 0.01              0.0099
P(D|+) = ───────────────────────── = ───────────────────────── = ──────── = 0.1667  =  1/6
          P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|¬D)P(¬D)   0.99·0.01 + 0.05·0.99       0.0594
```

**Sixteen point seven percent.** The test is "99% accurate," you tested positive, and the probability you are actually sick is about **one in six.** The false positives — `495` healthy people flagged — swamp the `99` true positives, because there were `99×` more healthy people to begin with. That flood is the base rate `P(¬D) = 0.99` doing its work in the denominator. A doctor who reads "99% sensitive" as "99% chance you're sick" is off by a factor of six, in the frightening direction. Lab 2 has you predict `1/6` by hand, then *simulate a million people* — draw disease status, draw test results, count the fraction of positives who are truly sick — and watch the empirical number land at `≈0.166`, corroborating the arithmetic. The simulation is not the proof; the arithmetic is. The simulation is what makes you *believe* the arithmetic.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"A 99% accurate test that says positive means 99% chance I'm sick."** This is `P(+|D)` masquerading as `P(D|+)` — the confusion of likelihood for posterior, and the entire reason this lesson exists. The true posterior also depends on the base rate; for a rare disease it can be an order of magnitude smaller. This is the **base-rate fallacy**, and Lab 2's whole point is to make you feel it in a simulated population.
- **"`P(A|B) = P(B|A)`."** Almost never. They share a numerator but divide by different denominators. "Most terrorists are young men" does not make "most young men are terrorists"; "most sick people test positive" does not make "most positive people are sick." The asymmetry *is* the base rate.
- **"The prior is subjective, so Bayes is just opinion."** The prior in the disease example is the *measured* base rate `1%` — an empirical fact, not a feeling. Priors range from hard frequencies to genuine judgment calls, and conflating the two lets critics dismiss all Bayesian reasoning and lets boosters smuggle in unjustified priors. Which kind of prior you have is a Tier-2 question with teeth.
- **"If I don't know the base rate, I can leave it out."** Then you cannot compute a posterior at all — you have only the likelihood, which is not the answer to "given the evidence, what should I believe?" Silently dropping the prior is equivalent to *assuming a 50% base rate*, which is almost never true and is the hidden assumption inside every "the test says positive so you're sick" claim. Lab 2 and the capstone both plant exactly this drop.

### Lesson 4 — Likelihood and MLE: the distribution formula read backwards

**The teaching.** Every distribution formula has two readings, and the switch between them is the birth of statistics. Take the Bernoulli — a coin with heads-probability `p`, observed outcome `x ∈ {0,1}`:

```
P(x ; p) = p^x (1−p)^(1−x)
```

**Forward reading (probability):** fix the parameter `p`, let the outcome `x` vary — "given this coin, how probable is heads?" This is Lessons 1–3. **Backward reading (likelihood):** fix the *observed data* `x`, let the *parameter* `p` vary — "given that I saw these outcomes, how well does each candidate `p` explain them?" Same formula, opposite variable held fixed. The likelihood `L(p)` is **not a probability distribution over `p`** — it does not integrate to 1 in `p`, and calling it one is a real error — it is a *score* you assign to each candidate parameter.

For `n` independent draws, the data's joint probability is the **product** of the per-draw probabilities, and that product, read as a function of the parameter, is the **likelihood**:

```
L(p) = ∏ᵢ P(xᵢ ; p) = p^k (1−p)^(n−k)          where k = Σ xᵢ (number of heads)
```

Products of many small probabilities underflow to zero on a computer and are miserable to differentiate, so you *always* work with the **log-likelihood** — the log turns the product into a sum, and `log` is monotone so it does not move the location of the maximum:

```
ℓ(p) = log L(p) = k log p + (n − k) log(1 − p)
```

**Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)** is then exactly what it says: the parameter that maximizes the (log-)likelihood — the value of `p` under which your observed data is least surprising.

```
p̂ = argmax_p  ℓ(p)
```

Find it the calculus way — set the derivative to zero:

```
dℓ/dp = k/p − (n−k)/(1−p) = 0
  ⟹  k(1−p) = (n−k)p  ⟹  k − kp = np − kp  ⟹  k = np  ⟹  p̂ = k/n
```

The maximum-likelihood estimate of a coin's bias is **the fraction of heads you observed.** Not a coincidence, not a definition you were handed — it *falls out* of maximizing the likelihood. This is the template for all of estimation: write the likelihood, take the log, maximize. The same procedure on a Gaussian gives `μ̂ = ` the sample mean and `σ̂² = ` the mean squared deviation with **`/n`, not `/(n−1)`** — the MLE variance is the biased one, and now you know *why* NumPy's `np.var` defaults to `ddof=0`: it is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Why this is the hinge of everything downstream: **training a model is maximizing a likelihood.** "Cross-entropy loss" is negative log-likelihood of a categorical distribution. "Mean squared error" is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian with fixed variance. When AIE-201 writes `loss = -log p(data | θ)` and runs gradient descent to *minimize* it, that is `argmin` of the negative log-likelihood, which is `argmax` of the log-likelihood — this lesson, with a minus sign and a bigger `θ`. You will have computed the two-parameter version by hand before you ever see it wearing a neural network.

**Worked example — MLE of a coin, by hand and by grid.** You flip a coin `10` times and see `H H T H T H H T H H` — `k = 7` heads in `n = 10`.

```
ℓ(p) = 7 log p + 3 log(1−p)

Analytic MLE:  p̂ = k/n = 7/10 = 0.7

Corroborate by grid (no calculus): evaluate ℓ(p) at p = 0.60, 0.70, 0.80
  ℓ(0.60) = 7·log(0.60) + 3·log(0.40) = 7·(−0.5108) + 3·(−0.9163) = −3.576 − 2.749 = −6.325
  ℓ(0.70) = 7·log(0.70) + 3·log(0.30) = 7·(−0.3567) + 3·(−1.2040) = −2.497 − 3.612 = −6.109
  ℓ(0.80) = 7·log(0.80) + 3·log(0.20) = 7·(−0.2231) + 3·(−1.6094) = −1.562 − 4.828 = −6.390
```

The log-likelihood is highest at `p = 0.70` (`−6.109`), lower on both sides — the peak is at the sample fraction, exactly as the calculus promised. Notice the likelihood does **not** integrate to 1 over `p`; these are scores, and `−6.109` being "bigger" (closer to zero) than `−6.325` is the only thing that matters. Lab 3 has you predict `p̂ = 0.7`, then confirm it two independent ways — the closed form `k/n` and a fine grid search over `ℓ(p)` — and watch both name the same `0.7`. When the analytic optimum and the numerical `argmax` agree, you have *proven the derivative was right* without trusting either alone.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"The likelihood is the probability that `p` is correct."** It is not a probability distribution over `p` at all — it does not sum or integrate to 1 in `p`, and `L(0.7) = 0.7⁷·0.3³ ≈ 0.00222` is not "a 0.2% chance the coin is fair." It is a *relative score*. Turning a likelihood into a probability-over-parameters requires a prior and Bayes' rule (Lesson 3) — that is the *posterior*, a different object. Conflating likelihood with posterior is the Lesson-3 base-rate error wearing Lesson-4 clothes.
- **"Multiply the probabilities — that's the likelihood."** Correct in math, catastrophic in code: the product of a thousand numbers each `<1` underflows to `0.0`, and `log(0.0) = −inf` poisons everything downstream. You *always* sum log-probabilities instead. A pipeline that multiplies raw likelihoods works on toy data and silently returns `0` (or the *wrong* argmax) on real data. Lab 3 and the capstone both exploit this.
- **"Maximizing likelihood and maximizing log-likelihood can give different answers."** They cannot — `log` is strictly increasing, so it preserves the location of the maximum, only the height changes. This is *why* you are free to use the numerically sane log version. Doubting it means re-deriving the MLE the hard way every time.
- **"The MLE variance divides by `n−1`."** The *maximum-likelihood* variance divides by `n` (`np.var(x)`, `ddof=0`); the *unbiased* estimator divides by `n−1` (`ddof=1`). They answer different questions — "most likely value" vs. "unbiased estimate" — and at `n=8` they differ by a seventh. Which one is "correct" depends on which question you asked, and the software's default (`/n`) is the MLE, not the textbook's usual `/(n−1)`. Lab 3 makes the gap concrete.

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

def expectation(values, probs):
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    probs = np.asarray(probs, dtype=float)
    return float(np.sum(values * probs))

def variance(values, probs):
    mu = expectation(values, probs)
    ex2 = expectation(np.asarray(values, dtype=float) ** 2, probs)
    return float(ex2 - mu ** 2)          # mean of the square minus SQUARE of the mean

def standard_error(values, probs, n):
    return float(np.sqrt(variance(values, probs) / n))

def sample_mean(values, probs, n, rng):
    draws = rng.choice(values, size=n, p=probs)
    return float(np.mean(draws))
```

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

def posterior_disease_given_positive(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    p_pos_given_d = sensitivity
    p_pos_given_nd = 1.0 - specificity
    num = p_pos_given_d * prevalence
    evidence = p_pos_given_d * prevalence + p_pos_given_nd * (1.0 - prevalence)
    return float(num / evidence)

def simulate_posterior(n, prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, rng):
    disease = rng.random(n) < prevalence
    positive = np.empty(n, dtype=bool)
    # sick people test positive with prob = sensitivity
    positive[disease] = rng.random(int(disease.sum())) < sensitivity
    # healthy people test positive with prob = 1 - specificity (false positive)
    positive[~disease] = rng.random(int((~disease).sum())) < (1.0 - specificity)
    if positive.sum() == 0:
        return 0.0
    return float(disease[positive].mean())
```

**Solution.**

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np

def bernoulli_mle(data):
    return float(np.mean(np.asarray(data, dtype=float)))

def bernoulli_log_likelihood(p, data):
    data = np.asarray(data, dtype=float)
    k = data.sum()
    n = data.size
    return float(k * np.log(p) + (n - k) * np.log(1.0 - p))

def grid_mle(data, grid):
    lls = np.array([bernoulli_log_likelihood(p, data) for p in grid])
    return float(grid[np.argmax(lls)])          # argMAX: most likely, not least

def gaussian_mle(data):
    data = np.asarray(data, dtype=float)
    mu = float(np.mean(data))
    var = float(np.mean((data - mu) ** 2))       # ddof=0, the MLE variance
    return mu, var
```

    return np.std(samples, ddof=1) / len(samples)

    rate = np.mean(samples)

def posterior_sick_given_positive(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity):
    # DEFECT 2
    return sensitivity

def best_rate(samples, grid):
    """MLE of the positive rate over a grid, by log-likelihood."""
    k = np.sum(samples)
    n = len(samples)
    lls = np.array([k * np.log(p) + (n - k) * np.log(1 - p) for p in grid])
    # DEFECT 3

## Elle pacing notes

**The walls, named.**

1. **The `1/√n` wall (Lesson 2 / Lab 1 / capstone defect 1).** The deepest conceptual error in the module is expecting the standard error to shrink like `1/n`. It feels right — "twice the data, half the error" — and it is wrong; twice the data is `1/√2 ≈ 0.71×` the error, and you need *four times* the data to halve it. **The tell:** a learner is surprised that `n=100` gives a wildly wobbling sample mean, or predicts that `n=200` will "basically nail it." **The reroute:** do not lecture — have them sample at `n=100, 400, 1600` and *watch* the spread halve only when `n` quadruples. The measurement teaches the exponent faster than the derivation. This is *productive* struggle; let them hit it.

2. **The mean-vs-outcome wall (Lesson 1 / Lab 1).** Learners resist that a favorable bet (`E[X]=+0.1`) loses on most individual plays and on most short runs. Their intuition equates "expected" with "what I'll get." **The reroute:** don't argue in the abstract; make them commit `E[X]` and the *most likely outcome* on paper *first* — opposite signs, in their own handwriting — then sample `100` plays and see a negative mean. If they call that negative mean a *bug*, that is the *blocked* signal: they have not separated "the distribution's center" from "a draw," and no code-running fixes a fused mental model. Send them back to "the expectation is a balance point, not a prediction."

3. **The base-rate wall (Lesson 3 / Lab 2 / capstone defect 2).** Silent and seductive: everyone, including doctors, reads "99% accurate, tested positive" as "99% sick." **The tell:** a learner predicts `P(D|+) ≈ 0.99` and is genuinely shocked by `1/6`. **This is the highest-value shock in the module** — do not soften it. The reroute is the `10,000`-person table, *not* the formula: `99` true positives drowning in `495` false positives is a picture, and the picture is what dislodges the fallacy. **The accelerate condition:** a learner who, unprompted, asks "but how rare is the disease?" *before* computing anything has internalized that the base rate is load-bearing — hand them the capstone's defect 2 immediately and skip the scaffolding.

## Three-tier reading

**Tier 1 — the material ground (verifiable facts).** A distribution is a list of outcomes with non-negative weights that sum to 1. Expectation is their weighted average, `Σxᵢpᵢ`, and is linear unconditionally — `E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]` even for dependent variables. Variance is `E[(X−μ)²] = E[X²]−μ²`; it adds only for independent variables, and the mean of `n` independent draws has variance `σ²/n`, so its standard error shrinks like `σ/√n` — quadruple the data to halve the error. Conditional probability is restriction-then-renormalization, `P(A|B)=P(A∧B)/P(B)`, and Bayes' rule makes the posterior `P(A|B)` depend on the prior/base-rate `P(A)`; a 99%-accurate test for a 1%-prevalence disease yields a posterior of exactly `1/6`. Maximum likelihood reads a distribution formula backwards — data fixed, parameter free — and the MLE of a Bernoulli rate is `k/n`, of a Gaussian mean the sample average, of a Gaussian variance the mean squared deviation (`/n`). Every one of these you derived by hand *and corroborated by seeded simulation* this module. These are among the least contestable facts in the entire AI stack — a simulation of a million draws either confirms your formula or exposes your bug, and there is no arguing with it.

**Tier 2 — what the mathematical dressing suppresses.** Every one of those clean results sits on assumptions the notation makes invisible, and probability's dressing is uniquely good at lending false authority. *The `σ/√n` standard error assumes independent, identically distributed draws* — but real data is correlated (the same user rated ten movies, the same site served a thousand requests), correlated draws carry *less* information than `n` independent ones, and the honest standard error is *larger* than `σ/√n` — so the naive formula **overstates confidence**, always in the reassuring direction. *A posterior is only as trustworthy as its prior*, and "prior" spans a range from a measured base rate (defensible) to a number someone picked (a value judgment wearing a probability's clothes); the formula processes both identically and reports the same crisp posterior, so a contestable assumption exits the equation dressed as a fact. *A likelihood is maximized under a model you chose* — "the MLE" is the best parameter *assuming the data really is Bernoulli / Gaussian*, and when the model is wrong the MLE is confidently, precisely wrong. The equation never lies; it answers *exactly* the question you encoded — the iid you assumed, the prior you fed, the family you chose — which is rarely exactly the question you meant. Boosters say "the model learned the probability"; critics say "it's just curve-fitting to assumptions" — both dodge that the assumptions are *load-bearing and often unverifiable*, and no amount of math can referee whether your draws were really independent or your prior really justified.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** Your model ships a probability with every decision: a `0.94` that this transaction is fraud, this applicant will default, this scan is malignant. That number has a standard error you may never have computed, a prior you may have inherited without auditing, and a distributional assumption — iid, Gaussian, Bernoulli — that your training data may quietly violate. The `0.94` looks the same whether it rests on ten thousand independent examples or two hundred correlated ones; the notation grants it the same authority either way. **The question you cannot unknow: of every probability your model reports, which single number would you least want an auditor to make you derive from first principles — the standard error, the base rate, or the assumption that your samples were independent — and is the reason you'd flinch that you never checked, or that you checked and looked away?**

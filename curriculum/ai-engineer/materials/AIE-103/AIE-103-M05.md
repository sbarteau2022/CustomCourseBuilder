# AIE-103-M05 — Statistics: estimation, intervals, and the lies of averages

**Foundation tier · 16 learner-hours · Prereq: AIE-103-M04 (probability, expectation, variance, Bayes, MLE) · Co-req: AIE-101 (Python + NumPy fluent)**

> **The descent, stated plainly.** M04 gave you a distribution and asked you to compute its expectation, its variance, its posterior. This module hands you the opposite problem, the one you will actually face: you do **not** have the distribution. You have a *sample* — a spreadsheet, a chart, a batch of API logs — and you must infer the distribution behind it, honestly report how uncertain that inference is, and decide whether a difference you're looking at is real or noise. Every symbol in this module is a piece of arithmetic you have already half-built: an **estimator** is a function of a sample, and — like the sample mean in M04, Lesson 2 — it is itself a random variable with its own mean and its own variance. A **confidence interval** is that estimator's standard error, multiplied by a lookup constant, stapled to both sides of a point estimate. A **p-value** is a probability computed the *forward* way from M04 Lesson 4 — assume the null is true, ask how surprising this data would be — read back out as a single number and compared to a threshold you chose *before* you saw the data. And the "lies of averages" — Simpson's paradox, survivorship bias — are not statistical sophistication; they are the single arithmetic fact that **a weighted average discards the weights**, applied to real charts where the discarded structure is exactly the story. You will compute a confidence interval by hand and then measure its actual coverage by simulating a thousand experiments. You will simulate a false-positive rate you were told was 5% and watch it *be* 5%. You will find a chart where treatment A beats treatment B in every subgroup and loses overall, and you will locate, in exact numbers, why. When AIE-201 reports "our model improved accuracy by 2%," you will already know to ask for the standard error before you believe the headline.

> **The loop under every lab is AI-PRIMM.** Predict (on paper, before code) → Prompt → Read → Verify → Integrate. In this module the prediction is almost always *a number with a claimed long-run frequency*: "the 95% CI procedure will cover the true mean in about 95% of 2,000 repeated experiments — not in 100%, and not in exactly 95.00%, but close, with a gap set by sampling noise on a proportion"; "if the null hypothesis is exactly true, running this same test 20 independent times will produce at least one false 'significant' result about 64% of the time — not 5%, because 5% was the *per-test* rate, not the *batch* rate"; "Treatment A wins in the Easy stratum and wins in the Hard stratum, so I predict — and will be wrong to predict — that A wins overall." A script that prints a number is never the evidence. The evidence is: **your predicted long-run frequency matched the measured long-run frequency — and where a gap remained, your written revision says whether it is sampling noise (shrinks with more trials) or a miscalibrated procedure (does not shrink, no matter how many trials you run).** That written trail is what the assessment grades.

Commands:

- `pytest -q` — runs the tests against `starter.py`; nonzero exit = fail.
- `pytest -q --sol` — (provided conftest flag) runs the same tests against `solution.py`, for after you have submitted, to compare.

**Randomness is seeded, always.** Every simulation in this module uses `rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)` with a stated seed — never the legacy `np.random.seed` global, never an unseeded call. Reference interpreter: **Python 3.12, NumPy ≥ 1.26.**

**No `scipy` in this module — the normal CDF is one line of the standard library.** This module needs the standard normal CDF `Φ(z)` (to turn a z-statistic into a p-value) and a handful of standard normal critical values (to build confidence intervals). Rather than reach for `scipy.stats.norm`, you will build `Φ(z)` yourself from Python's built-in `math.erf`:

```
Φ(z) = 0.5 · (1 + erf(z / √2))
```

This is not a shortcut — it *is* the standard normal CDF, exactly, to floating-point precision; `erf` is the error function, and the relationship above is its textbook connection to the Gaussian CDF. The corresponding **critical values** (the `z*` in `x̄ ± z*·SE`) are the *inverse* of this function, which has no closed form; rather than build a numerical root-finder, this module uses a small, honest lookup table of the three standard confidence levels (`{0.90: 1.645, 0.95: 1.96, 0.99: 2.576}`) — the same three numbers every intro-stats textbook prints on its inside cover. **Naming this limitation is part of the module**: a real statistics toolkit (`scipy.stats.norm.ppf`, or the `t`-distribution for small `n`) computes critical values for *any* confidence level and accounts for the extra uncertainty of an estimated `σ`; this module's z-interval assumes `n` is large enough that the normal approximation (Lesson 1's CLT) has kicked in and that using the sample standard deviation in place of `σ` doesn't matter much. That assumption is itself Lesson 2's first named misconception.

**Two kinds of "close" — carried over from M04, and now load-bearing.** (1) *Analytic vs. analytic* — two exact computations of the same quantity — agree to floating-point noise, `atol=1e-9`. (2) *Analytic vs. simulated* — a formula vs. a finite, seeded run — agree only to **sampling tolerance**. In this module the simulated quantity is usually a **proportion** (a coverage rate, a false-positive rate), and its own standard error is `√(p(1−p)/trials)` — a formula you already own from M04, applied one level up, to the reliability of the very check you're running. Asserting `1e-9` on a coverage simulation is a bug in your test; asserting a huge tolerance on an exact CI-width formula hides a bug in your code.

### Lesson 1 — An estimate is a random variable: bias, standard error, and the Central Limit Theorem

**The teaching.** Everything in M04 assumed you *had* the distribution — you knew `p`, or `μ`, or `σ`, and computed things from it. Statistics starts from the opposite side: you have a **sample**, `x⁽¹⁾, …, x⁽ⁿ⁾`, drawn from some unknown distribution, and you compute an **estimator** — a function of the sample, like the sample mean `x̄ₙ = (1/n)Σx⁽ⁱ⁾` — to stand in for the unknown parameter. The crucial reframe: *the estimator is itself a random variable.* Draw a different sample, get a different `x̄ₙ`. The distribution of that estimator, across all the samples you *could* have drawn, is called its **sampling distribution**, and two of its properties are the whole subject:

- **Bias**: `Bias(x̄ₙ) = E[x̄ₙ] − μ`. The sample mean is **unbiased** — `E[x̄ₙ] = μ` exactly, for any `n`, by linearity of expectation (M04 L1). Centered correctly, on average, across repeated sampling.
- **Standard error**: `SE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n`, exactly the quantity M04 Lesson 2 derived. It is the standard deviation *of the estimator itself*, and it is the single number that says how much any one sample's `x̄ₙ` can be trusted.

Unbiased does **not** mean accurate. An unbiased estimator is correct *on average, over infinitely many repetitions* — any single sample can still land far from `μ`, and how far is exactly what the standard error quantifies. Confusing "the sampling distribution is centered on the truth" with "my one estimate is close to the truth" is the seam this whole module lives on.

The second property is the **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)**, and it is the reason statistics works at all: for `n` independent draws from *any* distribution with finite mean `μ` and variance `σ²` — no matter how skewed, lumpy, or far from bell-shaped the population is — the standardized sample mean converges in distribution to standard normal as `n` grows:

```
(x̄ₙ − μ) / (σ/√n)   →   N(0, 1)     as n → ∞
```

Read this literally: the **population** can look like anything. The **sampling distribution of its mean**, for large enough `n`, looks like a bell curve centered at `μ` with spread `σ/√n` — *regardless* of the population's shape. This is what licenses the `z*` critical values of Lesson 2 and the p-values of Lesson 3: both assume the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, and the CLT is the reason that assumption is usually earned, not asserted.

**Worked example — the exact sampling distribution of a skewed population's mean, by hand, for n=2.** Take a deliberately skewed population: `P(X=20) = 0.8`, `P(X=200) = 0.2` — four "ordinary" outcomes for every one "extreme" outcome, the shape of a lot of real data (income, latency, claim size).

```
μ    = 20(0.8) + 200(0.2) = 16 + 40 = 56
E[X²] = 400(0.8) + 40000(0.2) = 320 + 8000 = 8320
σ²   = 8320 − 56² = 8320 − 3136 = 5184        σ = √5184 = 72
```

`σ = 72` is *larger than* `μ = 56` — this population is wild. Now enumerate the **exact** sampling distribution of `x̄₂` (mean of `n=2` independent draws) by hand — small enough that you don't need a simulation to get the exact answer:

```
(20,20): prob 0.8×0.8 = 0.64,  mean = 20
(20,200) or (200,20): prob 2×0.8×0.2 = 0.32,  mean = 110
(200,200): prob 0.2×0.2 = 0.04,  mean = 200

x̄₂ outcomes: [20, 110, 200]     probs: [0.64, 0.32, 0.04]     (sums to 1 ✓)
```

Check the two claims of this lesson against this exact object:

```
E[x̄₂]  = 20(0.64) + 110(0.32) + 200(0.04) = 12.8 + 35.2 + 8.0 = 56.0     = μ  ✓ (unbiased, exactly)
Var(x̄₂) = E[x̄₂²] − 56² = [400(0.64)+12100(0.32)+40000(0.04)] − 3136
        = [256 + 3872 + 1600] − 3136 = 5728 − 3136 = 2592
        = 5184 / 2 = σ²/n     ✓ (SE law, exactly)
```

Both identities hold **exactly**, not approximately — this is `n=2`, small enough to enumerate by hand instead of trusting a simulation. But look at the *shape*: `[20, 110, 200]` with probabilities `[0.64, 0.32, 0.04]` is nothing like a bell curve — it is three lumps, heavily left-loaded, exactly as skewed in spirit as the population it came from. **The CLT is a claim about the limit as `n → ∞`, and `n=2` is nowhere near that limit.** Quantify the skew directly: the population's skewness (third standardized moment) is `γ₁ = E[(X−μ)³]/σ³ = 559{,}872 / 72³ = 1.5`, and for a sum (hence mean) of `n` iid draws, skewness scales *exactly* as `γ₁/√n` — not approximately, an exact algebraic identity, because the third cumulant of a sum scales with `n` while variance^1.5 scales with `n^1.5`. At `n=2`: predicted skewness `1.5/√2 = 1.061`; computing it exactly from the enumerated distribution above gives `1.0607` — matching to four digits. At `n=30`: predicted skewness `1.5/√30 = 0.274` — visibly closer to the `0` skewness of a true normal, but not there yet. This is the same `1/√n` rate you met as a *shrinking error* in M04; here it shows up as a *shrinking asymmetry*, and it is the quantitative version of "the CLT kicks in eventually, and here is exactly how fast."

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"An unbiased estimator gives the right answer."** It gives the right answer *on average, across repeated sampling*. A single draw of `x̄₂` from the distribution above can be exactly `20` — off from the true `μ=56` by `36`, more than half the true mean's value — while still coming from an unbiased procedure. Bias describes the center of the sampling distribution, not the accuracy of any one estimate; that accuracy is what the standard error is for.
- **"The CLT means the population becomes normal."** The population here is two points, `20` and `200` — it never changes shape, no matter how much data you collect. It is the **sampling distribution of the mean** that approaches normal as `n` grows; the underlying data can stay as skewed, lumpy, or bimodal as it likes forever.
- **"Standard deviation and standard error are the same number."** `σ = 72` describes how spread out *individual draws* of `X` are. `SE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n` describes how spread out the *sample mean* is, and it shrinks as `n` grows while `σ` does not. Using `σ` where `SE` belongs (or vice versa) makes every confidence interval and p-value in Lessons 2–3 wrong by a factor of `√n` — sometimes wildly too narrow, sometimes wildly too wide.
- **"If I have 'enough' data, the normal approximation is safe."** How much is "enough" depends on the population's skew: this lesson's population needs `n` well into the hundreds before its sampling-distribution skewness (`1.5/√n`) drops near zero, while a population that starts symmetric needs almost no `n` at all. There is no universal "n=30 rule" that is safe for every population — it is a rule of thumb for *mildly* skewed data, and this lesson's population is not mild.

### Lesson 2 — Confidence intervals: what "95%" is a claim about, and what it is not

**The teaching.** A **confidence interval (CI)** wraps an estimate with a band wide enough that, if you repeated the whole sampling-and-computing procedure many times, the resulting band would contain the true parameter a stated fraction of the time. For a sample mean, using the CLT from Lesson 1 (`x̄ₙ` approximately normal, spread `σ/√n`), the `95%` interval is:

```
x̄ₙ ± z* · SE          SE = σ/√n          z* = 1.96 for 95% confidence
```

Where does `1.96` come from? It is the value such that a standard normal variable falls between `−z*` and `+z*` with probability `0.95` — i.e. `Φ(1.96) − Φ(−1.96) = 0.95`, equivalently `Φ(1.96) = 0.975` (splitting the missing `5%` evenly into both tails). Checking with the CDF from the harness contract: `Φ(1.96) = 0.5·(1+erf(1.96/√2)) = 0.97500` — the extra `2.1×10⁻⁵` beyond `0.975` is why textbooks round to `1.96` rather than reporting more decimals; it is close enough that the coverage is indistinguishable from `95.00%` at any sample size you'll run in this course.

**The sentence that must be exactly right:** a `95%` confidence interval means that **the *procedure* — draw a sample, compute this interval — covers the true parameter in `95%` of repetitions.** It does **not** mean "there is a 95% probability the true mean is in *this* interval." Once you have computed a specific interval, say `[42.36, 77.64]`, the true `μ` either is or is not in it — there is no more randomness left to assign a probability to; the randomness was in *which sample you happened to draw*, and it has already resolved. The `95%` is a property of the *machine that makes intervals*, not of any one interval it output. This is subtle enough that it is worth re-deriving from Lesson 1: `x̄ₙ` is the random variable, `μ` is a fixed (if unknown) constant, and the interval `x̄ₙ ± z*·SE` is *centered on the random thing*. Before you sample, `P(μ ∈ [x̄ₙ − z*SE, x̄ₙ + z*SE]) = 0.95` is a true statement about where the random interval will land relative to the fixed `μ`. After you sample and plug in the number, the randomness is gone, and probability language about `μ` no longer applies to that specific interval — only to the long-run behavior of the procedure that produced it.

**Worked example — a 95% CI by hand, and its coverage measured by simulation.** Using Lesson 1's population (`μ=56`, `σ=72`), suppose a sample of `n=64` gives `x̄ = 60`:

```
SE = σ/√n = 72/8 = 9.0
CI95 = 60 ± 1.96(9.0) = 60 ± 17.64 = [42.36, 77.64]
```

The true `μ = 56` is inside `[42.36, 77.64]` — but that is one draw's luck, not proof the procedure works. Proof requires *repeating the procedure*: draw `n=64` a few thousand times (seeded), build a fresh CI each time, and count how often `56` lands inside. Running this at `trials=5000, seed=1` gives an empirical coverage of **`0.961`** — close to the claimed `0.95`, with the small gap explained by the residual skew of the sampling distribution at `n=64` (Lesson 1's `1.5/√64 = 0.19` — not yet zero) rather than by a bug. Compare that against a **miscalibrated** procedure that uses `z*=1.645` (the *90%* critical value — `Φ(1.645)=0.950`, so it captures the *one-tailed* 95% point, not the two-tailed one) while still *labeling the output "95% CI."* Simulating that mislabeled procedure at the same `n=64` gives an empirical coverage of **`0.92`** — visibly, persistently below `0.95`, and it does not close the gap no matter how many trials you run, because the miscalibration is in the *formula*, not in the sample.

Widths obey the same `√n` law as everything else in this course: `width = 2·z*·σ/√n`. At `n=64`, `width = 2(1.96)(9.0) = 35.28`. At `n=256` (quadruple `n`), `width = 2(1.96)(72/16) = 17.64` — exactly half. **Quadruple the data, halve the interval** — the same rate that halved the standard error in M04 and the skewness in Lesson 1, because the interval width is just `SE` times a constant.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"There is a 95% probability the true mean is in this specific interval."** Once computed, the interval is fixed and `μ` is fixed; the interval either contains `μ` or it does not — no probability of `0.95` attaches to a settled fact. The `95%` describes the *procedure's* long-run coverage across repeated sampling, which the module's simulation makes literal: `5,000` fresh intervals, `~4,800` of them containing `56`.
- **"A 95% CI means 95% of the data points fall inside it."** That is a different object — a *tolerance* or *prediction* interval about individual observations. A confidence interval is about the location of the unknown *parameter* (the mean), and it typically covers a *narrow* band around `x̄`, not the bulk of the raw data — here the interval `[42.36, 77.64]` is much narrower than the population's actual spread (individual draws are either `20` or `200`, both far outside it).
- **"A wider interval is a worse interval."** Width trades off against confidence level at fixed `n`: a `99%` interval (`z*=2.576`) is *wider* than a `95%` one (`z*=1.96`) at the same sample size, and that is the interval doing its job correctly — being more conservative to hit a higher target coverage — not a defect. The only way to shrink width *without* lowering confidence is more data (`n`), not a smaller `z*`.
- **"Using a smaller z* just makes my interval more efficient."** A smaller `z*` at the *same claimed confidence level* is exactly the mislabeled-procedure bug above: it does make the interval narrower, and it makes the interval **wrong** — its true coverage drops below the number printed on the label. "Efficient" and "correctly calibrated" are different axes; you cannot buy efficiency by lying about the confidence level.

### Lesson 3 — Hypothesis testing done honestly: p-values, error rates, and the price of asking many questions

**The teaching.** A hypothesis test starts by naming two claims: the **null hypothesis** `H₀` (the boring, default explanation — "no difference," "the coin is fair," "this change did nothing") and the **alternative** `H₁` (what you suspect instead). You compute a **test statistic** that measures how far your data sits from what `H₀` predicts — for a sample mean, the familiar

```
z = (x̄ − μ₀) / SE          SE = σ/√n
```

— and then ask: *if `H₀` were exactly true, how surprising would a statistic this extreme (or more) be?* That surprise, expressed as a probability, is the **p-value**:

```
p = P(statistic at least this extreme | H₀ true)
```

This is a **forward** probability, in exactly M04 Lesson 4's sense — it fixes the hypothesis and asks about the data, not the other way around. It is *not* `P(H₀ true | data)` — that would be a posterior, and computing it would require a prior on `H₀` and Bayes' rule, which classical hypothesis testing does not supply. Treating the p-value as "the probability the null is true" is the base-rate fallacy of M04 Lesson 3, wearing a p-value's clothes: it substitutes a likelihood-shaped quantity for a posterior-shaped question.

You compare `p` against a **significance level** `α` (almost always `0.05`), chosen *before* looking at the data, and reject `H₀` if `p < α`. This threshold defines two ways to be wrong, and by construction one of them is *exactly* `α`:

- **Type I error** (false positive): rejecting `H₀` when it is actually true. Its rate is exactly `α`, **by the definition of a p-value** — if `H₀` is true, `p` is uniformly distributed between `0` and `1`, so `P(p<α) = α` exactly. This is not a claim to verify empirically; it is what "p-value" *means*, and Lab 3 makes you watch it be true.
- **Type II error** (false negative): failing to reject `H₀` when it is actually false. Its rate is `β`; **power** `= 1 − β` is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect, and it depends on the true effect size, `n`, and `α` in ways this module only names (a full treatment is a later course's job).

The dangerous arithmetic shows up the moment you run **more than one test**. If `H₀` is true and you run one test at `α=0.05`, your false-positive chance is `5%`. If you run **20** independent true-null tests and call the study a "finding" if *any one* clears the bar, the chance of at least one false positive is:

```
P(at least one false positive) = 1 − P(none) = 1 − (1−α)^m = 1 − 0.95^20 = 1 − 0.3585 = 0.6415
```

**64%**, not `5%`. Running many comparisons and reporting only the one that "worked" — **p-hacking** — silently converts a `5%` error rate into a coin flip. The honest fix is a **correction**: the simplest, **Bonferroni**, divides the significance threshold by the number of tests, `α/m`, so the *overall* chance of any false positive across all `m` tests stays near `α`. At `m=20`, the corrected threshold is `0.05/20 = 0.0025` — a single test's `p=0.03`, comfortably "significant" on its own, does *not* survive that correction.

**Worked example — a z-test by hand, and the multiple-comparisons trap, by simulation.** A baseline process has a known mean `μ₀=50` and known `σ=20`. A sample of `n=25` gives `x̄=57.2`.

```
SE = 20/√25 = 4.0
z  = (57.2 − 50)/4.0 = 1.80

Two-tailed p = 2·(1 − Φ(1.80)) = 2·(1 − 0.9641) = 2(0.0359) = 0.0719
```

`p = 0.072 > 0.05` — **not** significant at the standard threshold; the data is consistent with `H₀`. Now watch a subtle, realistic defect flip that conclusion: report the **one-tailed** p-value (`P(Z > 1.80)` only, no factor of 2) as if it answered the two-tailed question:

```
One-tailed p = 1 − Φ(1.80) = 1 − 0.9641 = 0.0359
```

`0.0359 < 0.05` — **"significant."** Same data, same `z=1.80`, opposite decision, because the wrong tail structure was used for the question actually being asked ("is the mean different," not "is the mean *specifically higher*"). This single silent halving is Lesson 3's most common real-world bug, and it is planted, deliberately, in this module's capstone.

Now the multiple-comparisons claim, checked by simulation rather than trusted on faith: draw `20,000` independent samples of `n=25` from a population whose mean really *is* `50` (so `H₀` is true by construction), run the two-tailed z-test on each at `α=0.05`, and count how often it falsely rejects. Measured rate at `seed=3`: **`0.0496`** — matching the predicted `0.05` almost to three digits, because Type I error rate is not an approximation, it *is* the definition. Then batch those tests into groups of `20` and ask, for each batch, whether *any* of the 20 falsely rejected: measured rate at `seed=4`, `3,000` batches: **`0.628`**, against the predicted `1 − 0.95²⁰ = 0.6415` — close, with the residual gap shrinking as more batches are simulated, because this is a *proportion* with its own sampling noise (`SE ≈ √(0.64·0.36/3000) ≈ 0.0088`), not a bug.

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"p < 0.05 means there's a 5% chance the null hypothesis is true."** The p-value is `P(data this extreme | H₀ true)`, not `P(H₀ true | data)` — the same likelihood-for-posterior substitution as M04's base-rate fallacy. Computing the actual posterior probability that `H₀` is true requires a prior on `H₀` and Bayes' rule; classical `p < 0.05` testing was never designed to answer that question and cannot be read as if it did.
- **"A non-significant result (p > 0.05) proves there's no effect."** Failing to reject `H₀` means the data did not provide *strong enough evidence* against it — which can happen because there truly is no effect, or because the study was underpowered (small `n`, small true effect, both pushing power down). "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," and a non-significant result with a tiny `n` is nearly uninformative either way.
- **"If I test enough hypotheses, a p<0.05 hit among them is meaningful on its own."** The `20`-test simulation above measured a `62.8%` chance of at least one false "significant" result when *every* null was true — an uncorrected single hit inside a batch of comparisons is close to a coin flip, not evidence. A `p<0.05` claim is only as trustworthy as the (stated, and ideally corrected) number of comparisons that produced it.
- **"Statistical significance means the effect is practically important."** A p-value conflates effect size with sample size: because `SE` shrinks as `n` grows, an arbitrarily tiny, practically meaningless difference becomes "significant" at a large enough `n`. A p-value answers "is this distinguishable from zero," never "is this large enough to matter" — that second question needs an effect-size number and a domain judgment call, not a threshold comparison.

### Lesson 4 — The lies of averages: Simpson's paradox and survivorship bias

**The teaching.** An average is a **compression**: it takes a whole distribution of numbers and reports one. Compression, by definition, throws things away — and sometimes what it throws away is precisely the structure that would have changed your conclusion. This lesson names two concrete, common shapes that loss takes.

**Simpson's paradox**: a comparison that goes one way in *every* subgroup can go the **opposite** way when the subgroups are pooled. It is not a paradox in the logical sense — it is arithmetic, and the mechanism is always the same: a **confounding variable** (something that affects both the outcome *and* which group a data point ends up in) is unevenly distributed between the two things you're comparing. Pool across it, and the pooled average is dominated by whichever group happened to get more of the *easy* cases — a fact about the mix, mistaken for a fact about the treatments.

**Worked example — Simpson's paradox in a two-treatment trial, exact numbers, by hand.** Two treatments, `A` and `B`, tested on patients in two severity strata, `Easy` and `Hard`:

```
                Easy cases                      Hard cases
Treatment A:    81/90  = 90%                    273/910 = 30%
Treatment B:    792/900 = 88%                   25/100  = 25%
```

**A beats B in the Easy stratum** (`90% > 88%`) **and A beats B in the Hard stratum** (`30% > 25%`). By every stratified comparison, A is the better treatment. Now pool:

```
Pooled A: (81 + 273) / (90 + 910)  = 354 / 1000 = 35.4%
Pooled B: (792 + 25)  / (900 + 100) = 817 / 1000 = 81.7%
```

**B looks dramatically better — 81.7% vs. 35.4% — the exact opposite of every subgroup comparison.** The mechanism is visible in the totals: A was given to `910` Hard cases and only `90` Easy ones (`91%` Hard) — A was mostly used on the harder population. B was given to `900` Easy cases and only `100` Hard ones (`90%` Easy) — B was mostly used on the easier population. The pooled rate is a weighted average dominated by *how many of each kind of case each treatment saw*, not by how well each treatment worked on a given kind of case. **Whenever the strata sizes are unbalanced between the groups being compared, the pooled comparison is measuring the mix, not the effect** — and the only fix is to check the stratified numbers before trusting a pooled headline.

**Survivorship bias** is the same compression trap wearing a different disguise: computing an average, a rate, or a "typical" description over only the members of a population that **survived some filter**, and mistaking that for a description of the whole population. The canonical case: WWII engineers studied returning bombers, tallied where the bullet holes clustered (wings, fuselage), and nearly reinforced *those* spots — until statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that the sample was already conditioned on survival. Planes hit in the engine or cockpit *did not return* to be tallied; the holes visible on survivors mark the spots a plane **can** take damage and still make it home. The armor belonged exactly where the data showed **no** damage — because the planes hit there were missing from the dataset entirely. The lie was never in the arithmetic of "average hole location" — it was in silently averaging over a population (survivors) that was not the population anyone meant to describe (all planes sent out).

Both traps share one root cause with each other, and with M04's base-rate fallacy: **an aggregate number is only meaningful relative to the population it was computed over, and that population — its strata mix, its survival filter — is exactly the information a single average erases.** "Find the lie in the chart" is, every time, "find the population the chart's average silently substituted for the one you assumed it meant."

**Named misconceptions:**

- **"If A beats B in every subgroup, A beats B overall."** Simpson's paradox is a direct, exact counterexample — `90%>88%` and `30%>25%` in every stratum, `35.4%<81.7%` pooled. Whenever group membership (here, which treatment) correlates with a confounding variable (here, case severity), the pooled comparison can reverse; always check whether the subgroup sizes are balanced between the groups before trusting an aggregate.
- **"The pooled number is the 'real' trend, and the stratified breakdown is just extra detail."** Often backwards. When a confound is present, the *stratified* comparisons are the causally meaningful ones — they compare like with like. The pooled number here is not "the treatments' overall effect" so much as "an artifact of who happened to get which treatment," and reporting it as the headline finding is the lie.
- **"Survivorship bias only applies to famous historical examples like WWII bombers."** Any dataset built from outcomes that "made it" — completed customers (not the ones who churned before you could measure them), published research (not the null results that never got submitted), companies still operating (not the ones that failed) — has silently dropped exactly the population whose absence is most informative. The fix is always the same: name the filter, and ask what the dropped group would have shown.
- **"Pooling more subgroups into one average always makes the estimate more reliable."** More data pooled *the wrong way* — across a confound, without stratifying — does not shrink toward the truth the way M04's `1/√n` law shrinks sampling noise; it can lock in a *worse* distortion, because you are averaging over an ever-more-lopsided mix rather than an ever-larger honest sample of the same population. Reliability from more data assumes the data is drawn from one population, or that you have stratified correctly — an assumption a bigger `n` does not repair on its own.

# solution.py
import numpy as np

def population_moments(values, probs):
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    probs = np.asarray(probs, dtype=float)
    mu = float(np.sum(values * probs))
    var = float(np.sum((values - mu) ** 2 * probs))
    m3 = float(np.sum((values - mu) ** 3 * probs))
    skew = m3 / var ** 1.5
    return mu, var, skew

def exact_n2_distribution(values, probs):
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    probs = np.asarray(probs, dtype=float)
    outcomes = {}
    for i in range(len(values)):
        for j in range(len(values)):
            m = (values[i] + values[j]) / 2.0
            p = probs[i] * probs[j]
            outcomes[m] = outcomes.get(m, 0.0) + p
    xs = np.array(sorted(outcomes.keys()))
    ps = np.array([outcomes[x] for x in xs])
    return xs, ps

def simulate_sample_means(values, probs, n, trials, rng):
    draws = rng.choice(values, size=(trials, n), p=probs)
    return draws.mean(axis=1)

def sample_skewness(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    mu = x.mean()
    m2 = np.mean((x - mu) ** 2)
    m3 = np.mean((x - mu) ** 3)
    return float(m3 / m2 ** 1.5)

# solution.py
import numpy as np
import math

Z_TABLE = {0.90: 1.645, 0.95: 1.96, 0.99: 2.576}

def normal_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))

def confidence_interval_z(xbar, sigma, n, confidence=0.95):
    z_star = Z_TABLE[confidence]
    se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
    return (xbar - z_star * se, xbar + z_star * se)

def ci_width(sigma, n, confidence=0.95):
    z_star = Z_TABLE[confidence]
    return 2.0 * z_star * sigma / math.sqrt(n)

def coverage_experiment(values, probs, true_mean, sigma, n, num_trials,
                         confidence, rng, z_star=None):
    if z_star is None:
        z_star = Z_TABLE[confidence]
    draws = rng.choice(values, size=(num_trials, n), p=probs)
    means = draws.mean(axis=1)
    se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
    los = means - z_star * se
    his = means + z_star * se
    covered = (los <= true_mean) & (true_mean <= his)
    return float(covered.mean())

```python
# solution.py
import numpy as np
import math

def normal_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))

def z_test_p_value(xbar, mu0, sigma, n, two_tailed=True):
    se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
    z = (xbar - mu0) / se
    if two_tailed:
        p = 2.0 * (1.0 - normal_cdf(abs(z)))
    else:
        p = 1.0 - normal_cdf(z)
    return z, p

def simulate_type1_error_rate(mu0, sigma, n, alpha, num_experiments, rng):
    draws = rng.normal(loc=mu0, scale=sigma, size=(num_experiments, n))
    means = draws.mean(axis=1)
    se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
    zs = (means - mu0) / se
    ps = 2.0 * (1.0 - np.vectorize(normal_cdf)(np.abs(zs)))
    return float((ps < alpha).mean())

def simulate_batch_false_positive_rate(mu0, sigma, n, alpha, m_tests,
                                        num_batches, rng):
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(num_batches):
        draws = rng.normal(loc=mu0, scale=sigma, size=(m_tests, n))
        means = draws.mean(axis=1)
        se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
        zs = (means - mu0) / se
        ps = 2.0 * (1.0 - np.vectorize(normal_cdf)(np.abs(zs)))
        if (ps < alpha).any():
            hits += 1
    return hits / num_batches

def bonferroni_correct(p_values, alpha):
    p_values = np.asarray(p_values, dtype=float)
    m = len(p_values)
    return p_values < (alpha / m)
```

`test_one_tailed_flips_the_decision_the_two_tailed_test_made` is the anti-gaming guard for the tailedness bug: it requires the *same* data to be simultaneously non-significant (honest) and significant (defective), which only a genuinely different formula — not a threshold tweak — can produce. `test_type1_error_rate_matches_alpha` treats `α` as a falsifiable prediction, not a definition to recite, and `test_batch_false_positive_rate_matches_one_minus_power` is the module's sharpest number: over `20,000` and `3,000`-batch seeded runs, the measured rates land at `0.0496` and `≈0.63` respectively, both inside the stated tolerances — your write-up must state that these are proportions with their own `√(p(1−p)/trials)` sampling error, which is why the tolerances are `0.01` and `0.03`, not `1e-9`.

def normal_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))

def confidence_interval(xbar, sigma, n):
    """Claims to build a 95% CI."""
    # DEFECT 1
    z_star = 1.645
    se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
    return (xbar - z_star * se, xbar + z_star * se)

def p_value_for_shift(xbar, mu0, sigma, n):

    z = (xbar - mu0) / (sigma / math.sqrt(n))
    # DEFECT 2
    return 1.0 - normal_cdf(abs(z))

        vis = sum(v for _, c, v in rows)

## Three-tier reading

**Tier 1 — the material ground (verifiable facts).** An estimator is a function of a sample and is itself a random variable with its own bias and standard error; the sample mean is unbiased and has `SE = σ/√n`. The Central Limit Theorem says the *sampling distribution* of the mean approaches normal as `n` grows, regardless of the population's shape, and the rate of that approach can be quantified exactly (skewness shrinks as `γ₁/√n`). A `95%` confidence interval is a claim about the long-run coverage of a *procedure* across repeated sampling, not a probability statement about one already-computed interval; you verified this by simulating 5,000 fresh experiments and counting coverage directly, and you verified a *miscalibrated* procedure (`z*=1.645` labeled "95%") undercovers and does not self-correct with more trials. A p-value is `P(data this extreme | H₀ true)`, not `P(H₀ true | data)`; the Type I error rate equals `α` by construction, and running `m` independent true-null tests inflates the chance of at least one false positive to `1−(1−α)^m` — `64%` at `m=20`, a number you measured, not just derived. Simpson's paradox — every subgroup favoring A while the pooled data favors B — is arithmetic, produced whenever group membership correlates with an unbalanced confounding variable; you computed an exact instance where A wins both strata (`90%>88%`, `30%>25%`) and loses pooled (`35.4%<81.7%`). None of this is contestable; it is checkable by hand and by seeded simulation, and it disagreed with you exactly where your intuition — not the arithmetic — was wrong.

**Tier 2 — what the mathematical dressing suppresses.** Every technique in this module answers *exactly* the question it was built to answer, and reports that answer with a precision that invites you to forget how narrow the question was. *A confidence interval assumes the CLT has "kicked in"* — that `n` is large relative to the population's skew — and the module's own population needed `n` in the hundreds before its sampling-distribution skewness (`1.5/√n`) was small; a report that quotes a `95%` CI without stating `n` or the underlying distribution's shape is quoting a number whose honesty you cannot check. *A p-value answers "is this distinguishable from zero," never "does this matter"* — and because `SE` shrinks with `n`, a company with enough traffic can manufacture statistical significance out of an effect too small to justify the engineering cost of shipping it; "statistically significant" is a phrase that sounds like a verdict and is actually a threshold comparison, and which threshold, and how many comparisons were run to find it, are the load-bearing facts the phrase itself hides. *A pooled average is a claim about a population* — and the population it silently substitutes (survivors, the mix you happened to observe) for the population you meant (everyone, including the ones who churned, died, or never got sampled) is exactly the fact a single number cannot carry. Boosters report the pooled number because it is simpler and often more flattering; critics dismiss aggregate statistics wholesale because they've seen this trick before — both dodge the actual discipline, which is checking the *segment breakdown and the sample size* every single time before trusting either the number or the dismissal.

**Tier 3 — sit with this.** Somewhere in your model's evaluation report is a number that looks exactly as solid as every number around it — `95% CI: [0.91, 0.94]` on accuracy, `p=0.02` on an A/B test, a `+3%` lift claimed for a new pipeline over the old one. That number was built from a sample size, a confidence procedure, and (if it involved a comparison across any kind of user segment, region, cohort, or time window) a pooling decision — three choices that are each individually checkable and that the final printed number does not show its work for. **The question you cannot unknow: of every number in your last report, which one would most change its meaning if someone asked "what's `n`, was this corrected for multiple comparisons, and what does the number look like stratified instead of pooled" — and is the reason you haven't asked that question yourself that you're confident of the answer, or that you'd rather not know?**
